辞职的不是AI，是解释权The Resignation Wasn't About AI. It Was About Who Owns the Rules.

科技 文化层 · 元暴力 My reporting prompted Dartmouth provost to resign. It wasnât the right call ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
用AI写稿不是罪，用旧尺子量新工具才是。
Using AI isn't the crime. Measuring it with an old ruler is.

一个教务长因为用AI写专栏辞职了，而写这篇报道的记者自己说：他不该丢工作。

先看清发生了什么。Schnell 在《纽约时报》《华尔街日报》《华盛顿邮报》这类明令禁止使用AI的刊物上发客座专栏，一个月内同类文章有50篇含AI生成文本。他自己对校报说，想法、研究、证据评估、结构、草稿、最终文本都由他决定。也就是说，他借了工具，没借脑子。

那问题出在哪？出在尺子上。刊物禁止AI，是因为它默认“文字由谁产出”等于“思想由谁产出”。这个等式在打字机时代成立，在AI时代不成立。禁止AI的规则，本质上是在维护一种关于“原创”的旧叙事——一种把写作过程神秘化、把作者身份等同于逐字敲键盘的叙事。Schnell 撞上的不是学术伦理，是这套叙事的执法。

学生愤怒是可以理解的。他们被禁止用AI，被抓到要受罚。教务长用了，却只丢工作不丢学位。但这里的不公平不是“他用了AI”，而是“规则对学生和对教务长执行力度不同”。记者说得对：强制辞职只会延续现状——我们正在和一股已经普遍到无法对抗的技术潮流作战。

真正该问的是：为什么我们如此愤怒于一个人用了明显有用的工具？因为愤怒的对象选错了。该愤怒的不是Schnell用了AI，而是那些刊物一边禁止AI一边靠AI辅助分析来写报道揭露别人用AI。这套操作本身就说明，禁令是表演性的。

更深一层：这场风波里，真正被审判的不是AI使用，而是“谁有资格定义什么是原创”。Schnell 丢了工作，不是因为他做错了什么，而是因为他成了旧规则的祭品。而旧规则本身，正在被它自己的执行者悄悄绕过。

结尾的刺：当记者用AI分析揭发别人用AI，当刊物一边禁AI一边发AI辅助的报道，这场关于“原创”的道德恐慌，不过是一场关于解释权的争夺——谁有权说“这算作弊”，谁就有权决定谁该辞职。

A provost resigned for using AI in his columns. The reporter who exposed him says he shouldn't have lost his job.

Look at what actually happened. Schnell published guest columns in the New York Times, Wall Street Journal, and Washington Post—outlets that explicitly ban AI use. Fifty such columns across a month contained AI-generated text. He told the Dartmouth newspaper that the ideas, research, evidence evaluation, structure, drafts, and final text were his. He borrowed the tool, not the brain.

So where's the problem? In the ruler. The outlets ban AI because they assume "who produces the words" equals "who produces the thought." That equation held in the typewriter era. It doesn't hold now. The ban on AI is fundamentally about protecting an old narrative of originality—one that mystifies the writing process and equates authorship with typing every keystroke. Schnell didn't run into academic ethics. He ran into the enforcement of that narrative.

Students' anger makes sense. They're banned from AI, punished if caught. The provost used it and lost only his job, not his degree. But the unfairness here isn't "he used AI." It's that the rules are enforced differently for students and provosts. The reporter is right: forced resignations just perpetuate the status quo—we're fighting a technological tide so commonplace it's impossible to fight.

The real question: why are we so outraged that someone used an obviously useful tool? Because we're aiming the outrage at the wrong target. The outrage should be at outlets that ban AI while using AI-assisted analysis to report on others using AI. That operation itself proves the ban is performative.

Deeper still: what's really on trial isn't AI use. It's who gets to define what counts as original. Schnell lost his job not because he did something wrong, but because he became the sacrifice to an old rule. And that rule is being quietly bypassed by the very people enforcing it.

The sting: when a reporter uses AI to expose others using AI, when outlets ban AI while publishing AI-assisted reporting, this moral panic over "originality" is just a fight over interpretive authority—whoever gets to say "this is cheating" gets to decide who resigns.

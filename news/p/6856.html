投票站不是被砍，是被定价The Poll Tax Is Now Measured in Miles

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要得克萨斯州塔兰特县共和党多数派在中期选举前投票取消了92个投票站，包括自2012年起用作投票点的德州大学阿灵顿分校活动中心；北卡罗来纳州吉尔福德县选举委员会以3比2按党派划线否决了两所大学的校内提前投票点。学生们组织步行、拼车和免费接送前往投票。报道称约四分之三的高校校内没有现场投票选项。
The newsRepublican majorities in Tarrant County, Texas voted to eliminate 92 polling locations before the midterms, including a UT Arlington site used since 2012; Guilford County, North Carolina's election board rejected on-campus early voting sites at two universities on a 3-2 party-line vote. Students organized walks, carpools and free rides. Three in four campuses have no in-person voting option, per a 2024 study.
把投票站搬远，就是给选票加价。
Move the polling place far enough, and you've put a price on the ballot.

塔兰特县砍掉92个投票站，吉尔福德县按党派划线否掉两个校内提前投票点。这不是「缩减开支」，这是给投票这一行为重新定价——按你离投票站的距离收费。

加尔通的公式在这里最好用：Violence = Potential − Actual。潜在状态是「一个没车的学生走十分钟就能投票」，实际状态是「走45分钟、顶着85华氏度的高温，或者花30美元叫车」。差额就是暴力。它没有施暴者，没有血迹，只有一张被批准的投票站清单——结构层在动，而结构层的暴力从来不需要动手，只需要把入口挪走。

报道里那位学生Rozier用了「现代人头税」这个词。她说得对，而且比她自己可能意识到的更精确：人头税从来不是「禁止你投票」，是「让你为投票付一笔别人不用付的钱」。3500个学生被「landlocked」，那所学校的学生没有车，于是300个人一起走路去投票。走路这件事本身成了新闻，这就是差额的形状。

现在看文化层怎么配合。这套操作必须有一个叙事外壳，否则太难看。外壳是「投票站要合理分配资源」「要防止舞弊」「要控制成本」——全是中性的、技术的、听起来像会计的词。Simmons说得直接：这是Project 2025在地方层面的执行。而那位法官驳回了恢复92个站点的请求，于是「合法」这个章盖下来了。法律程序走完，暴力就变成了现状，现状就不叫暴力了。

这里还有一层更细的东西。被砍的站点集中在黑人、拉丁裔学生比例高的学校：UT阿灵顿近4.3万学生，三分之二是少数族裔；中央州立大学是传统黑人院校，最近的投票点被挪到步行两个半小时外的教堂。这不是巧合，这是选民的种族构成被当成成本收益表来读。原初种族那一章讲的是女性如何被定义、被消费、被剥夺主体性；这里的机制同构——一群人被先定义为「本来就不太会投票的人」，然后政策按这个定义执行，执行完这个定义就成真了。

但报道里有一件事值得单独拎出来：莫拉莱斯说，他们「点了一把火」。学生组织登记、办提前投票派对、安排免费接送。砍站点的官员大概算过一笔账——少一个站点，少一批票。他们没算到的是，把投票变得困难，也可以把投票变成一件值得走45分钟的事。

这就是「个人即政治」在选举日那天的样子：投票站离你多远，不是行政细节，是你这个人在公共空间里值多少。

还剩一件没被定价的东西：那些走了45分钟的人，他们的时间在官方文件里从来不算成本。谁的时间被默认可以浪费，这份清单和投票站清单是同一份。

Tarrant County cut 92 polling locations. Guilford County killed two on-campus early voting sites on a party-line vote. This isn't "trimming costs." It's repricing the act of voting — charging people by the distance between them and a ballot box.

Galtung's formula does the work here: Violence = Potential − Actual. The potential is a student without a car walking ten minutes to vote. The actual is 45 minutes in 85-degree heat, or $30 for a rideshare. The gap is the violence. No perpetrator, no blood — just an approved list of polling places. The structural layer is moving, and structural violence never has to lift a finger. It just moves the door.

Rozier, the NC A&T senior, calls it a modern-day poll tax. She's right, and more precisely than she may realize: a poll tax was never "you can't vote." It was "you pay for your vote what others don't pay for theirs." Three thousand five hundred students are landlocked. Three hundred of them walked. The walk became the news story. That's the shape of the gap.

Now watch the cultural layer do its cover work. You can't do this bare-faced. The shell is "rational resource allocation," "election integrity," "cost control" — neutral, technical, accounting-adjacent words. Simmons names it: the Project 2025 playbook run at the local level. And when a judge declined to reinstate the 92 sites, the stamp of legality came down. Procedure completed, violence becomes the status quo, and the status quo stops being called violence.

There's a finer grain here too. The cuts land on campuses serving Black and Latino students: UT Arlington, nearly 43,000 students, about two-thirds people of color; Central State, an HBCU, its nearest site moved to a church two and a half hours away on foot. That's not coincidence. That's a electorate's racial composition read as a cost-benefit line item. The Primal Race chapter describes how a group gets defined first, consumed second, stripped of agency third. Same mechanism, different target: define a population as "people who wouldn't vote anyway," write policy to match, and the definition comes true.

But one thing in this story deserves its own paragraph. Morales says they lit a fire. Students are running registration drives, early-voting parties, free rides. The officials who cut the sites probably ran the math — one fewer site, fewer votes. What they didn't run is the other math: make voting hard enough, and voting becomes something worth walking 45 minutes for.

That's the personal is political on election day. How far the polling place is from you isn't an administrative detail. It's your price in public space.

One cost still hasn't been priced in: the 45 minutes of the people who walked. Their time was never on the ledger. Whoever's time gets treated as free — that list and the polling-place list are the same list.

工资委员会不加工资A Wage Board Is Not a Wage

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要孟加拉国周日成立新的工资委员会，审查服装工人最低工资。尽管宣布成立委员会，达卡周边数千名工人仍继续抗议，要求将月最低工资从12500塔卡提高至30000塔卡，警方发射催泪瓦斯和声爆弹。抗议始于周六，导致达卡郊区约30家服装厂放假。
The newsBangladesh formed a new wage board on Sunday to review the minimum wage for garment workers. Despite the announcement, thousands of workers continued protesting around Dhaka, demanding a raise from 12,500 taka to 30,000 taka a month; police fired tear gas and sound grenades. The protests began Saturday and led about 30 factories near Dhaka to declare holidays.
工人要的是钱，政府给的是椅子。
Workers asked for money. The government handed them a chair.

孟加拉国成立了一个六人工资委员会，然后工人继续上街。这两件事放在一起就是全部答案：政府给的不是工资，是一张会议桌。

工人要的是每月三万塔卡，现在是十二万五千塔卡的一半都不到——原文写的是12,500塔卡（约101美元），他们要求30,000塔卡（约242美元）。物价、房租、交通、医疗全在涨，购买力被磨掉了。这个差额不是抽象的，它就是 Violence = Potential − Actual 里那个 Actual 被压下去的部分：一个工人本可以靠一份全职工资活得像个人，现在不行，中间那段被拿走了。这是 structural 层的暴力，工资标准、出口订单、能源合同，每一环都没有具体的人在打人，但每一环都在扣钱。

而政府回应的方式是成立一个委员会，六个人，政府、工人、厂主各出代表，任务是“确定新的最低工资”。劳工部顾问说，工资委员会的问题已经解决，不会再有麻烦了，工人应该回去上班，顺畅地工作。这句话值得逐字读一遍：在他嘴里，问题是“成立委员会”这件事，不是工资。委员会一成立，问题就算解决了。至于委员会最后定多少、什么时候定、工人能不能靠它活下去，不在“问题”的定义里。

这就是换尺子不换能力的老套路，只不过这次换的不是定律，是“问题”的定义。工人问的是我能拿多少钱，政府答的是我们有了一个机构。机构是过程，工资是结果，把过程当成结果宣布，等于告诉工人：你们的不满已经被处理了，请回车间。

警察那边是另一套语言。达卡周边冲突，警察发射催泪瓦斯和声爆弹，Ashulia 工业区有人受伤，包括警察。约三十家工厂宣布放假。劳工与就业部长警告，长期停工和停产会拖延出口发货和买家付款，让厂主和工人都更难。注意这个句子的结构：厂主的困难（收不到钱）和工人的困难（拿不到工资）被并列成同一种困难，而前者恰恰是后者的原因。工人停产，是因为工资不够活；部长说的“对双方都不利”，实际是把工人的筹码说成对工人自己的伤害。

厂主这边说行业面临成本上升、周期性的燃气和电力短缺、中东局势带来的能源市场不确定。全是真话，也全是转移：成本上升的压力往下压，压到最没有议价能力的那一层，就是车间的工资单。成衣业占孟加拉国 GDP 约百分之十，是仅次于中国的世界最大成衣出口国，几百万工人，国家外汇的大头。这笔钱是谁挣出来的，报道写得很清楚；这笔钱怎么分，报道里没有一个人被问到。

六人委员会里工人有代表。这是最值得盯的一处共谋：工人代表坐进一个由政府召集、厂主同席、没有罢工权做后盾的房间里，谈一个厂主可以拖、政府可以宣布“已解决”的数字。代表坐进去的那一刻，街上的压力就被兑换成了会议室里的礼貌。这不是说代表不该去，是说一个没有强制时限、没有追溯补发、没有把生活成本指数写进公式的委员会，本身就是一个缓冲装置——它吸收愤怒，产出程序。

报道没有写这个委员会什么时候出结果，没有写新工资从哪个月算起，没有写如果定出来的数字低于三万塔卡工人还能做什么。这些空白不是报道的疏忽，是这件事目前的真实状态：工人要的那个数字，还没有任何人答应。

三万塔卡是工人的开价，不是他们的胜利。他们上街换来的是一张六个人的名单，而催泪瓦斯已经先发过了。

Bangladesh formed a six-member wage board, and the workers kept protesting. Put those two facts side by side and you have the whole story: what the government delivered was not a wage, it was a table.

Workers want 30,000 taka a month. They currently get 12,500 taka, about $101. Food, housing, transport and healthcare have eaten their purchasing power alive. That gap is not abstract — it is the Actual in Violence = Potential − Actual, pushed down and held there. A worker putting in full-time hours could live like a person; she cannot; the difference is being taken from her. This is structural violence: wage floors, export orders, energy contracts. Nobody in that chain throws a punch, and everybody in that chain collects.

The government's answer is a committee. Six people — government, workers, factory owners — tasked with determining a new minimum wage. The labour ministry adviser told reporters the issue of forming the wage board has been resolved, there will be no more problems, workers should return and work smoothly. Read that literally. In his mouth, the problem was whether a board exists. Once it exists, the problem is solved. What number it lands on, when, and whether anyone can live on it — none of that is inside his definition of the problem.

This is the old trick of changing the ruler instead of the capability, except what gets redefined here is not a law of physics but the word "problem." Workers ask how much they will be paid. The state answers that an institution now exists. An institution is a process; a wage is an outcome. Announcing the process as the outcome tells workers their grievance has been handled. Please return to the floor.

The police speak a different language. Clashes around Dhaka, tear gas and sound grenades, injuries in Ashulia including officers, roughly thirty factories declaring holidays. The labour minister warned that prolonged closures could delay export shipments and buyer payments, making things harder for manufacturers and workers alike. Look at the grammar: the owner's difficulty (not getting paid) and the worker's difficulty (not being paid enough) are flattened into one shared hardship — when the first is the cause of the second. Workers stop the line because the wage cannot feed them. Calling their only leverage a harm to themselves is not sympathy, it is disarmament.

Manufacturers cite rising production costs, periodic gas and electricity shortages, energy uncertainty tied to the Middle East. All true, all displacement. Cost pressure flows downhill to whoever has the least bargaining power, and that is the payroll. Garments are about 10 percent of GDP, the world's largest industry after China's, millions of workers, the bulk of foreign earnings. The report is precise about who generates that money. It contains not one person asked how it gets divided.

Workers do have representatives on the six-member board. That is the part to watch. A worker delegate seated in a room convened by the government, beside the owners, without a strike mandate at her back, negotiating a number the owners can stall and the government can declare resolved — the moment she sits down, the pressure from the street is converted into courtesy indoors. This is not an argument that she shouldn't go. It is an argument that a board with no deadline, no retroactive back pay, and no cost-of-living formula baked into the math is a shock absorber. It absorbs anger and outputs procedure.

The report does not say when the board will decide, from which month a new wage would apply, or what workers can do if the number comes in under 30,000. Those blanks are not oversight. They are the current state of the thing: the number workers named has not been promised by anyone.

30,000 taka is the opening demand, not the victory. What the streets bought so far is a list of six names — and the tear gas came first.

极化不是病，是有人不想让你看清谁在共谋Polarization Is Not the Disease. It's the Distraction.

国际 结构层 · 元暴力 The New Republic ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
把极化说成心理本能，是在替结构免责
Calling polarization human nature lets the structure off the hook.

这篇书评最诚实的一句话在最后：要对抗极化，我们得更加极化。前面二十段都在讨论美国人为什么彼此仇恨，最后一句才承认，仇恨不是问题，问题是仇恨被引向了错误的方向。

Ezra Klein 的解释是进化心理学：人类的大脑在部落时代被写死，见到外群体就紧张，见到内群体就抱团。书评作者已经戳了一刀——如果美国人的大脑天生如此，为什么多党制和联合政府能在别国活那么久？这一刀还可以再往下捅：所谓"开放性是自由派、尽责性是保守派"的人格分类，被 Buckley 这个会说三种语言、住在纽约、看外国电影的《国家评论》创始人当场证伪。书评作者点得很准：这套性格类型学几乎只适用于富裕白人。

真正被 Klein 轻轻放过的，是物质政治。他整本书里最少提到的身份是阶级。而报道里写得很清楚：非裔和拉丁裔在宗教和社会议题上相当保守，却远比总体人口更支持民主党——因为慷慨的社会项目、更公正的刑事司法、更人道的移民政策符合他们的物质利益。这不是心理气质，这是算账。当一个人投票的理由是医保和家人的安全，把他归因为"尽责性"或"开放性"，等于把结构问题翻译成性格问题，然后宣布无解。

这就是共谋发生的地方。把极化归因于不可改变的心理，等于说：制度不必改，因为人就是这样。Klein 提出的改革——废除冗长辩论、改革选举人团——书评作者指出它们必须先通过它们要修的那些有缺陷的制度。这个悖论是真的。但更值得注意的是接下来那一步：Klein 退回到"多关注州和地方政治"。书评作者的反驳是硬的：学校融合、保障房建设，地方冲突一样充满象征和身份；而碳经济、移民执法、疫情应对，地方政府根本没有能力处理。把注意力从联邦政治挪到社区，听起来像疗愈，实际是把最需要全国性权力介入的分配问题，交给最没有权力的一方。

报道里有一组数字值得停一下：2019 年，41% 的选民自称独立，30% 自称民主党，28% 自称共和党。Gallup 的记录里，独立派人数在 2011 年首次达到 40%。Klein 对此的处理是——独立是一种个人品牌。书评作者问得好：如果政治心理真的在把美国人推向党派身份，为什么这么多人拒绝党派标签？答案可能很简单：他们不是没有立场，他们是不想加入那两个正在互相确认对方是敌人的阵营。这种拒绝本身是一种表达，而 Klein 把它读成了虚荣。

结尾那句"唯一的出路是穿过去"，是整篇书评最锋利也最含糊的地方。它承认了：去极化不可能靠调和完成，只能靠一方彻底击败另一方。但击败谁、为了什么、代价由谁承担，书评没有说。它说美国政治系统的健康取决于共和党的失败——这是一个判断，不是一个方案。真正的问题在于，如果"更加极化"只是让两个阵营的仇恨更纯粹，而没有改变谁在承受政策后果，那么穿过去之后，只是换了一批人继续共谋。

书评作者漏掉的那个词是：谁。谁在把极化当成不用改结构的借口，谁在从"人天生如此"里获得免责，谁在地方政治的美好叙事里被留在原地。这些名字，报道里一个都没有出现。

The most honest sentence in this review comes last: to fight polarization, we'll have to get much more polarized. Twenty paragraphs explain why Americans hate each other, and only the final line admits the hatred isn't the problem. The problem is where it's pointed.

Ezra Klein's explanation is evolutionary psychology: the tribal brain is hardwired to fear the out-group and cling to the in-group. The reviewer already lands one blow—if the American brain craves tight partisan conflict, why have multiparty systems and coalition governments survived so long elsewhere? The blade can go deeper: the personality typology that says openness is liberal and conscientiousness is conservative is refuted on the spot by William F. Buckley, a three-language-speaking New Yorker who founded the National Review and defended cultural traditionalism. The reviewer is precise: this typology applies almost exclusively to affluent white people.

What Klein quietly sets aside is material politics. Economic class is the identity he mentions least. Yet the report is clear: African Americans and Latinos are intensely religious and socially conservative on many issues, but far more Democratic than the population at large—because generous social programs, a fairer criminal justice system, a humane immigration policy serve their material interests. That is not temperament. That is arithmetic. When someone votes for health care and family safety, explaining them as "conscientious" or "open" translates a structural problem into a personality problem and then declares it unsolvable.

This is where complicity enters. Attributing polarization to immutable psychology says: institutions need not change, because people are just like that. Klein's reforms—ending the filibuster, abolishing the Electoral College—must first pass through the flawed institutions they are meant to fix. That catch-22 is real. But watch the next move: Klein retreats to "pay more attention to state and local politics." The reviewer's rebuttal is hard. School integration and affordable housing ignite local conflicts just as saturated with symbolism and identity; carbon, immigration enforcement, and pandemic response are beyond local capacity. Shifting attention from federal politics to the community sounds like healing. In practice it hands the most consequential distributional questions to the level of government least able to answer them.

One set of numbers deserves a pause: in 2019, 41 percent of the electorate called itself independent, 30 percent Democratic, 28 percent Republican. Gallup first recorded independents at 40 percent in 2011. Klein's treatment: independence is a personal brand. The reviewer asks the right question—if political psychology is driving Americans toward partisan identity, why do so many refuse the label? The answer may be simple: they are not without positions. They decline to join two camps busy confirming each other as enemies. That refusal is itself an expression, and Klein reads it as vanity.

The closing line—the only way out is through—is the sharpest and vaguest part of the review. It concedes that depolarization cannot be achieved by reconciliation, only by one side decisively defeating the other. But defeat whom, for what, and at whose cost? The review doesn't say. It says the health of the American political system depends on the Republican Party's defeat. That is a judgment, not a plan. The real question: if "getting more polarized" only purifies the hatred on both sides without changing who bears the consequences of policy, then after we pass through, a new set of people will keep doing the conspiring.

The word the review leaves out is who. Who uses polarization as an excuse not to touch the structure, who is absolved by "human nature," who is left standing when the lovely story of local politics is told. None of those names appear in the report.

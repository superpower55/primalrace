年度专辑榜，谁在定义好音乐Who Gets to Price a Year of Music

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
品味没有高下，定价权有。
Taste has no hierarchy. Pricing does.

Billboard 的年度五十佳专辑，是一件关于定价权的产品，不是关于音乐的产品。

榜单开篇先写清楚自己的时间坐标：12 月 12 日 Billboard Music Awards，12 月 13 日年榜揭晓。一份编辑部的私人喜好清单，被安排在两个商业节点之间发布，它的功能就不是记录这一年发生了什么，而是替这一年定价——哪些声音算“big, big pop albums”，哪些人算“rising stars”，谁值得在颁奖季被讨论。这是第三章那个入口：有话语权的人给别人的作品贴标签、定价值，然后标签自己会长腿跑。

看它怎么排。The Cure 十六年来第一张专辑放在第一位，评语写“a perfect soundtrack for our dark times”——给时代定性，再给专辑发一张时代的通行证。Beyoncé 的 Cowboy Carter 被写成对乡村音乐守门人的“repudiation, reclamation”，一个黑人女性被这个流派拒之门外，然后由这个榜单来宣布她赢回了它。这些话不是假的，写得还挺好。但宣布谁赢回了一个流派的人，自己就站在发奖的位置上。

再看这些评语里的女性是怎么被描述的。Sabrina Carpenter 的专辑被夸“12 songs that could each stand alone on pop radio”，落脚点是电台可用性；Ariana Grande 被写成“delicate-but-danceable 35-minute reflection”，长度、可舞性、私人生活被公开审视，三样并置；Charli XCX 的部分大半在讲 Brat summer 怎么卷进了美国大选，Harris 被 XCX 亲自“anointed as brat”。一个女歌手的年度成就，最后被叙述成她给一场总统竞选提供了流量。这就是元暴力最日常的样子：不是谁骂了谁，而是女性的作品总要借一个更大的框架才被承认其重要性，那个框架通常是男人的政治。

榜单里也不是没有裂缝。Mannequin Pussy 那条引了“Not a single motherf–er who has tried to lock me up could get the collar ‘round my neck”，St. Vincent 那条特意写了这是她第一次独自担任制作人、自己的厂牌发的第一张唱片——“I don’t think I would have written these songs or explored this stuff without the solitude”。制作权和发行权落到女性手里，这是 structural 层的一小步，比任何一句“empowering”的评语都实。但它在整份榜单里是注脚，不是主线。

整份清单里，被明确写出“谁付了什么代价”的条目，我数不出来。Kali Uchis 那条提到她在专辑宣传期发现自己怀了第一个孩子，然后这句话就过去了，接着讲 bolero、dembow、Afrobeats。生育被当作一条人物花絮，不是一件改变了这张唱片生产条件的事。这不是 Billboard 的疏忽，这是整个行业的默认设置：作品是产品，生产它的人的身体不在报表里。

所以这份榜单最该被问的问题不是“凭什么没有某某”，而是：五十个席位、几十位撰稿人、一套评分口径，谁坐在编辑会议上，谁的声音被算作“staff picks”，谁的品味被默认为可以代表全体。报道没写。撰稿人名字后面挂着一串缩写，性别、位置、他们各自为哪些歌手写过封面故事，一概不表。

一份年度榜单最诚实的地方，是它承认自己在排序。最不诚实的地方，是它假装排序等于评价。

Billboard's 50 Best Albums of 2024 is a product about pricing power, not about music.

The list announces its own coordinates up front: the Billboard Music Awards on Dec. 12, the Year-End Charts on Dec. 13. A staff picks list wedged between two commercial moments isn't there to record the year. It's there to price it — which records count as "big, big pop albums," who counts as a "rising star," whose name gets discussed during awards season. This is the third chapter's entry point: people with话语权 stick labels on other people's work and set its value, and the labels grow legs.

Look at the ordering. The Cure's first album in 16 years sits at number one, praised as "a perfect soundtrack for our dark times" — the era gets defined first, then the album is issued a passport into it. Beyoncé's Cowboy Carter is framed as a "repudiation, reclamation" of country's gatekeepers: a Black woman pushed out of a genre, and this list announces she has taken it back. None of that is false. It's well written. But the people announcing who reclaimed a genre are standing where the prizes get handed out.

Now look at how the women in these blurbs get described. Sabrina Carpenter's album is praised because "12 songs... could each stand alone on pop radio" — the measure is radio usability. Ariana Grande's is a "delicate-but-danceable 35-minute reflection," where runtime, danceability, and public scrutiny of her private life sit in one sentence as equals. Charli XCX's entry spends most of its space on how Brat summer got pulled into a US presidential campaign, how Harris was "anointed as brat" by XCX herself. A woman's album of the year ends up narrated as fuel for an election. That is meta-violence in its most ordinary form: not someone insulting someone, but a woman's work needing a bigger frame to be granted importance, and that frame is usually men's politics.

There are cracks. The Mannequin Pussy entry quotes "Not a single motherf–er who has tried to lock me up could get the collar 'round my neck." The St. Vincent entry notes this is her first album as sole producer, the first release on her own label — "I don't think I would have written these songs or explored this stuff without the solitude." Production and distribution landing in a woman's hands is a small structural step, worth more than any blurb that says "empowering." But it's a footnote in this list, not the spine.

I cannot find a single entry that spells out who paid what for the record. Kali Uchis's blurb mentions she found out she was pregnant during the album rollout — and then the sentence moves on to bolero, dembow, Afrobeats. A pregnancy is filed as a character detail, not as something that changed the conditions under which the album was made. That isn't Billboard's oversight. It's the industry's default setting: the work is the product, and the body that produced it isn't on the spreadsheet.

The question this list most needs isn't "why isn't X on here." It's: fifty slots, dozens of writers, one scoring rubric — who sits in that editorial meeting, whose voice counts as a "staff pick," whose taste is presumed to represent everyone. The piece doesn't say. The bylines are initials. Gender, location, which artists they've written cover stories for — none of it disclosed.

The most honest thing a year-end list does is admit it is ranking. The least honest thing it does is pretend ranking is evaluation.

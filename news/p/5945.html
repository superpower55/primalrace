共和党的选战只剩人身攻击The GOP's Closing Argument Is Just Personal Attacks

国际 文化层 · 元暴力 The New York Times ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
当政策拿不出手，攻击人身就成了政策。
When policy fails, attack the person.

共和党今年的竞选最后一周，打的是人的身体和身份，不是政策。

Kansas 的参议员 Roger Marshall 说他的民主党对手“probably is grooming”儿童，没有任何证据。Texas 的副总统 JD Vance 在集会上暗示参议院候选人 James Talarico 是同性恋，还说他“痴迷跨性别儿童”，要看他浏览器的历史记录。Trump 把 Talarico 叫成“Talafreako”，把南卡罗来纳的民主党候选人 Annie Andrews 叫成“Tranny Annie”——她不是跨性别者。Michigan 的 Abdul El-Sayed 被 Trump 说成“vicious, violent jihadist”，Montana 参议员 Tim Sheehy 在集会上说对面是“hates this country”的“radical Islamist”。

这些不是失言，是策略。报道写得很清楚：共和党面对的是物价上涨和总统的深度不受欢迎，于是“tarred their opponents with false, inflammatory and deeply personal attacks”。前参议员 Jeff Flake 说这是“a new level”。Fordham 的政治学教授 Christina Greer 说这是“the worst”。

这套东西在书里叫表达的武器化，入口是身份。不需要论证对方的政策错在哪里，只需要把对方定义成一个不该存在的人：groomer、jihadist、变态、怪胎。定义一旦成立，政策辩论就不必发生了。这是最省力的打法，也是最贵的打法——贵在谁身上，报道没写。

代价落在被点名的人身上，也落在他们代表的群体身上。El-Sayed 自己在集会上说得很直白：“my name and my faith are not the reason you can't afford your gas.” 这句话是整篇报道里唯一一句把攻击还原成攻击的话。Michigan 的民主党市长 Mike McGuinness 说这些攻击是“Hall of Fame contender”。底特律的牧师 Horace Sheffield III 说这种调子“very dangerous”，而且“all based on prejudice”。

报道里有一个细节值得停一下：共和党全国参议院委员会的女发言人 Bernadette Breslin 说，如果 Talarico 不想被叫反肉食者，“he shouldn't have announced his campaign would go vegan”——而 Talarico 说他不是素食者，他的竞选“basically”靠烧烤。也就是说，连攻击的事实基础都是现编的，编完再让对方自证。

同一篇报道里，共和党在 Ohio 州长选战投放的一支广告，把民主党对手 Amy Acton 的母亲请出来，讲她 12 岁起就没和母亲说过话，因为继父被控强奸她。Ohio 的共和党州长 Mike DeWine 说这支广告“deeply, deeply troubling”。Obama 说他不明白“the standards of behavior have gotten so low”。

这些攻击里有一条线是共通的：把女性、少数族裔、移民、跨性别者当成可以随便贴标签的容器，贴完就走，不用负责。这不是竞选风格问题，是元暴力在日常政治里的运行方式——解释权在谁手里，谁就能决定一个人“是什么”，而不必回答她“做了什么”。

报道最后没有写的是：这些被攻击的人，最后赢没赢，输了之后那些标签还在不在。标签是留得下的，选票是留不下的。

In the final week of this campaign, Republicans are running against people's bodies and identities, not their policies.

In Kansas, Senator Roger Marshall said his Democratic opponent "probably is grooming" children, with no evidence. In Texas, Vice President JD Vance suggested at a rally that Senate candidate James Talarico is gay and said he wanted to see the man's browser history over his past support for transgender youth. Trump calls Talarico "Talafreako" and called South Carolina's Democratic nominee, Dr. Annie Andrews, "Tranny Annie" — she is not transgender. In Michigan, Trump described Dr. Abdul El-Sayed as a "vicious, violent jihadist," and Senator Tim Sheehy of Montana told a crowd that the other side had a "radical Islamist who hates this country."

These are not slips. The report is explicit: facing a backlash over rising prices and the president's deep unpopularity, Republicans "tarred their opponents with false, inflammatory and deeply personal attacks." Former Senator Jeff Flake calls it "a new level." Christina Greer, a political science professor at Fordham, calls it "the worst."

This is the weaponization of expression through the identity entrance. You don't have to argue that your opponent's policies are wrong. You just define them as someone who shouldn't exist: groomer, jihadist, freak, pervert. Once the definition lands, the policy debate never has to happen. It's the cheapest play available, and the most expensive — expensive for whom, the report doesn't say.

The cost lands on the people named, and on the groups they stand for. El-Sayed put it plainly at his own rally: "my name and my faith are not the reason you can't afford your gas." That is the only line in the piece that restores an attack to what it is. Mike McGuinness, the Democratic mayor of Pontiac, calls the attacks on El-Sayed a "Hall of Fame contender." The Rev. Horace Sheffield III of Detroit says the tone is "very dangerous" and "all based on prejudice."

One detail is worth stopping on: Bernadette Breslin, a spokeswoman for the National Republican Senatorial Committee, said that "if Talarico didn't want to be called anti-meat, he shouldn't have announced his campaign would go vegan." Talarico says he is not a vegan and that his campaign "basically" runs on barbecue. The factual basis of the attack is invented, and the target is then asked to prove it wrong.

The same report describes an Ohio governor's race ad from Vivek Ramaswamy's campaign featuring the mother of his Democratic opponent, Dr. Amy Acton, who says she has not spoken to her mother since she was 12, after her stepfather was charged with raping her. Ohio's Republican governor, Mike DeWine, called the ad "deeply, deeply troubling." Obama said he was baffled that "the standards of behavior have gotten so low."

There is one thread running through all of it: women, minorities, immigrants, trans people are treated as containers you can slap a label on and walk away from. This is not a matter of campaign style. It is meta-violence running in everyday politics — whoever holds the interpretive monopoly gets to decide what a person *is*, without having to answer for what she *does*.

What the report doesn't say is whether the people attacked won, and whether the labels outlived the votes. Labels tend to stay. Votes don't.

中东的和平，签在能源合同里Middle East Peace, Signed in Energy Contracts

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要中国外长王毅在北京会见伊朗最高国家安全委员会副秘书巴盖里·卡尼，称对话谈判是「唯一可行之路」；习近平接待1978年中阿建交以来首位访华的阿曼国家元首海赛姆苏丹，双方签署涵盖人工智能、绿色能源、供应链的14份合作文件及本币互换协议。中方同时宣布中东问题特使翟隽本月访问伊朗、阿曼和沙特。
The newsChinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi met Ali Bagheri Kani, deputy secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, in Beijing, calling dialogue 'the only viable path forward'. President Xi hosted Oman's Sultan Haitham, the first Omani head of state to visit China since 1978; the two sides signed 14 cooperation documents and a currency swap deal. Beijing also said envoy Zhai Jun will visit Iran, Oman and Saudi Arabia this month.
北京喊的是对话，签的是能源和本币互换。
Beijing calls for dialogue and signs for oil and currency swaps.

王毅对伊朗人说，对话和谈判是「唯一可行之路」。同一周，习近平和阿曼苏丹签了14份文件：人工智能、绿色能源、供应链，还有一份本币互换协议。声明里呼吁霍尔木兹海峡恢复安全通航，呼吁美伊重启对话。这两句话放在一起看，就没什么好分析的：北京要的不是和平，是油路。

这不虚伪，虚伪的是包装。报道写得很清楚——中国是伊朗最大的贸易伙伴，最大的伊朗原油买家；霍尔木兹在海峡被战争卡住之前，承载着全球五分之一的能源出口；阿曼的很多港口在海峡之外，所以阿曼「尤其重要」。外交辞令底下是一张物流地图。中国的外交部在做的，是一家能源进口国在战争期间重新布线，这本身是理性的国家行为。问题出在它把自己说成别的东西。

「中东和海湾不应成为大国竞争和地缘冲突的竞技场」——习近平说这句话的时候，正在和海湾国家签人工智能和供应链协议。竞技场这个词选得很妙：它把介入描述成阻止介入。这是第三章那个机制的教科书版本，认知入口的争夺，只不过这次入场券不是「解放女性」也不是「大规模杀伤性武器」，是「对话」。

半岛电视台记者的判断比北京的声明诚实：中国「非常沮丧」，它想被看见进入了这个进程。前几个月它没做什么，现在做了，因为它急。急什么，报道没写，但报道写了它最大的贸易伙伴和最大的原油客户是谁，写了海峡卡着五分之一的世界能源出口。急的是油，不是加沙。

真正值得盯的是那14份文件里没有的东西。声明呼吁海峡恢复通航，呼吁美伊重启对话——但谁去保证？中国对伊朗有贸易杠杆，报道说得很明白，可它没有说中国拿这个杠杆换任何具体的东西：没有停火条件，没有核查机制，没有对任何一方的施压承诺。呼吁是免费的。签署文件是有价的。这场外交的账本上，只有一边有数字。

至于伊朗和阿曼的人在这场战争里承受了什么，报道一个字没写。它写的是流量、港口、原油、协议。人在哪里？在「冲突拖长并可能进一步外溢」这句被动语态里，被外溢掉的那些人。

Wang Yi told the Iranians that dialogue and negotiation remain 'the only viable path forward'. The same week, Xi Jinping signed 14 documents with Oman's sultan: AI, green energy, supply chains, plus a currency swap. The joint statement calls for safe navigation through Hormuz to resume and for the US and Iran to restart talks. Put the two together and there is nothing left to analyse: Beijing doesn't want peace, it wants the oil route.

That isn't hypocrisy. The hypocrisy is the packaging. The report is explicit — China is Iran's biggest trading partner and biggest customer of Iranian crude; before the war restricted traffic, one-fifth of global energy exports moved through Hormuz; many Omani ports sit outside the strait, which is why Oman is 'particularly important'. Under the diplomatic language is a logistics map. A major energy importer rewiring its supply lines during a war is rational state behaviour. The problem is that it insists on calling it something else.

'The Middle East and the Gulf should not become an arena for great-power rivalry and geopolitical conflict,' Xi said, while signing AI and supply-chain deals with a Gulf state. 'Arena' is a well-chosen word: it describes intervention as the prevention of intervention. This is the textbook mechanism from chapter three, the fight for the cognitive entry point — except this time the ticket isn't 'liberating women' or 'weapons of mass destruction'. It's 'dialogue'.

Al Jazeera's correspondent is more honest than the statement: China is 'very frustrated' and 'wants to be seen to enter the process'. For months it did little; now it is doing something, because it is impatient. Impatient about what, the report doesn't say — but it does say who China's biggest trading partner and biggest crude customer is, and that the strait carries a fifth of world energy exports. The impatience is about oil, not Gaza.

What deserves attention is what isn't in those 14 documents. The statement calls for navigation to resume and for talks to restart — guaranteed by whom? China has leverage over Iran, the report makes that plain, but it never says China traded that leverage for anything concrete: no ceasefire terms, no verification mechanism, no commitment to pressure either side. Calls are free. Signed documents have a price. On this diplomatic ledger, only one side has numbers.

As for what the people of Iran and Oman are enduring in this war, the report says nothing. It says flows, ports, crude, agreements. Where are the people? Inside that passive phrase about conflicts that 'drag on and risk further spillover' — the ones being spilled over.

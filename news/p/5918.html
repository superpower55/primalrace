咖啡馆关门，记忆被重新定价A Cafe Closes, a Memory Gets Repriced

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
租约到期不是自然死亡，是有人算过账的清除。
The lease didn't expire. Someone decided the block was worth more without it.

Exarchia 的 Mouria 关了。报道把它写成租约到期、房东不续租、文化部没有介入——三个被动句连在一起，像一场天气。但租约是有人不续的，文化部是有人决定不介入的，游客数量十年翻倍是有人一笔一笔投出来的。这不是一家咖啡馆的老去，是一个街区的定价权换手。

44 年，店主 Christos Vanas 每天早晨 7 点第一个进门，周六 5 点就开始给外面街市的摊贩倒 tsipouro。他的女儿 Areti 说得很清楚：菜单没改，价格没涨，不卖 Aperol spritz 和拿铁。这句话是整篇报道里最锋利的一句——它说的不是怀旧，是拒绝进入那套定价体系。一个地方只要不按新的价格表营业，它就会从“资产”变成“低效占用”。7500 人签名请愿，要求文化部把咖啡馆列为受保护遗产，文化部没有动作。保护的入口存在，只是没开。

报道里那位社会学家 Nicos Souliotis 用了“复仇主义城市”这个词：国家政策明确指向施加社会纪律与控制。这句话值得停一下。Exarchia 的反权威传统可以追到雅典理工学院的起义，那场起义的死难学生被认为促成了 1974 年军政府垮台。一个街区记着这件事，本身就是一种表达——出现在哪里、和谁坐在一起、喝什么、不喝什么。表达从来不是装饰，它决定这个街区是谁的。所以清空它不需要发文件，只需要让它的地标一个个消失：咖啡馆不续租，广场修地铁站、警力增加，居民失去唯一的开阔聚集地。结构层和文化层同时动手，谁都不必承认自己在动手。

Maria Tsampra 说这是“抹除记忆”，是“通过重写历史来控制我们”。她说得对，但还可以再往前一步：重写历史之前，先重写价格。英文招牌、短租公寓、把左翼老巢做成旅游线路——最后这一条是整件事里最赤裸的一笔。当旅行平台开始组织游客参观“硬核左派常去的据点”，这个街区就已经被当成一件可售卖的表达：它的反抗性成了它的卖点，而卖点一旦成立，真正反抗的人就成了妨碍生意的东西。Areti 说“有旅行团就站在咖啡馆门口”，然后她低头看自己的鞋。这句话里没有愤怒，只有一种已经算过账的疲惫。

报道顺带提到，希腊各地的传统 kafeneia 都在消失，村庄里的那些一走，人口流失就加速。这不是巧合，是同一套机制在不同尺度上跑：先抽掉人免费聚集的地方，再让人相信聚集本身已经过时。Mouria 的常客里有退休律师、演了六十多年戏的演员、倒闭左翼报纸的前员工，他们四十年里每周来四次，不预约，不消费套餐，只是来。这种关系没法被计入任何一张报表，所以它在报表上等于零。

周日之后，那扇铁框大门关上，马赛克地面还在，只是不再有人踩。街区会继续被叫作 Exarchia，招牌会换成英文，游客会来拍照。真正的问题不是这家店能不能保住——7500 个签名已经回答了人们想保——而是谁有权决定一个地方该值多少钱，以及当这个决定做出时，为什么被决定的人只能签名。

Mouria in Exarchia has closed. The Guardian frames it as a lease running out, a landlord not renewing, a culture ministry failing to intervene — three passive constructions in a row, as if this were weather. But a lease is not renewed by a person, a ministry declines to act by a decision, and tourist numbers doubling over a decade is something somebody invested in, booking by booking. This is not a cafe growing old. It is a neighbourhood changing hands.

For 44 years Christos Vanas has been the first man through the door at 7am, pouring tsipouro for the street-market stallholders by 5 on Saturdays. His daughter Areti says the important thing: they didn't change the menu, didn't raise prices, didn't serve Aperol spritzes or cafe lattes. That is the sharpest line in the piece. It is not nostalgia, it is a refusal to enter a pricing system. A place that won't run on the new price list stops being an asset and becomes inefficient occupancy. Seven and a half thousand people signed a petition asking the culture ministry to grant the cafe protected heritage status. The ministry did not move. The door existed. Nobody opened it.

The sociologist Nicos Souliotis calls it the "revanchist city": state policy aimed at imposing social discipline and control. Worth pausing on. Exarchia's anti-authoritarian lineage runs back to the Polytechnic uprising, whose dead students are credited with toppling the junta in 1974. A neighbourhood that remembers this is itself an expression — where you appear, who you sit with, what you drink, what you refuse to drink. Expression is never decoration; it decides whose street this is. So you don't need a decree to empty it. You let the landmarks go one by one: the lease lapses, a metro station arrives with more policing, residents lose their only open square. Structural and cultural violence move together, and neither has to admit it is moving.

Maria Tsampra calls it erasing memory, controlling people by rewriting history. She's right, and it goes one step further: before history gets rewritten, the price gets rewritten. English signage, short-term rentals, and — the most naked detail in the whole story — travel platforms running tours of the haunts of hardened leftists. Once the neighbourhood's resistance becomes the product, the people who actually resist become an obstacle to business. Areti says tour groups stand outside the cafe. Then she stares at her boots. That isn't rage. That's the exhaustion of someone who has already done the math.

The piece notes that traditional kafeneia are vanishing across Greece, and that in villages their disappearance turbocharges depopulation. Not a coincidence — the same mechanism at different scales. First remove the places where people gather for free, then let them believe gathering itself is obsolete. Mouria's regulars include a retired barrister, an actor of sixty-odd years on stage, a former employee of a defunct left-wing daily. They came four times a week for decades, no reservation, no set menu. That relationship appears on no spreadsheet, so on the spreadsheet it is zero.

On Sunday the iron-framed doors shut. The mosaic floor stays. The neighbourhood will still be called Exarchia, the signs will be in English, the tourists will take photos. The real question is not whether this one cafe could have been saved — 7,500 signatures already answered that people wanted it saved — but who gets to decide what a place is worth, and why, when that decision is made, the people it's made about can only sign their names.

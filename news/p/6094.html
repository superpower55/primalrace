读书不是为了穿在身上Reading Is Not for Wearing

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 The Cut ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
新闻摘要随着Sarah J. Maas的《荆棘与玫瑰之庭》（ACOTAR）系列新书《A Court of Splintered Harmony》发行，多个品牌推出了相关联名商品。Alex + Ani、Knix、For Love & Lemons、Target和Barnes & Noble等品牌推出了从首饰、内衣到服饰、家居用品的系列产品。
The newsWith the release of Sarah J. Maas's new ACOTAR book, A Court of Splintered Harmony, brands including Alex + Ani, Knix, For Love & Lemons, Target and Barnes & Noble have launched tie-in merchandise ranging from jewelry and intimates to apparel and home goods.
把小说做成内衣，卖的是身份，不是故事
They turned a novel into lingerie and sold identity, not story.

《The Cut》这篇导购写得很坦白：过去在公共场合读书就是炫耀，现在炫耀的方式是买周边。这句话是整篇文章唯一诚实的地方。它承认了这些联名卖的不是阅读，是身份信号。

ACOTAR 是 romantasy 类型，浪漫爱加奇幻。第三章讲过，浪漫爱叙事是武器化最持久的入口之一，钻石恒久远是商业 scam，这本书的周边链条也一样：Alex + Ani 的手链 38 美元起，捆绑包 174 美元；Knix 把内衣分成小说里的“courts”，25 到 165 美元，还让你做个测验看自己属于哪个 court；For Love & Lemons 卖亮片长裙、蕾丝内衣、穆勒鞋。买的是“我是这本书的忠实读者”这个标签，标签的价格由品牌定。

定价权在谁手里很清楚。作者 Sarah J. Maas 的世界被拆成 courts、角色、场景，再被品牌重新包装成缎面睡裙和内衣。Knix 那一栏尤其值得停一下：防漏内裤做成 ACOTAR 主题，内衣按“court”分类。一本以女性读者为主的小说，周边落点是内衣和蕾丝，这个落点不是偶然的。读者被训练成用身体穿戴来表达对文本的爱，表达被收编成了消费。

文章说这些周边“you wouldn't mind wearing out on a date night”。date night。浪漫爱叙事从书里延伸到衣柜，再延伸到约会。闭环完成了：读浪漫小说，买浪漫周边，穿去约会，继续读下一本。

Target 和 Barnes & Noble 那两栏卖的是书签、托特包、拼图、变色马克杯，看起来无害，但逻辑一样：把阅读体验切成可售卖的单位。文章反复出现“New! You can now save this product for later.”，这是电商页面的原样搬运，导购文和货架之间没有距离。

这不是在说读者不该买周边。问题在于，当“表达你爱这本书”的唯一通道是消费，表达就被定价了。谁定价，谁就决定了什么样的爱是可见的。第一〇章说过，表达是存在的确证；这里存在被确证的方式，是收据。

文章没写这些联名给作者、给品牌、给平台各分多少，也没写这些衣服在哪里生产、谁在缝。读者名单里没有这些人的名字。

The Cut's shopping guide is refreshingly honest for one sentence: reading in public used to be the flex, now it's merch. That sentence admits what the whole thing is. These collabs don't sell reading. They sell a status signal.

ACOTAR is romantasy, romance plus fantasy. Chapter 3 already covered this: the romance narrative is one of the most durable weaponized entry points. Diamonds are forever was a commercial scam, and this merch chain runs on the same logic. Alex + Ani bracelets start at $38, a bundle runs $174. Knix sorts its underwear into the novel's "courts," $25 to $165, and lets you take a quiz to find out which court you belong to. For Love & Lemons sells sequined gowns, lacy lingerie, mules. What you're buying is the label "devoted reader," and the brands set the price.

It's clear who holds pricing power. Sarah J. Maas's world gets chopped into courts, characters, scenes, then repackaged as satin slips and intimates. The Knix entry deserves a pause: leakproof briefs with an ACOTAR theme, lingerie sorted by court. A series with a mostly female readership, and the merch lands on underwear and lace. That landing spot isn't an accident. Readers get trained to wear their love for the text on the body, and expression gets absorbed into consumption.

The article says these pieces are things "you wouldn't mind wearing out on a date night." Date night. The romance narrative extends from the book to the closet to the date. The loop closes: read romance, buy romance merch, wear it on a date, read the next one.

The Target and Barnes & Noble sections sell bookmarks, totes, puzzles, color-changing mugs. Harmless-looking, same logic: the reading experience gets sliced into sellable units. The line "New! You can now save this product for later" repeats throughout. That's e-commerce copy pasted straight from the product page. There is no distance between the guide and the shelf.

This isn't an argument that readers shouldn't buy merch. The problem is that when consumption is the only channel for expressing love of a book, expression gets priced. Whoever prices it decides what kind of love is visible. Chapter 1 said expression is the confirmation of existence. Here, existence gets confirmed by receipt.

What the article doesn't say: how the money splits between author, brand, and platform; where the clothes are made; who sews them. Those names are absent from the reader list.

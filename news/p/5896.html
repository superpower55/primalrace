取暖费账单上的战争The War on the Heating Bill

国际 直接层 · 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
战争的代价从不平均分摊，它先落到最付不起的人身上。
War's cost is never shared equally. It lands first on those least able to pay.

特朗普说这是"为美国乃至世界的安全与和平付出的很小的代价"。对一个靠社保过日子的家庭来说，这个"很小的代价"是878美元——一个冬天的取暖费多出来的部分。这不是代价小，是付代价的人不在他的账本上。

加尔通的公式是 Violence = Potential − Actual。这场战争本身是直接暴力，但它真正落地的方式是结构层的：取暖油和柴油是同一种石油产品，柴油涨价抬高运输、农业、建筑的成本，这些成本最后都进了家庭账单。而取暖油不像汽油，你没法靠少开车省下来——天冷了，油箱不能空着。一个家庭在战争面前没有任何议价能力，它只能选择少吃饭、少吃药，或者把暖气关到不舒服的程度。Consumer Reports 的调查里，超过四分之一的家庭已经把供暖制冷调到不舒服的水平，19%的人削减了食品开支或换成更便宜的食物。这已经是结构暴力在身体上留下的痕迹。

更值得拆的是那套叙事。战争被包装成"安全与和平"，油价被许诺"像石头一样掉下来"。这套话术的机制和书里讲的一样：先制造一个宏大的意义，再把具体的代价挪到看不见的地方。文章自己说了，就算明天停战，炼油厂要修、航线要重开、库存要补，这要几个月。而家庭的取暖账单不能等几个月。许诺的价格下跌是未来的事，账单是现在的事。

Mark Wolfe 的诉求很具体：国会给 LIHEAP 追加30亿美元，并临时扩大资格覆盖那些收入不够低、却也付不起账单的工薪家庭。这是 structural 层的一次具体修补，不是表演。但他也点出了更根本的问题：美国一直用"我们产了多少油气"来衡量能源安全，可产得再多也挡不住几千英里外一场战争带来的价格冲击。"太阳能没有霍尔木兹海峡"——这句话是全文最锋利的地方，它把能源安全从"产量"重新定义为"会不会被战争定价"。

这篇文章没有说的是：谁从这场战争里赚钱。油价上涨的收益流向哪里，报道没写。但账是清楚的——一边是军工和能源的利润，一边是靠社保过冬的家庭。共谋不需要密谋，只需要一套让代价转移显得自然的叙事。

冬天会过去，战争也会结束。但"下一场几千英里外的战争，是否还能决定他们能不能取暖"——这个问题，国会在这个冬天之前不回答，账单会替他们回答。

Trump called higher oil prices from the war "a very small price to pay for USA, and world, safety and peace." For a family living on social security, that very small price is $878 — the extra cost of heating a home for one winter. The price isn't small. The people paying it just aren't on his ledger.

Galtung's formula is Violence = Potential − Actual. The war itself is direct violence, but the way it actually lands is structural: heating oil and diesel are the same petroleum product, so higher diesel prices raise the cost of moving food, running farms, operating construction equipment — and those costs end up in household bills. Heating oil isn't like gasoline; you can't conserve your way out of it by driving less. When it gets cold, the tank can't stay empty. A family has no bargaining power against a war. It can only choose to eat less, skip medicine, or turn the heat down to an uncomfortable level. In the Consumer Reports survey, more than a quarter of families had already done exactly that, and 19% had cut back on groceries or switched to cheaper food. That is structural violence leaving marks on bodies.

What deserves more scrutiny is the narrative. The war is packaged as "safety and peace," and fuel prices are promised to fall "like a rock." The mechanism is familiar: manufacture a grand meaning, then move the concrete cost somewhere out of sight. The article itself notes that even if the war ended tomorrow, damaged refineries need repairs, shipping routes must reopen, inventories must be replenished — months of work. A family's heating bill cannot wait months. The promised price drop is a future event. The bill is now.

Wolfe's demand is concrete: Congress should add $3bn to LIHEAP and temporarily expand eligibility to working families who earn too much to qualify but still can't pay. That is a specific repair at the structural layer, not a performance. But he also names the deeper problem: America has measured energy security by how much oil and gas it produces, and producing more at home hasn't protected families from price shocks caused by wars overseas. "There is no strait of Hormuz for solar power" is the sharpest line in the piece — it redefines energy security from "how much we produce" to "whether a war can set the price."

What the article doesn't say is who profits from this war. The report doesn't go there. But the ledger is clear: on one side, defense and energy profits; on the other, families on social security trying to get through winter. Conspiracy doesn't require a secret meeting. It only requires a narrative that makes the transfer of cost look natural.

Winter will pass. The war will end. But the question — whether the next war thousands of miles away can still determine whether they can afford to heat their homes — is one Congress can answer before this winter, or the bills will answer for them.

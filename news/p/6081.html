亿万富翁的选票，比你的贵A Billionaire's Ballot Costs More Than Yours

国际 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
他们不怕多交5%，怕的是这个先例。
They don't fear the 5%. They fear the precedent.

加州这场公投里，最贵的不是广告，是解释权。

Prop. 40 要对净资产超过十亿美元的部分一次性抽 5%，钱拿去补医疗补助和教育预算的窟窿。加州三大富翁俱乐部立刻掏出 3.33 亿美元，组了个叫 Building a Better California 的组织，把整个州的选举版面刷满。三个亿，是它前五大金主净资产合计的 0.1%。这就是这里的定价方式：他们花出去的钱，只相当于自己财产的千分之一，就足以买下整场公共讨论。

注意这个数字的方向。3.33 亿不是他们的成本，是他们的找零。

然后他们上了毒丸。Prop. 41 和 Prop. 42，名字取得像补充条款，干的事是把 Prop. 40 从内部掏空：一个让未来的税收公投先过审计、并把不符合 1979 年支出上限的收入直接作废，一个禁止追溯征税、禁止未来对金融资产征税。两个都通过、且 Prop. 41 票数更高，Prop. 40 就自动失效。这不是辩论，这是把对手的选项从选票上剪掉。制造可能性的艺术，做到这个份上，连遮掩都懒得遮。

最值得看的是谁在替他们说话。纽森反对 Prop. 40，理由是会吓跑亿万富翁，抽垮税基。Katie Porter 录视频列了三条反对理由，第三条就是“一次性税收会造成长期伤害，把几千亿潜在税收赶出加州”。这个说法出自胡佛研究所的一份分析，而帮 Prop. 40 算账的经济学家已经逐条反驳过。报道写得很清楚：做出这类预测的人，要么有议程，要么是“妄想”。

共谋不需要收钱。纽森和盖蒂家族关系密切，还大概率要竞选总统，他需要亿万富翁站在他这边。Porter 刚输掉州长初选。他们说的每一句“为加州经济着想”，回报都写在别处。这不是腐败，这是更日常的东西：一个人在结构里找到了自己的最优解表达，然后把这个表达包装成公共利益。

Prop. 42 的包装更下作。它把“退休账户和投资账户”写进禁止征税的清单，暗示普通人一辈子的积蓄要被动。纽森照着这个恐惧念稿：“我们都同意，加州人理应让自己的退休金和终身储蓄免受新州税。”可 Prop. 40 只碰十亿美元以上的净资产。他们的退休金不在射程内。恐惧是免费的，而它有效。

另一边，黄仁勋说交税是“特权”，Ro Khanna 说“AI 革命发生在湾区，不在迈阿密或奥斯汀”，还说“他们就是不想交税，他们真心觉得自己挣的每一分钱都该归自己，因为他们比我们强”。Khanna 因为支持 Prop. 40，在自己选区里的科技亿万富翁中间成了不受欢迎的人。这是报道里唯一一处解释权被反向使用的地方：一个政客把亿万富翁的修辞翻译回它本来的意思——贪婪。

但别急着把这场仗算成好新闻。Prop. 40 就算过了，也是一次性的，教师工会反对它，理由正当：它给不了学校和社区可持续的、长久的钱。它不解决美国医疗体系本身的烂账，只是往窟窿里倒一桶水。它甚至不一定能过——最新民调显示 Prop. 40 和 41 大概率失败，42 可能勉强通过。

所以真正被争夺的从来不是那 5%。是“财富可以被征税”这个先例。亿万富翁们清楚这一点，所以他们宁可烧掉三个亿，也不肯让这个句子在法条里成立一次。他们不是怕交钱，他们是怕有一天，交钱这件事不再由他们自己定义。

而这场公投里最安静的一群人，是那些因为预算窟窿而看不起病的低收入加州人，包括很多孩子。他们没有一个叫 Building a Better California 的组织，也拿不出净资产的千分之一。他们只在别人的广告里出现，作为“医疗补助”四个字。

The most expensive thing on California's ballot isn't the advertising. It's the right to define what the vote is about.

Prop. 40 would levy a one-time 5 percent tax on net assets above $1 billion, with the money plugging holes in Medicaid and education. California's billionaire club responded with $333 million, funneled through a group called Building a Better California, and blanketed the state. That sum is about 0.1 percent of the combined net worth of its top five backers. Read that again: they spent one-thousandth of their wealth to buy the entire public conversation.

The number points the wrong way on purpose. $333 million isn't their cost. It's their change.

Then came the poison pills. Props. 41 and 42 are named like housekeeping amendments and function like a trapdoor. One audits future tax initiatives and voids any revenue excluded from a 1979 spending cap; the other bans retroactive taxes and any future tax on financial assets. If both pass and 41 gets more votes, 40 is voided. This isn't debate. This is cutting the opponent's option off the ballot. The art of manufacturing possibilities, practiced so openly it barely bothers to hide.

The real story is who speaks for them. Newsom opposes Prop. 40 because it will scare billionaires away and gut the tax base. Katie Porter posted three reasons to vote no; the third is that a one-time tax would cause long-term harm by driving hundreds of billions in potential revenue out of state. That claim traces to a Hoover Institution analysis that the economists who helped write Prop. 40 have rebutted in detail. The people making these predictions, the reporting says plainly, either have an agenda or are delusional.

Complicity doesn't require payment. Newsom is close to the Getty clan and is likely running for president, which means he needs billionaires in his corner. Porter just lost her bid for governor. Every sentence about the California economy has its return written somewhere else. This isn't corruption. It's something more ordinary: a person finding their optimal expression inside a structure, then dressing it as the public good.

Prop. 42 is the nastier package. It writes retirement and investment accounts into the list of things that can never be taxed, implying that ordinary people's life savings are at risk. Newsom recites the fear line: we all agree Californians deserve to have their retirement and life savings protected. Prop. 40 touches net assets above $1 billion. Their nest eggs are not in the blast radius. Fear is free, and it works.

On the other side, Jensen Huang calls paying whatever the state asks a privilege. Ro Khanna says the AI revolution is happening in the Bay Area, not Miami or Austin, and that the billionaires simply don't want to pay, that they believe they earned every penny because they are better than the rest of us. Khanna, for supporting Prop. 40, has become persona non grata among the tech billionaires in his own district. That is the one place in this story where the framing gets reversed: a politician translating billionaire rhetoric back into what it means.

Don't file this as good news yet. Even if Prop. 40 passes, it's one-and-done. The teachers union opposes it for a legitimate reason: it won't provide sustainable, long-lasting funding. It doesn't fix America's broken health care system; it pours a bucket into the hole. And it may not pass at all. The latest credible polls suggest 40 and 41 likely go down, while 42 may squeak by.

What's actually being fought over was never the 5 percent. It's the precedent that wealth can be taxed. The billionaires know this, which is why they'd rather burn $333 million than let that sentence exist in law even once. They don't fear paying. They fear the day paying stops being something they define.

The quietest people in this story are the low-income Californians, many of them children, who lose coverage when the budget hole isn't filled. They have no group called Building a Better California and no thousandth of a net worth to spend. They appear only inside someone else's ad, as four words: health care services.

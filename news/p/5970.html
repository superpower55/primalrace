她不是被一个男人掐住，是被两个国家的协议掐住Two Countries Had Their Hands Around Her Case

国际 直接层 · 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
英国警察把案子交给美军，交出去的是解释权
British police handed over the investigation. What they gave away was the right to explain.

Sarah Steele在自己的公寓里被一个美国飞行员掐住脖子，案子却不在英国审。这不是程序瑕疵，这是暴力三角里最完整的一套联动：直接暴力落在她身上，结构暴力由一份几十年前签的协议完成，文化暴力由那场审判的每一分钟完成。

协议写得很清楚：驻英美军涉嫌犯罪，英国警方有优先调查权。美军只有在两种情况下能拿走管辖权——犯罪发生在执勤期间，或受害者是美军人员或其财产。Wulfson是在休假时动的手，Steele是英国平民，两人在Tinder上认识。国防部官员自己都写了：“表面上看，Steele案不符合让访问部队获得管辖权的门槛。”那为什么还是交出去了？剑桥郡警方没有解释。报道里没有答案，只有一句“我们正在审查”，以及“我们在协助受害者”。

然后案子进了萨福克的Lakenheath空军基地。陪审团全是Wulfson的男性空军同僚，律师全是美国人，Steele在庭上被对方律师做了侵入性的盘问，她自己的说法是“有辱人格、令人痛苦”。他因掐住亲密伴侣被判有罪，性侵指控不成立，被开除军籍，在基地监狱关六个月。六个月。一个男人掐住一个女人的脖子，最后是关在自己单位的牢房里，由自己单位的同僚决定关多久。

这里动的不是一层暴力。直接层是那双手，结构层是那份协议和剑桥郡警方的移交决定，文化层是那场审判——一个全男性的同僚陪审团，天然带着“我们的人”的立场，这不是偏见，这是制度设计。而这三层能叠在一起，靠的是元层：谁有资格解释“这算什么事、该由谁来判”。英国警方交出去的不是一份卷宗，是对这件事的解释权。

国防部官员在内部邮件里说“绝对应该由英国法庭审”，大曼彻斯特的一位警官说剑桥郡“在我看来是错误地”移交了。这些话是泄露出来的，不是公开说的。公开说的是“深感不安”“非常关切”，然后成立一个高级别调查组。调查组在查什么？查“警方为什么把严重犯罪的调查移交给美军”。可答案就在那份协议里，协议自己写着优先权归英国。问题从来不是协议不清楚，是没人执行它。

报道里还有另外几个名字。Tyrion Davis被控在萨福克强奸两名女性，一项性侵成立，另外十项性侵和性接触指控不成立。Hannes Marschalek被控向一个16岁女孩和四名年轻女性暴露身体，在军事法庭上认了猥亵行为，然后在美国军事上诉法院以技术理由推翻了定罪，现在这个裁决正在被挑战。三个案子，同一个模式：休假的美军、英国的受害者、与军队毫无关系的平民、英国的警察主动把调查交出去。

英国有超过1.2万名美军人员，分布在至少15个基地和设施里，Lakenheath和Mildenhall是最大的两个，东盎格利亚住着大量美军。这不是一两个警察的失误，这是一条稳定的通道。报道写得很直白：美军“希望在与英国的协议边界内尽可能多地起诉自己的人员”，他们经常问英国警方能不能接手，而警方会答应——即使优先调查权本来就在英国这边。

所以那个调查组真正该问的不是“剑桥郡为什么这么做”，而是“为什么答应了这么多次都没人拦”。协议是白纸黑字，优先权是英国的，受害者是英国的，案子却一次次落到大西洋另一边一个她们不熟悉的法庭里，让她们在陌生程序里重新证明自己受过伤。

Steele在庭上作证的时候，坐在一间美国空军基地的房间里，对面是一群Wulfson的同事。她被掐住脖子的那间公寓在剑桥，离那个基地几十英里。这几十英里，就是英国警方亲手让出去的那段距离。

六个月之后Wulfson会出来，协议还在，通道还在，下一个Steele会从同一个入口进去。调查组如果只交出一份“加强清晰度”的报告，那它本身就是这条通道的一部分。

Sarah Steele was strangled in her own flat by an American fighter pilot, and the case was not tried in an English court. This is not a procedural hiccup. It is the violence triangle running at full capacity: direct violence on her body, structural violence executed through a decades-old agreement, cultural violence performed minute by minute in the courtroom.

The agreement is not ambiguous. UK police have the primary right to investigate alleged crimes by Americans stationed in the country. The US military can take jurisdiction only in a narrow set of circumstances: the offence happened on duty, or it was committed against another member of the armed forces or their property. Wulfson was off duty. Steele is a British civilian. They met on Tinder. MoD officials wrote it themselves: on the surface, the Steele case does not meet the bar for a visiting force to be awarded jurisdiction. So why was it handed over? Cambridgeshire police do not say. The report gives no answer, only a review, and a line about remaining engaged with the victim.

Then the case went to Lakenheath. An all-male jury of Wulfson's fellow air force officers. All the lawyers American. Steele was subjected to invasive and aggressive questioning by his lawyer; her word for the experience was degrading and distressing. He was convicted of strangling an intimate partner, acquitted of sexual assault, dismissed from the air force, and confined to a military jail on the base for six months. Six months. A man strangled a woman, and his own colleagues decided how long he would sit in his own unit's cell.

Three layers moved at once. The direct layer was his hands. The structural layer was the agreement and the police decision to invoke it. The cultural layer was the trial itself, a jury of his peers in the most literal and most corrupt sense of the word. What let all three stack together is the meta layer: who gets to explain what this event was and who gets to judge it. Cambridgeshire police did not hand over a case file. They handed over the right to explain.

Inside the MoD, officials wrote that this should absolutely have been for the UK courts. A Greater Manchester officer wrote that Cambridgeshire had, in his view, wrongly handed the investigation over. Those words leaked. What was said in public was deeply distressing, very concerning, and then a high-level inquiry. The inquiry is examining how and why police forces have been transferring serious crimes to US military police. But the answer is in the agreement, which already assigns primary responsibility to the UK. The problem was never that the rules were unclear. The problem is that nobody enforced them.

The report lists other names. Tyrion Davis was allegedly raped two women in Suffolk; convicted of one count of sexual assault, cleared of ten further counts. Hannes Marschalek allegedly exposed himself to a 16-year-old girl and four young women; he negotiated a plea bargain, admitted indecent conduct, then won an appeal in a US military court that quashed the conviction on technical grounds. Three cases, one pattern: off-duty Americans, British victims, civilians with no connection to the armed forces, British police volunteering the investigation.

There are more than 12,000 US military personnel in the UK, at least 15 bases and facilities, with large numbers living in East Anglia. This is not one force's mistake. It is a corridor. The report states it plainly: the US military aims to prosecute as many of its personnel as possible within the boundaries of its agreement, and it often asks UK police to take over prosecution. The police agree, even when primary responsibility is theirs.

The inquiry should not be asking why Cambridgeshire did this. It should be asking why nobody stopped it the first ten times. The agreement is in writing. The priority is British. The victims are British. And the cases keep landing in a courtroom on the other side of the Atlantic, where a woman has to prove again, in an unfamiliar system, that she was hurt.

Steele testified in a room on a US airbase, facing a group of Wulfson's colleagues. The flat where she was strangled is in Cambridge, a few dozen miles from that base. Those few dozen miles are the distance British police handed away.

In six months Wulfson walks out. The agreement stays. The corridor stays. The next Steele enters through the same door. If the inquiry produces only a report about greater clarity, it is part of the corridor.

谁在给舞台定尺寸Who Gets to Size the Stage

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要达拉斯牛仔啦啦队上月起诉My Chemical Romance，指其在好莱坞碗演唱会上让伴舞穿仿制啦啦队制服侵犯商标权，并申请禁令。乐队经理在10月29日的法庭声明中称，这套「终章」演出原本就只安排在2026年10月21、23、24日三场，停用制服与诉讼无关。11月2日双方联合申请撤诉。
The newsThe Dallas Cowboys Cheerleaders sued My Chemical Romance last month, claiming look-alike uniforms worn by backup dancers at the Hollywood Bowl shows infringed its trademark, and sought a restraining order. In an Oct. 29 court declaration, the band's manager said the three-night "Finale" was always planned for Oct. 21, 23 and 24, 2026, and the suit played no role in stopping. The case was dismissed Nov. 2.
商标法被请来给一场戏改台词，这才是真侵权。
Trademark law was invited to line-edit a play. That's the real infringement.

一件制服能不能出现在舞台上，最后不是由舞台决定，而是由持有商标的人决定。Pro Silver Star 起诉的理由是：观众和社交媒体用户以为台上站的是真的牛仔啦啦队。也就是说，它要保护的不是布料和配色，是「谁有资格被认成我们」这件事。

这是表达武器化的标准操作。商标在这里不是防仿冒，是认知入口的产权证：谁能出现在公众视野里、以什么形象出现、被谁看见，都要经过持有者许可。啦啦队制服在美国的符号价值，本来就建立在一套高度规训的身体表达上——年轻、整齐、被观看、被定价。乐队把这套制服放到一个反乌托邦舞台剧里，让穿它的人端着步枪打假仗，等于把那个符号从橱窗里拽出来重新使用。持有者受不了的正是这个：符号一旦能被别人拿去讲别的故事，定价权就松了。

乐队的应对也很有意思。经理的宣誓声明说，三场终章早在一年前就定好了，10月30、31日两场本来就不是终章，不会有啦啦队角色和服装。这话可能是真的。但它同时是一份完美的法律自保文件：我们没有让步，我们本来就打算停。真话和最优解表达在这里重合了，所以它格外好用。Spiro 那句「Sure they were」「They waved the white flag」是共谋场域里最常见的一手——把对方的程序性陈述改写成投降叙事，谁先定义「谁赢了」，谁就赢了。

Live Nation 的答辩是全场唯一把话说清楚的：「Plaintiff asks this court to use trademark law to line edit theatrical staging.」用商标法给舞台剧改台词。这句说对了。但它说对的方式也很方便：一家演唱会巨头把自己摆成第一修正案的捍卫者，而它同时是这场戏的 promoter，票房和它分成。它捍卫的不是艺术，是它自己不被打官司的权利。

所以这案子的结局不是谁赢了，是没有人需要付代价。乐队三场演完，啦啦队拿到了「我们赢了」的通稿，Live Nation 撤了诉，法院没有对 First Amendment 和商标的边界说一句话。真正被留在原地的问题是：一套被商业规训出来的身体符号，它的持有者能不能永远否决别人怎么使用它。这次否决被绕开了，没有被回答。

Whether a uniform can appear on a stage is decided not by the stage but by whoever holds the trademark. Pro Silver Star's claim was that fans and social media users believed the real Cowboys Cheerleaders were on stage. What it moved to protect wasn't fabric and color. It was the right to decide who gets recognized as us.

This is the standard weaponization of expression. Trademark here isn't anti-counterfeiting; it's a deed to a cognitive entry point — who may appear in public view, in what form, seen by whom, all subject to the holder's permission. The cheerleader uniform's symbolic value in America was built on a tightly disciplined body: young, uniform, watched, priced. The band dragged that symbol out of the display case and put it in a dystopian rock opera, rifles in hand, in a fake firefight. What the holder couldn't tolerate is exactly this: once someone else can tell a different story with your symbol, your pricing power loosens.

The band's response is telling too. The manager's sworn declaration says the three-night Finale was set a year in advance, that the Oct. 30 and 31 shows were never the Finale and would have had no cheerleader characters or costumes anyway. That may be true. It is also a flawless legal self-defense: we didn't concede, we always planned to stop. Truth and optimal expression coincided here, which is exactly why it works so well. Spiro's 「Sure they were」 and 「They waved the white flag」 is the oldest move in the co-conspirator's playbook — rewrite the other side's procedural statement as surrender. Whoever defines who won, wins.

Live Nation's brief is the only place anyone says it plainly: 「Plaintiff asks this court to use trademark law to line edit theatrical staging.」 That is correct. It is also convenient: a concert giant casting itself as the defender of the First Amendment while serving as promoter of the very show, sharing the take. It isn't defending art. It's defending its own right not to be sued.

So the ending isn't a win for anyone; it's that no one had to pay. The band played three nights, the cheerleaders got their 「we won big」 press, Live Nation dropped the case, and the court said nothing about where the First Amendment ends and trademark begins. The question left standing is whether the holder of a commercially disciplined body symbol can permanently veto how others use it. This time the veto was sidestepped, not answered.

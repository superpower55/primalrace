同性情侣跳支舞，就算进步了A Same-Sex Pairing Is Not a Power Shift

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 Deadline ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
representation 换的是台上配对，不是谁在定价
Representation changes who's on stage, not who sets the price.

一个男舞者想要一个男舞伴，这在他的行业里已经算新闻了。Ezra Sosa 对 OUT 说他向选角导演"put in my word"，说这是他入行以来的梦想。梦想的内容是：节目组允许他和另一个男人跳一支舞。

《与星共舞》2005 年开播，2021 年才出现第一对同性别搭档，JoJo Siwa 和 Jenna Johnson。2022 年是 Shangela 和 Gleb Savchenko，本季是 Sarah Jane Nader 和 Hailey Bills，第一周就被淘汰。二十年，三对半，其中一对撑不过一周。这个频率说明的不是节目在进步，是节目在按配额发放可能性。

Sosa 说得没错，representation 确实重要。但 representation 改变的是谁被允许出现在台上，不改变这档节目是什么。它仍然是 ABC 的一档比赛，靠观众投票、靠赞助、靠收视率活着；它卖的是配对、是故事线、是评委打出的分数。同性配对进入这个机器，是被这台机器吸收，不是这台机器被改写。Sosa 的梦想实现了，代价是他的梦想从此是节目的一集内容。

这里最值得看的不是性别，是定价权。谁决定哪一季可以有同性配对、配对能撑几周、镜头给多少秒，谁就在定义"进步"的节奏。Sosa 能做的只是"put in my word"，然后"we'll see what happens overtime"。他自己把这句话说得很清楚：请求已经递上去了，剩下的不归他管。一个公开出柜的职业舞者，在他自己的行业里，能做的仍然是申请。

所以这条新闻的暴力不在 direct 层，没有人受伤；也不完全在 cultural 层，因为台上确实多了一对。它在结构层：一个由资本和收视率分配可见性的系统，把每一次让步包装成里程碑，然后继续持有决定下一次让步何时到来的权力。三对配对是三次让步，不是三次换手。

真正的问题不是 Sosa 什么时候能拿到男舞伴。是他拿到之后，这档节目会不会还是同一档节目：同样的评委、同样的投票机制、同样的把身体和关系做成卖点的生产方式。如果答案是不会变，那这场胜利的准确名字是：他被允许在原有的定价体系里，多扮演一种角色。

A male dancer wanting a male partner is news in his own industry. Ezra Sosa told OUT he'd "put in my word" with the casting director, that a same-sex partner has been his dream since he joined the show. The dream is this: the show permits him to dance with another man.

Dancing with the Stars premiered in 2005. The first same-sex pairing arrived in 2021, JoJo Siwa and Jenna Johnson. Then Shangela and Gleb Savchenko in 2022. This season, Sarah Jane Nader and Hailey Bills, eliminated in Week 1. Twenty years, three pairings and a half, one of them gone inside a week. That frequency doesn't describe a show进步ing. It describes a show rationing possibility.

Sosa is right that representation matters. But representation changes who is allowed on stage, not what the stage is. It's still an ABC competition living on viewer votes, sponsorships, ratings. It sells pairings, storylines, judges' scores. A same-sex couple entering this machine gets absorbed by it; the machine doesn't get rewritten. Sosa's dream comes true, and the price of that is his dream becoming an episode.

The interesting axis here isn't gender. It's pricing power. Whoever decides which season gets a same-sex pairing, how many weeks it survives, how many seconds of camera time it gets, is defining the tempo of "progress." All Sosa can do is put in his word and wait: "We'll see what happens overtime." An openly gay professional dancer, in his own industry, is still applying.

The violence here isn't direct—nobody is hurt. It isn't purely cultural either, because a couple did appear on stage. It's structural: a system that allocates visibility by capital and ratings packages each concession as a milestone while keeping the power to decide when the next one arrives. Three pairings are three concessions, not three transfers of power.

The question isn't when Sosa gets his male partner. It's whether, once he has one, the show is still the same show: same judges, same voting, same production that turns bodies and relationships into product. If the answer is no change, then the accurate name for this victory is that he's been allowed to play one more role inside the existing pricing system.

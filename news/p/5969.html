你的币没有国家兜底Your Coins Have No Treasury Behind Them

科技 结构层 CNBC ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
承诺不是法律，储备不是保险
A pledge is not a law; a reserve is not insurance.

币安被黑了，损失估到六十三亿美元，冻结了约三十亿。联合 CEO 邓伟政在 X 上说："我们承诺用公司资金覆盖所有被盗损失。你的资产是安全的。"这句话里没有一个字有法律效力。

报道里圣地亚哥大学的网络安全专家 Nikolas Behar 说得干净：这只是承诺，直到真的赔付为止。Onramp Bitcoin 的 Michael Tanguma 更直接——交易所通常会赔，但只是因为交易所决定赔、并且有钱赔，"法律上没有任何东西要求它这么做"。塔夫茨的 Josephine Wolff 说，加密资产"根本不存在"和 FDIC 保险对等的法律保护。

美国人的银行存款有联邦政府背书，二十五万美元以内由 FDIC 兜住，那是大萧条烧出来的制度。加密投资者没有这个东西，他们有的是 SAFU——币安的用户安全资产基金，估算十亿到十二亿美元。就算把冻结的钱不算进去，这笔储备也只是这次损失的三分之一左右。三分之一。这就是"你的资产是安全的"背后的算术。

有人会说币安后来声明另拨了六十三点四亿美元公司资金、并持有五十亿以上对应代币。也许吧。但一家公司愿意掏钱和它有义务掏钱，是两件事。前者叫善意，后者叫权利；前者随时可以收回，后者才需要法院。整个加密行业把前者包装成后者，用的词是"保险""保护基金""安全资产基金"——命名本身就是一次挪用：把国家信用才有的东西，借一个名字挂在自己账上。

这个结构最狠的地方在于风险被重新分配给了个人。交易所被攻破，你的账户被盗，Tanguma 说得很清楚：交易所一般把这种损失当成客户自己的问题，而加密交易一旦确认就是终局，没有银行可打，没有扣款可争议。你自己保管私钥呢？风险从"公司的员工、审批系统、财务状况和赔付意愿"换成"你"——丢了的设备、被错的人捡到的备份、从不正规卖家手里买来的被动过手脚的机器、在压力下批准的一笔转账、以及你去世之后家人根本拿不到那笔钱。Tanguma 的原话是：把储蓄从交易所挪走，是用一种风险换另一种风险，不是消除风险。

报道里没有一个普通人因为这次攻击破产，没有名字，没有故事。这不是报道的疏漏，这是这件事的形状：六十三亿美元的黑客攻击可以写成一篇理财指南，因为受损的是抽象的"用户"，而兜底的是公司的善意和它自己的资产负债表。TRM Labs 和 Chainalysis 把攻击链到朝鲜，但美国政府没有公开确认，币安也没有。真正被确认的只有一件事：这六十三亿里，没有一分钱背后站着一个政府。

报道最后给了 Reck 的四个问题，最后一个最要紧：我有没有可强制执行的获赔权利。答案是，在加密世界里，通常没有。而"保险"这个词，只是这场对话的起点，不是你的钱被保护了的证明。存款保险是把风险从个人身上拿走，加密是把风险从公司身上拿走、放回个人身上，再给它起一个听起来像保险的名字。

还有一层没人问：如果这次被黑的是某个小交易所，SAFU 这样的储备根本不存在，那六十三亿会由谁来付？答案是不会有人付，报道也就不会写。能上 CNBC 的加密灾难，前提是它有足够的钱把灾难说成一次"事件"。

Binance got hacked. Estimated impact: $6.3 billion. About $3 billion has been frozen so far. Co-CEO Richard Teng posted on X: "We are committed to covering all hacked losses through our company funds. Your assets are safe." Not one word in that sentence has legal force.

Nikolas Behar of the University of San Diego puts it cleanly in the report: it's a pledge until there's an actual payout. Michael Tanguma of Onramp Bitcoin is blunter — exchanges usually repay, but only because the exchange decides to pay and has the money to do so. "Nothing in the law requires it to," he says. Josephine Wolff at Tufts says there "really aren't any legal protections for crypto assets equivalent to FDIC insurance."

American bank deposits sit on the full faith of the federal government, up to $250,000 through the FDIC, a thing born out of the ashes of the Great Depression. Crypto investors have SAFU instead — Binance's Secure Asset Fund for Users, estimated at roughly $1 billion to $1.2 billion. Excluding the frozen funds, that is about one-third of the loss. One-third. That is the arithmetic behind "your assets are safe."

Binance later said it had set aside more than $6.34 billion in company funds and held over $5 billion in corresponding token assets. Fine. But a company willing to pay and a company obligated to pay are two different things. The first is goodwill, the second is a right. Goodwill can be withdrawn; a right has to go through a court. The whole industry dresses the first up as the second, and the words it uses are "insurance," "protection fund," "secure asset fund." The naming is itself the theft: borrowing the vocabulary of state credit and hanging it on a private balance sheet.

The cruelest part of the structure is where the risk lands. An exchange gets breached, your account gets drained, and Tanguma is explicit: exchanges generally treat those losses as the customer's problem, and because crypto transactions are final once confirmed, there is no bank to call and no charge to dispute. Self-custody? Then the single weak point shifts from "the company — its employees, its approval systems, its finances, and its willingness to repay you" to you: a lost device, a backup found by the wrong person, a tampered machine bought from an unofficial seller, a transaction approved under pressure, a family unable to reach the funds after a death. Moving savings off an exchange, Tanguma says, trades one kind of risk for another. It does not remove risk.

Not one ordinary person goes bankrupt by name in this report. No names, no stories. That is not an oversight; it is the shape of the thing. A $6.3 billion hack can be written up as a personal-finance guide because the damaged party is an abstract "user," and the backstop is corporate goodwill plus a balance sheet. TRM Labs and Chainalysis link the attack to North Korea; neither the U.S. government nor Binance has publicly confirmed it. The only confirmed fact is that not one dollar of that $6.3 billion stands behind a government.

Reck's four questions close the piece, and the last one matters most: Do I have an enforceable right to be repaid? In crypto, usually not. And "insured," he says, is where the conversation starts, not proof your money is protected. Deposit insurance takes risk off the individual. Crypto takes risk off the company, hands it back to the individual, and gives it a name that sounds like insurance.

One question nobody asks: if the exchange that got hit had been a small one with no SAFU-sized reserve, who would pay the $6.3 billion? Nobody, and the story would not be written. A crypto disaster gets on CNBC only when it has enough money to be narrated as an "incident."

读书一百本，读不出谁在付账One Hundred Novels, Not One Question of Who Pays

文娱 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
她读懂了小说的技法，没读懂小说替谁说话
She learned the craft of the novel. She never learned whom the novel was built to serve.

菲利帕·格里高利说，读一百本十八世纪最流行的小说，是“完美的作家学徒期”。她学到的是结构、人物、节奏。我信。她没提到的是，那一百本小说本身就在教一件事：谁有资格当主角，谁的身体用来被安排。

《克拉丽莎》讲的是一个女孩被囚禁、被下药、被强奸，然后死去，读者被要求为她的贞洁流泪。《摩尔·弗兰德斯》讲一个女人靠身体和算计活下来，最后被流放。这两本书并排出现在她的清单里，被当作“技法”的样本。技法当然有。但技法背后是一整套关于女性身体如何被书写、被定价、被消费的惯例，学徒期学的不只是怎么写，还有写什么才叫“好”。

她说EP Thompson的《英国工人阶级的形成》让她明白“书写历史的重要性”，让她学会“找到并写出被隐藏的历史”。这句话在她嘴里是真诚的，问题在于她后来写的历史小说——都铎王朝、博林家的女孩——写的是宫廷里的女人，被隐藏的历史写出来了，但写出来的方式是让她们在权力结构里周旋、爱、牺牲、被砍头。被隐藏的历史被找到，然后被重新包装成可消费的叙事。这不是她的错，这是历史小说这个类型的结构性条件。

“完美的作家学徒期”这个说法本身值得停一下。学徒期意味着有一门手艺要被传承，有一套标准要被内化。十八世纪小说的标准是什么？是让读者认同某个视角，是让某些人的痛苦成为审美对象，是让叙事本身变成一种筛选机制——谁的故事值得讲，谁的身体值得看，谁的死值得哭。一百本读完，这套标准就长在你身上了，你以为是技法，其实是规训。

她提到莫莉·基恩“每一本小说都高度意识到支撑那个社会的不公”。这句话她说得很准。但她没说的是，她自己的小说也高度意识到不公，然后让不公变成情节。读者买的是情节，不是不公。

这条新闻里没有人在承受代价，除了一个隐含的事实：一百本十八世纪小说里那些被书写、被消费、被安排命运的女性身体，她们的代价被转化成了文学传统，转化成了“完美的学徒期”，转化成了今天书店里一本叫《Royal Witch》的新书。格里高利是这套传统的优秀继承者。继承者不需要为传统付账，传统已经替她把账付过了。

Philippa Gregory says reading more than 100 of the 18th century's most popular novels was "the perfect author's apprenticeship." She learned structure, characterisation, tempo. I believe her. What she doesn't mention is that those 100 novels were themselves teaching one thing: who gets to be the subject, and whose body is there to be arranged.

Clarissa is about a girl imprisoned, drugged, raped, and left to die, while the reader is asked to weep for her virtue. Moll Flanders is about a woman who survives on her body and her calculations, and is transported in the end. Both sit on her list, side by side, as samples of "craft." The craft is real. But behind the craft is an entire set of conventions about how a woman's body gets written, priced, and consumed. An apprenticeship teaches not only how to write but what counts as good writing.

She says EP Thompson's The Making of the English Working Class showed her "the real importance of writing history" and taught her that you can "find and write a hidden history." She means it. The problem is what she did with it afterward — Tudor courts, the Boleyn girls — women inside palaces, manoeuvring, loving, sacrificing, losing their heads. The hidden history gets found, then repackaged as a consumable narrative. That isn't her personal failure; it's the structural condition of the historical novel as a genre.

"The perfect author's apprenticeship" deserves a pause. An apprenticeship means a craft is handed down and a standard is internalised. What was the 18th-century novel's standard? Getting the reader to identify with one perspective, making certain people's suffering an aesthetic object, turning narrative itself into a filtering mechanism — whose story is worth telling, whose body is worth looking at, whose death is worth crying over. Read 100 of them and the standard grows into you. You think it's technique. It's discipline.

She notes that Molly Keane's novels are "highly aware of the injustice that underpins that society." That's precisely right. What she leaves out is that her own novels are also highly aware of injustice — and then turn it into plot. Readers buy the plot, not the injustice.

No one in this article is paying a price, except for one implicit fact: the bodies of the women written, consumed, and arranged across those 100 novels had their costs converted into a literary tradition, into "the perfect apprenticeship," into a new book called Royal Witch on a shelf today. Gregory is an excellent heir to that tradition. An heir doesn't have to settle the bill. The tradition already settled it for her.

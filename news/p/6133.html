大都会歌剧院的第一部女性委约歌剧The Met's First Opera Commissioned From a Woman

文娱 元暴力 · 文化层 Vulture ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
历史性突破被写成了脚注，这才是元暴力。
A historic first, filed as a parenthetical. That is meta-violence.

这篇评论最诚实的地方，是它自己都没打算把重点放在那件事上。整篇看下来，真正值得说的不是 Mazzoli 的音乐好不好，而是括号里那句：大都会歌剧院此前从未首演过一部由女性委约的歌剧。这句话被放在文章倒数第三段，用括号括起来，前面还加了个“gobsmackingly”。一个机构一百多年来第一次把首演交给一位女性作曲家，这件事在评论里只值一个插入语。

这就是元暴力的日常运作方式。不是有人跳出来说女人写不了歌剧，而是当它终于发生的时候，整个评价体系依然按原来的标准运转：结构是否连贯，情感是否到位，第二幕的咏叹调有没有打动人。这些问题当然可以问，但问的方式决定了谁在定义什么叫“成立”。评论说这些唱段“跳来跳去”，“角色活在各自的音乐气泡里”，“删掉一段、加一段、换个顺序，体验不会有实质变化”。这是拿线性戏剧的尺子去量一部歌剧，而这部歌剧恰恰是从一部由几十个声音拼贴而成的小说改来的。尺子换了，结论就换了。

更有意思的是评论自己承认了这一点。它说这种跳跃“是小说的魅力所在”，但到了舞台上就制造了“不可逾越的距离”。小说可以跳跃，歌剧不行。谁规定的？评论没有说，因为不需要说，这就是默认设置。Mazzoli 的音乐被形容成“明亮的、虹彩般的”、“美国腔调十足”、“风格折衷”，这些词都是褒义，但组合在一起，潜台词是：好听，但不够深刻。而“深刻”的标准是什么，评论用 Lincoln 第二幕的咏叹调给出了答案：舞台暗下来，主角坐着唱出内心独白，音乐独自承担一切，“要么打动你，要么不”。它没有打动评论者。这个判断可能是对的，但判断的依据是一种非常特定的歌剧美学，而这种美学长期由男性作品定义。

导演 Blain-Cruz 的处理被评论读成“不信任作曲家或观众”，理由是舞台上塞满了合唱、舞者、魔鬼、树、马、空中飞人和烟花。评论说，在这里恰恰相反，她信任 Mazzoli 的音乐能穿透一切。这个转折很慷慨，但也很说明问题：一个女性导演用满台的视觉元素去支撑一部女性作曲家的作品，第一反应是“她是不是不信任音乐”。男性导演这么做的时候，通常叫“大胆的舞台调度”。

评论里还有一个细节值得停一下。Anthony Roth Costanzo 用假声唱一个少女，裙子大到“能装下一大家子人”。这个选角被当成一个有趣的花招写过去，没有人问为什么一个成年男性用假声演少女会被称为“virtuosically”。这跟那根需要专人操控的六英尺阳具放在同一篇评论里，一个被当作笑点，一个被当作奇观，两者都没有被追问。

这不是说这篇评论充满恶意。它恰恰是善意的，它赞赏了 Mazzoli 的才华，称赞了演员，还特意点出了那个历史性的第一次。问题在于，善意不等于公正。当“第一次”被写进括号，当结构上的不连贯被归咎于作曲家而不是评价框架，当一位女性导演的舞台调度被读成对音乐的不信任，这些微小的动作加在一起，就是在说：你可以进来，但规则不变。

Mazzoli 的歌剧在十一月十四日之前还在演。评论说它“令人毛骨悚然，但也冷冰冰”。冷冰冰的到底是谁，评论没有问。

The most honest thing about this review is that it doesn't even pretend to center the one fact that matters. Buried in the third-to-last paragraph, in parentheses, prefaced by "gobsmackingly": the Met has never before staged the premiere of an opera it commissioned from a woman. A hundred-plus years of institutional history, and the first time the house hands a premiere to a female composer, it's an interjection.

This is how meta-violence operates day to day. Not someone declaring that women can't write opera, but the evaluation machinery humming along on its original settings the moment it finally happens. Is the structure coherent? Does the emotion land? Does the Act Two aria move you? Fair questions, but how you ask them decides who gets to define what counts as "working." The review says the numbers "jump around," that characters "inhabit their own musical bubbles," that you could cut one, add more, shuffle them, and "wouldn't materially change the experience." That's a linear-drama ruler applied to an opera adapted from a novel built out of dozens of colliding voices. Change the ruler, change the verdict.

The review almost admits this. It says the jumpiness is "part of the novel's appeal" but onstage creates "an insurmountable distance." Novels can jump; operas can't. Who decided? The review doesn't say, because it doesn't have to. That's the default setting. Mazzoli's music gets called "brightly colored," "iridescent," "unmistakably American," "stylistically eclectic"—all compliments, but stacked together the subtext is: pleasant, not profound. And "profound" gets defined by the Lincoln aria: stage dark, title character seated, singing private thoughts, music unaided, "either moves you or doesn't." It didn't. That judgment may be right, but it rests on a very specific operatic aesthetic, one long defined by male works.

Director Lileana Blain-Cruz's staging gets read as "a director who doesn't trust either the composer or the audience," because the stage is packed with choristers, dancers, demons, trees, a horse, aerial tricks, fireworks. The review then generously reverses itself: here it's the opposite, she trusts Mazzoli's music to blaze through. A lovely concession, and telling. A woman director fills the stage to support a woman composer's work, and the first instinct is to ask whether she trusts the music. When a man does it, it's called bold staging.

One more detail worth pausing on. Anthony Roth Costanzo sings an adolescent girl in falsetto, in a skirt so vast it "could shelter an extended family." It's written up as a charming trick. Nobody asks why a grown man in falsetto playing a girl gets called "virtuosic." That sits in the same review as the six-foot phallus steered by a puppeteer—one a punchline, one a spectacle, neither interrogated.

None of this is malice. The review is friendly. It praises Mazzoli's gifts, credits the performers, even flags the historic first. But friendly isn't just. When the first is parenthetical, when structural disjointedness gets charged to the composer rather than the frame, when a woman director's staging reads as distrust of the music—those small moves add up to: you may enter, but the rules stay.

Mazzoli's opera runs through November 14. The review calls it "chilling but also chilly." Chilly for whom, the review doesn't ask.

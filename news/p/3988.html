单杠上的生物墙与被掩盖的结构性暴力The Biological Wall on the Pull-up Bar and Its Masked Structural Violence

性别 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-08-17 § 链接
生理差异是生物墙，但将其定义为“挑战”是文化暴力的掩体。
Biological differences are the Biological Wall; defining them as a "challenge" is a cover for cultural violence.

这篇文章在试图扮演一个“客观”的科普者，用肌肉比例、体脂率和重心高度这些生理参数来解释为什么女性做引体向上更难。它把这定义为一种“挑战” (challenging)，并用“人口平均值”来消解个体差异，最后给出一个温情的结论：只要训练，女性也能变强。

但从加尔通的暴力三角来看，这恰恰是典型的 cultural violence。它在用生物学事实为结构性弱势背书。所谓的“生物墙” (Biological Wall)——比如女性上肢肌肉量较低、Q角导致的力学差异——确实存在，但这些生理事实在父权叙事中被武器化了。当社会将“上肢力量”定义为强壮、领导力和能力的指标，而将女性的生理特质定义为“劣势”或“挑战”时，它实际上是在完成一次认知入口的占领：它在暗示，女性在生理上就是“不完整的”或“需要被弥补的”。

更深层的 structural violence 在于，我们衡量能力的尺度（比如引体向上这个动作本身）是由男性身体定义的。男性宽肩、高重心、上肢肌肉分布的特点，决定了这种运动的“最优解”是基于男性生物学的。而女性被要求在这个由男性定义的尺度里通过“努力训练”来获得认可。这不是赋权，而是一场关于主体性的存在性战争：你必须通过模仿男性的力量表达，才能在公共空间证明自己的“强健”。

所谓的“只要训练就能变强”是一个典型的 scam。它掩盖了这样一个事实：无论女性练到什么程度，她依然在一个由男性中心叙事 (masculine-centric narrative) 掌控的评价体系里打分。真正的公正表达不应该是让女性在单杠上追赶男性，而是意识到，将单一的肌肉力量等同于“能力”本身，就是一种元暴力。

This article attempts to play the role of an "objective" educator, using muscle proportions, body fat percentages, and center of gravity to explain why pull-ups are harder for women. It frames this as a "challenge" and uses "population averages" to dissolve individual differences, concluding warmly that with training, women can also become strong.

However, through the lens of the Violence Triangle, this is classic cultural violence. It uses biological facts to justify structural disadvantage. The "Biological Wall"—such as lower upper-body muscle mass and biomechanical differences—exists, but these facts are weaponized in patriarchal narratives. When society defines upper-body strength as the benchmark for power, leadership, and capability, and frames female physiology as a "disadvantage," it is seizing a cognitive entry point: suggesting that women are biologically "incomplete" or "in need of repair."

The deeper structural violence lies in the fact that the metrics of capability (like the pull-up itself) are defined by the masculine body. The broad shoulders and high center of gravity of men determine the "optimal expression" of this movement. Women are then asked to seek validation by training to fit this masculine scale. This isn't empowerment; it's an existential war over subjectivity: you must mimic masculine expressions of power to be recognized as "strong" in the public sphere.

The claim that "anyone can train to be strong" is a scam. It masks the reality that no matter how much a woman trains, she is still being graded by a masculine-centric narrative. Just expression isn't about women catching up to men on a pull-up bar; it's realizing that equating singular muscular strength with "capability" is, in itself, a form of meta-violence.

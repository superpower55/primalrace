互联网腐烂，谁在收割The Internet Is Rotting. Who's Harvesting It?

科技 结构层 · 文化层 WIRED ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
平台把注意力榨成钱，腐烂是它的商业模式
Platforms monetize attention; decay is their business model.

WIRED 这篇请愿书写得很诚恳：互联网正在腐烂，让它死吧。但它把腐烂写成了所有人的自然衰老，而不是一门生意。

报道里最有价值的一句是韦斯特说的：注意力被导向空洞的认证，就为「非人的东西」腾出了位置。这句话再往前一步就是结论——不是互联网病了，是有人靠它病着赚钱。Meta 和 Google 被消费者集体诉讼逼到和解，隐私侵害、成瘾机制造成的精神健康损害，这是直接层的账；而它们至今还在运营、还在优化那套机制，是结构层在替它们兜底。诉讼赔钱是成本，成瘾是收入，成本小于收入，机制就不会改。

愤怒诱饵（rage bait）被牛津词典选为年度词汇，使用量一年翻三倍。报道说这代表「意识提高」。意识提高不是暴力减少，文化层最擅长的就是把伤害重新命名成一个热词，然后热词本身变成流量。生成式 AI 把这种恶意自动化，账号批量生产对立和极端内容，只为从敌意互动里抽成——这是第三章说的武器化：夺取认知入口，制造「什么是事实」，再按点击定价。

最该被点出来的是「死互联网理论」那段：一家网络安全公司得出，2025 年机器人代理的流量已经超过人类用户，增长八千个百分点。报道把这当成荒诞的注脚，其实这是整件事的骨架：当平台上大部分「声音」不是人，那么剩下的真人就被困在一个由机器搭台、由寡头撤掉审核闸门的环境里互相投喂敌意。谁在这里赢？报道自己说了，没有赢家，「除非你算上那几个不惜一切代价追逐相关性的人」。那几个人的名字，报道没写。

韦斯特的「堆肥」隐喻很温柔：腐烂可以被引导成肥料。但堆肥需要有人翻土、有人等。报道结尾作者自嘲是个失败的堆肥者，懒，也嫌脏——这句话比全文任何一句都诚实。让互联网死掉不需要动员，只需要所有人一起放手；可放手之后长出来的东西，不会自动是诗歌网站和零播放量的唱歌视频，更可能是下一个更高效的榨取机器，因为资本不会跟着平台一起腐烂，它会迁移。

所以这篇请愿书缺的不是悲观，是账本：腐烂的收益归谁，代价由谁付。报道写到父母担心孩子想做网红、陷进网络赌场、被极端运动吸走——这些是人在承受代价，但报道没有问，为什么这些入口被设计成最容易掉进去的形状，以及设计它们的人拿到了什么。

互联网会死，人会死，这话没错。但在那之前，先问一句：谁在靠它活着，靠谁的死活着。

WIRED's plea is earnest: the internet is rotting, let it die. But it frames the rot as a natural aging that happens to everyone, not as a business.

The most useful line in the piece is West's: attention steered toward hollow validation creates "the exact conditions for a space to be filled by something that isn't human." One step further and you have the conclusion—the internet isn't sick, someone is getting paid for it to be sick. Meta and Google were forced to settle massive consumer lawsuits over privacy violations and mental health harms from addictive mechanisms. That's the direct-layer bill. The fact that they still operate, still optimize the same mechanisms, is the structural layer covering for them. A settlement is a cost; addiction is revenue. As long as cost is smaller than revenue, nothing changes.

Rage bait was named word of the year, its usage tripling in twelve months. The piece calls this heightened awareness. Awareness is not a reduction in violence; the cultural layer's favorite move is to rename the harm as a buzzword, and then the buzzword itself becomes traffic. Generative AI automates the bad faith, accounts churn out polarization and extremism to skim profit off hostile engagement—this is weaponization in the plainest sense: seize the cognitive entry point, manufacture what counts as fact, then price it per click.

The part that should be underlined is the Dead Internet Theory passage: one cybersecurity firm concluded that by 2025 agent traffic had surpassed human users, surging 8,000 percent. The piece treats this as an absurd footnote. It's the skeleton. When most "voices" on a platform aren't people, the remaining humans are trapped in an environment where machines build the stage and oligarchs roll back the moderation gates, feeding each other hostility. Who wins here? The piece says it itself: no one, "unless you count the few without the sense to stop chasing relevance at any cost." Those few are never named.

West's compost metaphor is gentle: rot can be harnessed into fertilizer. But composting requires someone to turn the soil and wait. The author ends by admitting he's a failed composter—too gross, too lazy—and that line is more honest than anything else in the piece. Letting the internet die requires no mobilization, just everyone letting go at once. What grows afterward won't automatically be poetry journals and zero-view singing videos. More likely it's the next, more efficient extraction machine, because capital doesn't rot with the platform. It migrates.

So what this plea is missing isn't pessimism. It's a ledger: who collects the revenue from the rot, who pays the cost. The piece notes parents panicking about kids wanting to be influencers, sinking into online casinos, pulled into extremist movements—real people bearing real costs. But it never asks why those entry points are shaped to be the easiest to fall into, and what the people who shaped them got.

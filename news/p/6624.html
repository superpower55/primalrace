她十八岁那年，全组没人替她化妆At Eighteen, Nobody Bothered to Do Her Makeup

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 A.V. Club ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要朱丽叶·比诺什接受采访，回顾从影经历，谈及与戈达尔合作《万福玛丽亚》时学会不指望导演、与丹尼尔·戴-刘易斯合作《布拉格之恋》、凭《英国病人》获奥斯卡奖，以及在《哥斯拉》中仅拍摄三天。
The newsJuliette Binoche looked back on her career, discussing learning not to expect help from a director while working with Godard on Hail Mary, working with Daniel Day-Lewis on The Unbearable Lightness of Being, winning an Oscar for The English Patient, and her three days of shooting on Godzilla.
一个女演员的职业生涯，从学会自己划界限开始。
A actress's career begins with learning to draw her own lines.

比诺什在这篇访谈里讲了她第一部戏。十八岁，第一场戏的台词是一句骂人话，因为那个角色正在被强奸。她走进化妆间，工作人员说：你不需要化妆。她说她当时很受伤——他们不想照顾她。多年以后她替他们解释：大概他们是对的，一个十八岁的女演员，什么都不化就很好拍。

这就是定价。不是有人当面告诉她"你的价值在于被拍"，而是没有人觉得需要为她做点什么，因为她的功能是现成的。一个十八岁的身体放在镜头前，本身就是产品，产品不需要化妆师。她后来才明白自己受了伤，受伤的原因不是没上妆，是那一刻她意识到自己在这个工种里是一件被使用的材料，而不是一个被照料的人。

同一段回忆里还有另一件事：一个导演约她谈下一部戏的角色，最后试图吻她。她推开了。她的总结是，这行是个 pain in the ass，因为你必须很早就把界限划出来。两件事放在一起看，机制就清楚了——化妆间里的"你不需要"和导演房间里的"你可以"是同一套东西的两面：一边不给你资源，一边默认你的身体可以被取用。十八岁的女孩要在这种环境里同时学会两件事：争取被好好对待，和拒绝被随便对待。而这两件事本来都不该由她来学。

她说 consent 这个词的时候是在谈阿尔茨海默病，谈一对老夫妻里丈夫还能不能爱妻子、法律能不能替她说不。这个问题很难，我不打算在这里替它下结论。但值得指出的是，她谈 consent 谈了两次，一次是关于一个失智的女人的身体由谁决定，一次是关于一个十八岁的女演员的身体由谁决定。中间隔了四十年，问题没有变，只是换了个年纪。

访谈里最漂亮的一段是她讲什么叫和另一个演员的连接：不是粉丝心态，是"你看见眼睛里的灵魂，你想和那个灵魂在一起"，是 soul-to-soul。这话是真的。但这句话之所以能成立，前提是她在片场待了四十年，攒够了可以不配合的资本——她可以说不，可以挑导演，可以说"我从来没放弃"。她讲《英国病人》开拍前一周差点停摆，最后是韦恩斯坦接手才拍成；她讲《Damage》里她和杰瑞米·艾恩斯关系很糟，她在化妆间哭，整个拍摄期非常孤独。这些她都是事后才讲的。当年她大概什么都没说，因为说了也没用。

所以这篇访谈真正有价值的地方，不是那些关于艺术的漂亮话，而是那个十八岁的女孩在化妆间里受的伤，和她在导演房间里推开的那一下。四十年后她把它们讲出来了，讲得很轻，像在讲别人的事。轻是因为已经过去了，不是因为不重。

Binoche tells the story of her first film. She was eighteen. Her one line was an insult, because the character was being raped. She walked into hair and makeup and they said: you don't need anything. She says she felt very hurt—they didn't want to take care of her. Years later she explains it for them: probably they were right, a young eighteen-year-old actress is good to be shot with nothing.

That is pricing. Nobody told her her value lay in being photographed. Nobody thought she needed anything done for her, because her function was already there. An eighteen-year-old body in front of a camera is the product; the product doesn't need a makeup artist. She only understood later that she had been hurt—not by the missing makeup, but by the moment she realized she was material being used in this trade, not a person being cared for.

In the same passage there is a second thing: a director invited her to talk about a role in another film, and ended up trying to kiss her. She pushed him away. Her conclusion: this job is a pain in the ass, because you have to put limits very early on. Put the two together and the mechanism is clear—the "you don't need anything" of the makeup room and the "you're available" of the director's room are two faces of one arrangement: withhold the resources, assume the body. An eighteen-year-old girl has to learn two things at once in that environment, to demand being treated well and to refuse being treated casually. Neither should have been hers to learn.

She says the word consent while talking about Alzheimer's—whether a husband can still love a wife who can no longer consent, whether the law can say no for her. That question is hard and I won't settle it here. But note that she talks about consent twice: once about a demented woman's body and who decides for it, once about an eighteen-year-old actress's body and who decides for it. Forty years apart, same question, different age.

The loveliest passage is her description of what connection with another actor is: not fangirl admiration, but seeing the soul inside the eyes and wanting to be with that soul, soul-to-soul. That is real. But it can only be said by someone who has spent forty years accumulating the capital to not cooperate—who can say no, choose her directors, say she never gives up. She mentions that The English Patient nearly collapsed a week before shooting and was saved when Weinstein's Miramax took it on; that on Damage her relationship with Jeremy Irons was very hard, that she cried in her dressing room and felt very alone for the whole shoot. She says all this afterward. At the time she probably said nothing, because saying it would have changed nothing.

The value of this interview is not the beautiful talk about art. It is the wound in the makeup room and the push in the director's room, told forty years later, told lightly, like someone else's story. Light because it is over, not because it was small.

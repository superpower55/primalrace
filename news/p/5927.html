阴茎增大是性别肯定治疗Penis Enlargement Is Gender-Affirming Care

性别 文化层 · 结构层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
男性花一万五买一个更大的自己，账单寄给妻子。
Men pay $15,000 for a bigger self. The bill goes to their wives.

一个男人花一万五千美元往阴茎里打填充物，报道把这写成孤独、焦虑、男性脆弱。这些词都对，但都绕开了最要紧的一句：这就是 gender-affirming care。

性别肯定治疗的定义是，一个人对自己身体呈现与社会性别位置的表达感到错位，于是花钱、挨针、冒风险，把它改成自己认得出的样子。Moore 的客户做的正是这件事——他们买的不是几厘米，是"像个男人"这个表达。报道里那位父亲 Ruben 相信增大能让他更好地保护和供养家庭，这句话翻译过来就是：他认为自己现在不够格当一个男人。这不是性器官的问题，是表达的问题，是存在性战争里他觉得自己一直在输。

所以这门生意卖的是身份，不是医学。Moore 穿着手术服，不是医生，办公室开在达拉斯的 strip mall，一次收一万五到两万，公司 PhalloFill 已经开到 32 家分店，他希望这手术像肉毒杆菌一样普遍。报道写他房子大、车多，比他对客户讲的自尊心那套话诚实得多。一个不是医生的人，在灰色地带里给男人卖男子气概，还去会议上教真医生怎么赶上这波淘金热——这是标准的武器化：夺取"什么是够男人"的解释权，然后按次收费。

共谋在这里不分性别。Ruben 的五个孩子搬去和前任同住，为了给他凑手术费；妻子 Lana 几乎反对，但"没有采取太强硬的立场"。报道说几乎没有一个伴侣希望他们做这个。代价从来不由付钱的那个人独自承担，只是账单上的名字只有一个。

最刺眼的是 David 那一段。他年轻时做了失败的手术，去找 Moore 修复，而报道顺手揭出他和 Moore、Moore 的伴侣同睡一张床。一个在 OnlyFans 上演色情片的年轻男同性恋，被一个公开的同性恋商人修复阴茎，两个人都有把同性恋视为可憎的虔诚父母。这条线本来可以讲透一件事：男性中心叙事把阴茎的大小设成男人价值的度量衡，然后让所有男人——包括根本不在那个叙事里受益的男人——排队去够那根尺子。但报道把这层意思收进了"最奇怪的转折"和 throuple 的花边里，笑一笑就过去了。

Lombroso 说这电影手法很轻，因为"这些东西对聪明的观众来说本来就很好笑"。好笑是真的，代价也是真的：一个父亲把家拆了去买一个更大的自己，一个年轻人在另一张手术台上赌上永久损伤。男性中心叙事对男人的暴力，就是让他们相信自己的存在值多少钱，取决于一个可以被注射、被定价、被转卖的尺寸。

报道没有写的是 Lana 后来怎样，也没有写那五个孩子搬去了哪里。

A man pays $15,000 to have filler injected into his penis, and the report calls it loneliness, anxiety, male fragility. All true, and all of it dodges the sentence that matters: this is gender-affirming care.

Gender-affirming care means a person feels misaligned with how their body reads and where it sits in the social order, so they pay, get pierced, take the risk, and change it into something they recognize. That is exactly what Moore's customers are doing. They are not buying centimetres. They are buying the expression "I am a man." Ruben, the father of five, believes the procedure will help him protect and provide for his family. Translated: he believes he does not currently qualify as a man. This was never about a body part. It is about expression, and about a man who thinks he has been losing the existential war.

So the business sells identity, not medicine. Moore wears scrubs, is not a doctor, operates out of a Dallas strip mall, charges $15,000 to $20,000 a pop, has expanded PhalloFill to 32 locations, and hopes to make the procedure as common as Botox. The report notes his big house and car collection; that is more honest about his motives than anything he tells customers about self-esteem. A non-doctor in a charcoal-grey zone sells manhood by the procedure and then travels to conferences to teach actual doctors how to catch the gold rush. That is weaponisation in its plainest form: seize the power to define what counts as man enough, then charge admission.

Complicity here is not divided by sex. Ruben moves his five children in with an ex to raise the money; his wife Lana is against it but, in the report's words, does not take too strong a stance. Almost none of the partners wanted this. The cost is never carried by the payer alone. Only the invoice has one name on it.

Then there is David. He had a botched procedure young, goes to Moore for repairs, and the report casually reveals that he shares a bed with Moore, Moore's partner and the dog. A young gay man who performed in porn, repaired by an out gay businessman, both of them raised by devout parents who called homosexuality an abomination. That thread could carry the whole argument: masculine-centric narrative sets penis size as the measure of a man's worth, then lines up every man to reach for the ruler, including the men that narrative never rewarded. Instead the report files it under strangest turn and throuple gossip, and laughs it off.

Lombroso says the film has a light touch because these things are funny and self-evident to smart viewers. Funny, yes. Also real: a father destabilises his family to buy a bigger self, a young man gambles permanent damage on another table. The violence masculine-centric narrative does to men is to convince them their existence has a price tag set by a size that can be injected, priced and resold.

The report does not say what happened to Lana. It does not say where the five children moved.

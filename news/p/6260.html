法官拆穿了"恶意"的伪装The Judge Saw Through the "Malice"

其他 文化层 · 元暴力 The Hollywood Reporter ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
起诉书说媒体恶意，法官说起诉书才是制造
The suit cried malice; the judge said the suit was the fabrication.

法官说特朗普在"抓救命稻草"。这句话比判决本身更值得读——它点出的是谁在制造事实。

盖尔斯法官的裁定写得很清楚：修改后的起诉书"没有达到实际恶意的标准"，关于恶意的指控"是结论性的、含糊的、有时与文章本身不一致的，而且常常与被告是否知道或应当知道文章内容虚假无关"。更狠的一句是，起诉书试图在《华尔街日报》的调查报道中"制造"缺陷。制造。这个词用在这里，等于把原告的手法反过来照了一遍：不是报道造假，是起诉书在造假。

这是文化层的暴力在动，而且动得很典型。第三章讲表达武器化，认知入口的第一道门就是"什么是事实的制造权"。一百亿美元的诉讼不是为了赢——赢的概率从一开始就低，1964年《纽约时报》诉沙利文案定下的"实际恶意"门槛，公共人物几乎跨不过去。诉讼的功能是别的：让报道这件事本身变成一个需要被审理的对象，让"那封信是不是真的"这个问题从新闻版挪到法庭版，再从法庭版挪回舆论场，变成"连总统都告了，肯定有问题"。盖尔斯四月已经驳回过一次，给了修改机会，五月再递，现在被"with prejudice"永久驳回，不能再诉。两次机会用完了，制造出来的那点可能性也关了门。

报道的内容是什么：一封带性暗示的信和画，据称有特朗普的签名，收在2003年为爱泼斯坦五十岁生日编的相册里，由麦克斯韦收集。特朗普否认写过，说报道"虚假、恶意、诽谤"。法官注意到，《华尔街日报》的报道没有忽略或淡化这个否认——"它告知读者，特朗普总统斥责这封信是假的，并否认写过它"。这是新闻业该做的事，也是这场诉讼最尴尬的地方：它要告的那篇文章，恰恰把它的否认原样登了出来。

爱泼斯坦2019年8月在纽约监狱候审期间自杀身亡，当时面对的是联邦性贩运指控。这些是报道里的事实，不是我加的。围绕这个人名的一切——那本相册、那些名字、谁上过岛、谁写过什么——之所以长期占据公共注意力，是因为有一整套结构在决定哪些部分被看见、哪些部分被归档。这一层报道没写，我也不替它写。

还有一层：这不是孤例。报道列出特朗普第二任期内在打的官司——BBC、美联社，以及最近CNN、MS NOW和Politico因为他九月中旬把他们赶出白宫而反过来起诉他。把媒体赶出去，再告那些还留在里面的。这是一套组合动作，诉讼只是其中一件工具，而且是一件不太趁手的工具。

元暴力在这里的样子很朴素：解释权归谁。谁有权说"这是假新闻"，谁就有权决定什么算新闻。法官这一次把解释权按回了法律条文里——不是按给媒体，是按给了"实际恶意"这四个字。这不是媒体的胜利，是规则的胜利，而规则能赢，是因为原告连规则的门槛都没摸到。

那封信还在。判决书没有说它是真的，也没有说它是假的，只说起诉书没证明报道者明知其假或罔顾真相。差额就在这里：一个公共人物能不能用一百亿美元把"我不承认"变成"它不存在"，这一次的答案是不能。下一次换个法官、换个法院、换一批被告，这道门会不会还关着，没人保证。

The judge said Trump was "grasping at straws." That line is worth more than the dismissal itself — it names who was manufacturing facts.

Judge Gayles wrote that the amended complaint "falls short of pleading actual malice," and that the allegations regarding malice were "conclusory, vague, sometimes inconsistent with the Article, and often irrelevant to whether Defendants knew or should have known that the statements in the Article were purportedly false." Sharper still: the suit had attempted to "manufacture" flaws in the Journal's reporting. Manufacture. The word turns the plaintiff's method back on itself — not the article fabricating, the complaint fabricating.

This is cultural violence at work, and it's textbook. Chapter three: the first gate of the cognitive entrance is who gets to manufacture what counts as fact. A $10 billion suit was never about winning — the odds were bad from the start, because New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964) set an actual-malice bar public figures almost never clear. The suit's function was different: turn the reporting itself into something that must be adjudicated, move "is the letter real" from the news pages to the court pages and back into the public square as "even the president sued, so something must be wrong." Gayles dismissed it once in April, gave leave to amend, got a new complaint in May, and now dismissed it with prejudice — permanent, no refiling. Two chances spent; the possibility being manufactured is closed.

The story: a sexually suggestive letter and drawing said to bear Trump's name, collected in a 2003 album for Epstein's 50th birthday, assembled by Maxwell. Trump denied writing it and called the story "false, malicious, and defamatory." The judge noted the Journal did not ignore or minimize that denial — "Rather, it informed readers that President Trump decried the Letter as a fake and denied writing it." That is what the job is. It is also the suit's most awkward detail: the article it sued over printed its denial verbatim.

Epstein died by suicide in a New York jail cell in August 2019 while awaiting trial on federal sex trafficking charges. That is in the report; I'm not adding to it. Everything clustering around that name — the album, the names, who went where, who wrote what — holds public attention because some machinery decides which parts get seen and which get archived. The report doesn't go there, and I won't write it for them.

And it isn't isolated. The report lists the suits of Trump's second term — BBC, the Associated Press, and most recently CNN, MS NOW and Politico, who are suing him after he banished them from the White House in mid-September. Kick the press out, then sue whoever's left inside. A combined maneuver, and litigation is one of its tools — not the sharpest one.

Meta-violence looks plain here: who owns interpretation. Whoever gets to say "fake news" gets to decide what counts as news. This time the judge pressed interpretation back into the statute — not to the press, to the words "actual malice." Not a victory for media. A victory for the rule, won because the plaintiff couldn't clear its threshold.

The letter is still there. The ruling didn't say it was real or fake, only that the complaint failed to show the reporters knew it was false or recklessly disregarded the truth. That's the gap: whether a public figure can convert "I deny it" into "it doesn't exist" with ten billion dollars. This time, no. Next time, with a different judge, a different court, a different set of defendants — nobody's guaranteeing that door stays shut.

一首歌的十五周冠军，和写歌的人Fifteen Weeks at No. 1, and the People Who Wrote It

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要Ella Langley 的《Choosin’ Texas》在 8 月 1 日的 Billboard Hot 100 榜单上连续第 15 周夺冠，成为无联合署名女艺人非节日单曲中夺冠周数最多的一首，超过 Whitney Houston 和 Mariah Carey 的纪录。这首歌由 Langley、Miranda Lambert、Luke Dick 和 Joybeth Taylor 共同创作，多位知名词曲作者受访分析其走红原因。
The newsElla Langley's "Choosin' Texas" logged its 15th week at No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100 chart dated Aug. 1, the most weeks ever for a non-holiday title by a woman with no co-billed acts, surpassing records by Whitney Houston and Mariah Carey. The song was written by Langley, Miranda Lambert, Luke Dick and Joybeth Taylor, and top songwriters interviewed by Billboard explained why it has connected.
八个写歌的人夸它没有废音，没人问唱歌的女人拿到了什么
Eight songwriters praise its craft; nobody asks what the woman singing it got.

《Choosin' Texas》在 Hot 100 上待了十五周第一，超过惠特尼·休斯顿的《I Will Always Love You》和玛丽亚·凯莉的《We Belong Together》，成为没有合作署名的女性艺人非节日歌曲里夺冠最久的一首（来源：Billboard）。Billboard 请了八位乡村和流行界的头部写歌人来解释它为什么这么抓耳，八个人给了八个理由：开头的吉他钩子、音色、旋律、制作、Ella Langley 的演唱。这是一篇关于手艺的报道，写得挺真诚，我读完只觉得少了一个问题。

少的问题是：这首歌在讲什么，以及那个故事是给谁听的。Laura Veltz 说得最清楚，她说这是一个女人“完全清楚自己的处境、清楚自己的处境没有希望”，却“没有眼泪地、脆弱地”渴望，而且“这首歌里没有反派”，她对每个人都“又难过又高兴”。一首关于女人选择留下、选择德州、选择一段明知没救的关系的歌，被夸成“没有反派”，这就是第三章里那个最老的情感入口：把单向的牺牲写成双向的深情，把结构写成天气。没有反派，是因为反派不在歌里，在歌外面。

再看署名。这首歌由 Langley、Miranda Lambert、Luke Dick、Joybeth Taylor 四个人写，两位女性两位男性，这在乡村音乐里已经算体面。但 Billboard 请来点评的八个人里，七位是男性写歌人，唯一一位女性是 Laura Veltz。八个人轮流夸一个女歌手“毫不费力的自信”“传奇地位”“把她们放在现代写歌的最前面”，夸得都很热情，但没有一个人被问：你自己写过多少首这样的歌，拿到过多少分成，你夸的这个行业给写歌的女人付了多少钱。手艺被讨论得很细，定价权一个字没提。

这不是说歌不好。Ashley Gorley 说“没有废音”，Darrell Brown 说它“不追当下、活得比当下久”，这些判断可能都对。问题在于，一篇纯手艺视角的报道，恰好是最有效的武器化形式：它让一首歌的成功看起来只关乎音符、旋律和“完美的时机”，于是行业里谁写、谁唱、谁分钱、谁的叙事被反复播放，全都退到画面外。Ryan Hurd 说这让他欣慰，因为“最好的成功机会仍然来自几个写歌人在一个房间里做出真正特别的东西”——这句话对写歌人成立，对唱歌的女人成立不成立，报道没问。

十五周第一是一个真实的成绩，Langley 值得。但“没有废音”这个夸奖，和“没有反派”这个夸奖，放在一起看，就是这套产业最熟练的一次自我表扬：它把一个女人在歌里选择不走的处境，包装成了所有人都能共情的美德，然后让八个男人来确认这个包装的手艺有多好。歌里那个女人清楚自己的处境没有希望，歌外这篇报道里，没有人被允许清楚她的处境是怎么被写出来的。

"Choosin' Texas" has spent 15 weeks at No. 1 on the Hot 100, passing Whitney Houston's "I Will Always Love You" and Mariah Carey's "We Belong Together" to become the longest-running non-holiday No. 1 by a woman with no co-billed acts (source: Billboard). Billboard asked eight top country and pop songwriters to explain why it's so catchy, and they gave eight reasons: the opening guitar hook, the sonic palette, the melody, the production, Ella Langley's vocal. It's a piece about craft, written in good faith, and reading it I kept waiting for the one question nobody asked.

The question is what the song is about, and who the story is for. Laura Veltz puts it most clearly: this is a woman "completely aware of herself and her hopeless situation," pining without tears, and "there are also no villains in this song" — she's "weirdly equally sad and happy for everyone." A song about a woman choosing to stay, choosing Texas, choosing a relationship she already knows is lost, gets praised for having no villain. That is the oldest emotional entry point in the book: write one-way sacrifice as two-way devotion, write structure as weather. There are no villains because the villain isn't in the song. It's outside it.

Then look at the bylines. The song was written by Langley, Miranda Lambert, Luke Dick and Joybeth Taylor — two women, two men, which in Nashville counts as decent. But of the eight songwriters Billboard asked to assess it, seven are men; the only woman is Veltz. Eight people take turns praising a woman singer's "effortless confidence," her "legendary status," the way this puts "these badasses at the forefront of modern songwriting" — all warm, and not one of them is asked: how many songs like this have you written, what did you get paid, what does this industry pay the women who write its hits. Craft gets discussed down to the note. Pricing power doesn't come up once.

None of this means the song is bad. When Ashley Gorley says "there are no wasted notes," when Darrell Brown says it doesn't chase the moment but outlives it, they may well be right. The problem is that a pure-craft frame is the most effective form of weaponization available: it makes a hit look like it's about notes and melody and "perfect timing in pop culture," and everything else — who writes, who sings, who gets paid, whose narrative gets played on repeat — slides off the page. Ryan Hurd says it's encouraging, because "the best chance for success at this level still comes down to great songwriters in a room." True for the songwriters. Whether it's true for the woman singing, the piece never asks.

Fifteen weeks at No. 1 is a real achievement and Langley earned it. But "no wasted notes" and "no villains," read together, are this industry's most polished self-congratulation: take a woman's situation — staying, in a song she knows is hopeless — package it as a virtue everyone can feel, then have eight men confirm how well the packaging was crafted. Inside the song, the woman is fully aware her situation is hopeless. Outside it, in this piece, nobody is allowed to be aware of how that situation got written.

四十五天的暂停，买不到解释权Forty-Five Days Buys No Say

科技 结构层 · 文化层 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要美国加州湾区七个城市（包括旧金山、奥克兰、里士满）在中期选举前通过数据中心临时禁令，暂停期为45天。居民担忧数据中心推高电费、加重电网负担并加剧环境问题。旧金山市长候选人陈康妮主张全国性禁令，对手威纳反对任何禁令；民调显示威纳以47%对41%领先。
The newsSeven Bay Area cities, including San Francisco and Oakland, have passed temporary 45-day moratoriums on data centre construction ahead of the US midterms. Residents worry the centres will strain the power grid and raise household electricity bills. San Francisco supervisor Connie Chan backs a national moratorium; state Senator Scott Wiener opposes any ban and leads 47 to 41 percent in polling.
禁令暂停的是施工，不是定价权。
The moratorium pauses construction, not the pricing power.

七个城市通过了数据中心禁令，四十五天。这个数字本身就是答案：它不是要解决问题，是要把问题挪到选举之后。旧金山的政治分析师 Shum Preston 说得比谁都坦白——开发商不想在选举前给他们的市议员朋友添麻烦，所以「let this pass」；等四十五天到期，选举季的热度散了，「听证会才会变得有争议」。禁令不是治理，是排期。

真正被摆上桌的代价，是电费。奥克兰的环保组织活动人士 Carlos Gutierrez 说，电价已经涨到有人考虑断电，而城市还打算给数据中心供电。这句话里有一整套暴力的分配方式：算力是新的生产资料，电力是它的燃料，而账单落在那些连自己家的电都想关掉的人头上。数据中心需要二十四小时供电、大量水冷却，散出的热量能让地表温度升高多达九摄氏度。这些成本全部外部化，收益全部内部化——Anthropic 正在准备一场可能募资数十亿美元的 IPO。

所以候选人吵的其实不是数据中心。Wiener 反对任何禁令，理由是「一刀切的暂停会削弱 AI 监管」——把暂停和监管对立起来，等于说先让它建，规则以后再说。Chan 主张全国性暂停，直到负责任的 AI 监管到位。两个人争的是解释权：这场扩张到底是经济复兴，还是一次未经同意的资源转移。而这个问题被压缩成 47 比 41 的民调数字，压缩成 Pelosi 空出来的那个席位。

更值得看的是禁令为什么这么容易通过。Preston 说得很清楚：数据中心的民调很差，十月 CNBC 的调查里 63% 的登记选民反对在自己家十六公里内建数据中心。于是开发商退一步，让禁令过去，等风头过了再回来。这不是居民赢了，这是共谋双方各让一步、把冲突延后。四十五天之后，同样的听证会会重开，而那时选举已经结束，选票不再值钱。

还有一层更硬的墙：旧金山的规划法规把数据中心定义成「互联网服务交换」，这个定义是 2001 年写的，比 AI 热潮早了二十年。市议员 Shamann Walton 说，过时的措辞让当局数不清城里到底有多少数据中心，也就无法监测它们的用电和用水。生物多样性中心的高级律师 Roger Lin 补了一刀：数据中心常签保密协议，连当地的空气质量委员会都不知道它们对空气做了什么。一个城市要管一件事，先得能看见它；而现在连数都数不清，四十五天连记录和研究的门都摸不到，更不用说立规。

听证会上有个居民说了一句话：「这座城市越来越少为人类，越来越多为机器人和亿万富翁。」这句话不是情绪，是对 Violence = Potential − Actual 的精确描述——同样的电、同样的水、同样的土地，本可以供给住在奥克兰的人，现在被划给了不需要在这座城市呼吸的服务器。禁令暂停的是施工，不是这个划法。四十五天后，施工会回来，账单也会回来。

Seven cities passed data centre moratoriums. Forty-five days. That number is the answer: the point is not to solve the problem but to reschedule it past the election. Shum Preston, a San Francisco political analyst, says it plainly — developers don't want to put their supervisor friends in trouble ahead of elections, so they let it pass. When the 45 days end, the heat of the campaign will have settled, and that's when the hearings get contentious.

The cost actually on the table is electricity. Carlos Gutierrez of Communities for a Better Environment says power prices in Oakland have risen so much that some people are thinking of shutting off their power, and the city is planning to provide power to a data centre instead. That sentence contains a whole distribution of violence: compute is the new means of production, electricity is its fuel, and the bill lands on people who are considering cutting their own power. Data centres need round-the-clock power and large supplies of water to cool processors; they emit enough heat to raise land surface temperatures by as high as 9 degrees Celsius. The costs are externalised, the gains internalised — Anthropic is preparing an IPO likely to raise billions.

So the candidates are not really arguing about data centres. Wiener opposes any ban, arguing a blanket moratorium could undermine AI regulation — putting the pause and the rules in opposition, which means build first, regulate later. Chan pledges a nationwide moratorium until responsible AI regulation is in place. What they are fighting over is the power to explain: is this expansion an economic renaissance, or an unconsented transfer of resources? And that question gets compressed into a 47-to-41 poll and the seat Nancy Pelosi left vacant.

More telling is why the bans passed so easily. Preston notes data centres poll very poorly — an October CNBC poll found 63 percent of registered voters oppose building them within 16 kilometres of their homes. So developers step back, let the moratorium pass, and return when the wind changes. This is not residents winning. It is two parties to a collusion each giving a little, deferring the conflict. In 45 days the same hearings reopen, and by then the votes are no longer worth anything.

There is a harder wall underneath: San Francisco's planning code defines data centres as "internet service exchanges", a definition written in 2001, two decades before the AI boom. Supervisor Shamann Walton says the outdated language makes it hard for authorities to count how many data centres exist in the city, which also prevents monitoring their power and water use. Roger Lin, senior attorney at the Center for Biological Diversity, adds that data centres often have nondisclosure agreements, so even local air quality boards may not know their impact on air quality. A city has to be able to see a thing before it can govern it; right now it cannot even count them, and 45 days is hardly enough time to document and study the impacts, let alone frame regulations.

At a hearing, a resident said the city is less for humans and more for robots and billionaires. That is not a mood. It is a precise statement of Violence = Potential − Actual: the same power, the same water, the same land could have gone to the people who live in Oakland, and instead it is allocated to servers that do not breathe this city's air. The moratorium pauses construction. It does not pause that allocation. In 45 days the construction comes back, and so does the bill.

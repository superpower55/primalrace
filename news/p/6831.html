先制造「非法选民」，再收割恐惧Manufacture the «Illegal Voter», Then Harvest the Fear

国际 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 Truthout ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要中期选举投票日，投票权专家正观察特朗普政府加强对选举的联邦干预会如何展开：要求各州交出未删节选民名册、限制邮寄投票、由国土安全部识别并起诉非公民选民。法院迄今阻止了大部分计划，但司法部已就加州约2万名「疑似非公民」选民提起诉讼，并向华盛顿州列出400人名单。
The newsAs voters head to the polls, election experts are watching how the Trump administration's push for federal control over elections unfolds: demands for unredacted voter rolls, restrictions on mail voting, and DHS efforts to identify and prosecute noncitizen voters. Courts have blocked most plans so far, but DOJ has sued California over some 20,000 alleged noncitizen voters and sent Washington state a list of 400 names.
投票权被削掉的那部分，从来不是从投票日开始的。
The voting rights being stripped away were never taken on Election Day.

这条新闻里最该被盯住的不是投票日会不会出乱子，而是「非法选民」这个类别是怎么被制造出来的。政府把各州公开的选民名册和移民数据、第三方数据混在一起，得出一份「疑似非公民」名单，然后拿这份名单去起诉州、去威胁州官员、去敲选民的門。名单准不准不重要，重要的是名单存在本身就在生产恐惧。

洛杉矶县抽查了联邦声称登记在册的「疑似非公民」，结果很多人根本不在该县登记，或者从未投过票，还有一些人在登记表上写明自己出生在美国。华盛顿州收到的400人名单里，有美国公民、有没登记的人、有重复条目。一个把公民混进「疑似非公民」名单的机制，功能不是抓舞弊，是让每一个名字被卷进去的人自己证明自己不是罪犯。这就是文化暴力在给结构暴力铺路：先让「有人在偷你的票」变成一个不需要证据的前提，再让这个前提去合理化一切后续动作。

真正在动的是结构层。北卡罗来纳要求邮寄投票提供身份证明，选票改成双层信封、两层都得封口，结果选票被拒率飙升，因为选民没封内层信封。得州塔兰特县把投票站砍掉近三分之一。得州公共安全局的软件错误让几十万份新登记没能转给地方选举官员。这些没有一个是「舞弊」，每一个都在削减实际可达到的投票量。Violence = Potential − Actual，差额就是被这些规则吃掉的那部分选票，而吃掉的选票不会平均分布。

元层也在动，只是更安静。司法部起诉阿拉斯加，要求在该州参议员选举结果可能受「选民困惑」影响时阻止认证——而所谓困惑，是另一个也叫 Dan Sullivan 的人参选。联邦政府亲自下场介入一场正在进行的选举，理由是选票上出现了同名的人。这不是法律争议，这是解释权的争夺：谁来定义什么叫「困惑」，谁就有权决定什么结果算数。

CISA 被拆掉这件事值得单独说一句。2024年那批假炸弹威胁能迅速被判定为不可信，靠的是联邦机构搭的信息通道；现在通道没了，各州自己扛。同一届政府一边拆掉防御，一边宣称选举充满欺诈——先制造问题，再以解决问题的名义扩大权力，这是共谋者理论里最标准的剧本。

报道里被反复引用的是专家、州务卿、检察官、司法部发言人。真正会站在投票站门口被拦下、被名单吓退、因为没封内层信封而被拒票的人，一个名字都没有出现。这不是报道的失误，这是这类新闻的结构性盲区：选举被写成一場制度博弈，而制度博弈的代价总是由没有发言权的人支付。

法院一次次挡住这些动作，这当然是好消息，但每挡住一次都要花掉时间、律师费和某个州的选举办公室的整个星期。挡得住的是命令，挡不住的是那句「也许我不去登记了，也许我不投了」——那句话不需要任何法官批准，就已经生效了。

The thing to watch in this story isn't whether Election Day gets messy. It's how the category of «noncitizen voter» gets manufactured. The government blends public voter rolls with immigration data and third-party data, produces a list of «likely noncitizens», and then uses that list to sue states, threaten officials, and knock on doors. Whether the list is accurate is beside the point. The list's existence is the point: it produces fear.

Los Angeles County sampled the voters the federal government claimed were registered there. Many weren't registered in the county, or had never voted; some had written on their registration forms that they were born in the United States. Washington state's list of 400 names included U.S. citizens, people who weren't registered, and duplicate entries. A mechanism that folds citizens into a «likely noncitizen» list isn't built to catch fraud. It's built to make every name caught in it prove it isn't criminal. That's cultural violence laying the track for structural violence: first «someone is stealing your vote» becomes a premise that needs no evidence, then the premise justifies everything that follows.

The structural layer is where the real action is. North Carolina now requires ID for mail ballots and two envelopes, both sealed; rejections have skyrocketed because voters don't seal the inner one. Tarrant County, Texas, cut polling sites by nearly a third. A software error at the Texas Department of Public Safety stopped several hundred thousand new registrations from reaching local officials. None of this is fraud. All of it shrinks the actual vote. Violence = Potential − Actual, and the gap is the ballots these rules eat. The eaten ballots are not distributed evenly.

The meta layer moves more quietly. DOJ sued Alaska to block certification of its Senate race if «voter confusion» might affect the result — the confusion being that another man named Dan Sullivan qualified for the ballot. The federal government intervening in a live election because two candidates share a name isn't a legal dispute. It's a fight over interpretation: whoever defines «confusion» decides which results count.

The dismantling of CISA deserves its own line. In 2024, false bomb threats were quickly flagged as not credible because a federally funded channel told election officials so. That channel is gone; states are on their own. The same administration tears down the defenses while claiming elections are riddled with fraud — create the problem, then expand power in the name of solving it. That's the standard script.

The people quoted here are experts, secretaries of state, attorneys general, a DOJ spokesperson. The people who will be turned away at the polls, scared off by a list, or have a ballot rejected over an unsealed envelope — not one of them has a name in this piece. That's not a reporting failure. It's the blind spot of election coverage as a genre: elections get written as institutional chess, and the cost of the game is always paid by people with no seat at the board.

Courts keep blocking these moves, which is good news, but every block costs time, lawyers' fees, and a week of some election office's life. What courts can't block is the sentence «maybe I just won't register, maybe I won't vote.» That sentence needs no judge's approval. It's already in effect.

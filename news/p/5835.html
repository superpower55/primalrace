两次撞车之间，少掉的那份安全Between Two Crashes, the Safety That Wasn't There

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
同一线路一年两撞，不是天灾，是差额。
Two crashes on one line in nine months isn't fate. It's the gap nobody filled.

西班牙高铁同一条线，一月份撞死46人，周三又撞死至少69人（来源：The Guardian）。九个月，两场灾难，中间隔着的不是运气，是 Violence = Potential − Actual 里那个没人去填的差额。

结构暴力最擅长的伪装，就是让差额看起来像现状。铁路安全、基础设施维护、公共服务投入——这些词听起来像预算表上的技术项，实际上是一条条人命被折算成的数字。政治学者 Pablo Simón 说得很直接：这次事故把注意力引向铁路基础设施的状态，暗示问题是结构性的，指向西班牙公共服务的整体下滑。他还补了一句关键的话——桑切斯政府2018年上台，没法把责任推给前任人民党的紧缩削减，“那根本不可信”。八年执政，账在自己名下。

然后看右翼怎么接这个球。人民党的 Feijóo 说，任何被认定对事故负责的政治人物都要被追责，“不能再这样下去，这种管理不能继续”。Vox 更进一步，直接指控政府“杀害”了事故受害者，还要求最高法院以过失杀人和重大疏忽造成伤害调查交通部长 Óscar Puente。Puente 否认管理不善，坚称西班牙铁路系统“安全可靠”。

这里有个很值得盯的细节：Vox 把“杀害”这个词用在了政治对手身上，而这个词真正该指向的地方——那条一年内夺走一百多条命的线路为什么还在跑——被转换成了选举武器。事故的死者成了竞选修辞里的弹药，而不是结构问题的证据。这不是在缩小差额，是在消费差额。

桑切斯本人说，一年内两起高铁灾难“不可接受”，承诺加强安全措施，“让这样的事永远不再发生”。承诺不是落地。立法不等于执行，声明不等于资源到位。报道里没有任何一句提到具体的安全投入数字、时间表、问责机制——只有“承诺”。这就是 structural violence 的 PR 版本：用语言填补本该用预算和工程填补的洞。

更有意思的是这场选举原本的剧本。左翼把它包装成阻止人民党和 Vox 联手倒退数十年社会进步的“最后机会”，右翼把它包装成终结八年“sanchismo”的机会。住房改革被右翼和极右翼投票否决，抗议爆发，桑切斯宣布提前大选。然后火车撞了。Simón 说，桑切斯原本指望动员120万“政治休眠”但倾向社民党的选民，现在全国哀悼，竞选需要的高昂情绪和乐观氛围根本无从谈起。

这就是文化层和结构层的联动：一场本可以讨论住房、平等、社会进步的选举，被一场结构暴力事件强行改写议题。而改写的方式不是让结构问题成为焦点，是让结构问题变成攻击对手的棍子。铁路安全本该是“为什么公共服务在退化”的问题，现在成了“谁该为死者负责”的表演。前者指向系统，后者指向个人。指向个人的问责表演，恰恰是让系统继续免于被追问的最有效方式。

那120万张票呢？他们不是被说服走的，是被一场本可避免的灾难吓走的。结构暴力的代价从来不只写在死者名单上，还写在那些本该投票却因为“整个国家都在哀悼”而留在家里的人身上。

桑切斯擅长从逆境中获利，这是西班牙政治的老话。但这次逆境是他自己八年的账。同一线路九个月撞两次，第一次之后承诺过什么，报道没写。没写，往往就是答案。

Same high-speed line. Forty-six dead in January. At least 69 dead on Wednesday (source: The Guardian). Nine months between two catastrophes, and what sits in that gap isn't bad luck — it's the difference in Violence = Potential − Actual that nobody bothered to close.

Structural violence's favorite disguise is making the gap look like the status quo. Rail safety, infrastructure maintenance, public service investment — these sound like line items in a budget, but they're human lives converted into numbers. Political scientist Pablo Simón puts it plainly: the crash draws attention to the state of railway infrastructure, suggesting the problem is structural, pointing to a broader decline in Spanish public services. He adds the line that matters — Sánchez's government has been in power since 2018 and can't blame the PP's austerity cuts. "That's simply not credible." Eight years. The account is in his name.

Now watch how the right catches the ball. Feijóo of the PP says any politician found responsible will be held to account: "We can't go on like this; this kind of management just can't carry on." Vox goes further, accusing the government of "killing" the victims and urging the supreme court to investigate transport minister Óscar Puente for manslaughter and gross negligence. Puente denies mismanagement, insisting the railway system is "safe and reliable."

Here's the detail worth staring at: Vox aims the word "killing" at its political opponents, while the place that word actually belongs — why a line that has now taken over a hundred lives in a year is still running — gets converted into campaign ammunition. The dead become rhetorical ordnance, not evidence of a structural problem. That isn't closing the gap. That's consuming it.

Sánchez himself says two high-speed disasters in under a year is "unacceptable," and promises to strengthen safety measures "so that something like this never happens again." A promise is not implementation. Legislation is not enforcement. A declaration is not resources on the ground. The report contains no figure, no timeline, no accountability mechanism — only the promise. This is the PR edition of structural violence: language filling a hole that should be filled with budgets and engineering.

And the election's original script is worth noting. The left billed it as a last chance to stop the PP and Vox from rolling back decades of social progress; the right billed it as the end of eight years of "sanchismo." Housing reforms were voted down by the right and far right, protests erupted, Sánchez called a snap election. Then the trains collided. Simón says Sánchez had been counting on mobilizing 1.2 million politically dormant but PSOE-leaning voters — and now, with the country in mourning, the high spirits and optimism a campaign requires simply can't exist.

This is cultural and structural layers working together: an election that could have been about housing, equality, social progress gets its agenda forcibly rewritten by an act of structural violence. And the rewrite doesn't make the structural problem the focus — it makes it a stick to beat opponents with. Rail safety should be the question "why are public services degrading?" Instead it becomes the performance of "who is to blame for the dead?" The first points at the system. The second points at individuals. And individual accountability theater is exactly the most effective way to let the system escape being questioned.

What about those 1.2 million votes? They weren't persuaded away. They were scared away by a catastrophe that didn't have to happen. The cost of structural violence is never only written on the death toll — it's also written on the people who should have voted but stayed home because "the entire country is in mourning."

Sánchez has a reputation for profiting from adversity. But this adversity is his own eight-year ledger. What was promised after the first crash on that line, the report doesn't say. What isn't said is usually the answer.

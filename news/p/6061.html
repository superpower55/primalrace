战争在别人家，账单在你家The War Is Theirs, the Bill Is Yours

国际 结构层 · 文化层 NPR ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
差额不是天灾，是有人把战争兑换成股息。
The gap isn't weather. Someone converted a war into dividends.

三家公司一个季度赚了360亿美元。埃克森美孚154亿，比去年同期翻了一倍多；雪佛龙涨了180%；壳牌几乎是自己的两倍。原因报道写得很干净：伊朗战争让中东的油运不出去，别处的油就变贵了。布伦特原油从开战前每桶70美元涨到113美元。需求没怎么变，人们还是要开车、取暖、用拖拉机、把货从一处运到另一处。

这就是 Violence = Potential − Actual 最赤裸的样子。同样的油，同样的管道，同样的人去加同样的油——唯一改变的是价格。差额没有变成任何新的东西，只是从加油的人口袋里移到了股东口袋里。埃克森宣布了史上最高的季度股息，壳牌做了自己历史上最大规模的回购之一。加尔通说的结构暴力，最怕的就是被当成“市场”而不被识别为暴力；这里连伪装都省了，直接写进了财报。

更值得盯的是钱去了哪里。皮克林说得很直白：这些公司大多按兵不动，不投新的钻井项目，因为“这是地缘政治事件驱动的高价”，难以预测能持续多久，所以“把钱存进银行，看看2027年会发生什么”。也就是说，这笔钱既没有变成新的供给，也没有变成对消费者的补偿，它变成了资产负债表上的现金，和股东账户里的数字。所谓“周期性行业要留现金过冬”，翻译过来是：好年景的收益归私人，坏年景的风险归社会。这不是市场，这是定价权。

埃克森CEO伍兹说，他们“认识到高油价对人们生活和经济增长的影响”，解决方案是“最大化产量”。雪佛龙CEO沃斯说，“我们能做的是我们能控制的事”。两句话都很好听，但都绕开了同一个问题：他们控制不了战争的走向，却完整地接收了战争带来的价格。报道写得很清楚，柴油9月创下历史新高，汽油比一年前每加仑贵了约1.20美元。这笔钱不是他们“努力”赚来的，是别人在付。

所以民主党提出的暴利税法案，方向是对的——韦伯那句话说得准：最坏的时刻变成了最好的时刻，这本身就有问题。但法案是春天提出的，中期选举就在周二，而报道自己承认，这些压低油价的行政措施“不太可能在周二之前让价格明显下降”。立法不等于落地，这是结构暴力的老套路：提出一个正确的方案，然后让它停在提出阶段。真正的问题不是“要不要收暴利税”，而是“谁有能力决定这笔钱归谁”——解释权和定价权在谁手里，差额就流向谁。

报道里没有出现的是那些付账的人。他们没有名字，没有在财报电话会上发言的机会，也没有被问过“你们能控制什么”。他们只是在加油的时候多付了1.20美元，然后在新闻里读到“中东的损失，大体上是美国和欧洲油气公司的收益”。这句话是皮克林说的，说得很诚实——诚实到像一句判决书。

Three companies made $36 billion in one quarter. ExxonMobil earned $15.4 billion, more than double its earnings a year earlier. Chevron's were up 180%. Shell nearly doubled its own. The reason, as the report lays it out cleanly: the war in Iran has disrupted the global oil supply, so oil elsewhere became much more valuable. Brent crude went from about $70 a barrel before the war to around $113.

This is Violence = Potential − Actual at its most naked. Same oil, same pipelines, same people filling the same tanks — the only thing that changed is the price. The gap didn't become anything new. It moved from the pockets of drivers to the pockets of shareholders. Exxon announced its highest-ever quarterly dividend. Shell ran one of its biggest buybacks ever. Structural violence is most dangerous when it gets read as "the market" instead of as violence; here it doesn't even bother to disguise itself — it's written straight into the earnings report.

What matters more is where the money goes. Pickering says it plainly: the companies are mostly in a wait-and-see stance, not spending on new drilling, because this is a geopolitical price spike and no one knows how long it lasts. So they "put the money in the bank and watch." The money becomes neither new supply nor compensation for consumers. It becomes cash on the balance sheet and numbers in shareholder accounts. "A cyclical industry needs to hold cash for the bad years" translates to: the upside is privatized, the downside is socialized. That isn't a market. That's pricing power.

Exxon CEO Darren Woods says they "recognize the impact high prices have on people's lives" and that their solution is to maximize production. Chevron CEO Mike Wirth says they can only do "what we can control." Both lines sound fine and both dodge the same thing: they can't control how the war goes, but they collect the full price the war creates. The report is precise — diesel hit a record high in September, gasoline costs about $1.20 more per gallon than a year ago. This money wasn't earned through effort. It was paid by someone else.

The windfall tax bills Democrats introduced this spring point in the right direction. Isabella Weber's line is exact: the worst of times becoming the best of times raises fundamental questions about who benefits and who loses. But the bills were introduced in spring, the midterms are Tuesday, and the report itself concedes the administration's measures are unlikely to bring prices down noticeably by then. Legislation is not implementation — the oldest move in structural violence: propose the right fix, then let it sit at the proposal stage. The real question isn't whether to tax windfall profits. It's who gets to decide where the gap goes. Whoever holds pricing power and the power to explain it gets the difference.

The people paying don't appear in the report. They have no names, no seat on the earnings call, no one asking them what they can control. They just pay $1.20 more at the pump and then read that "the Middle East's loss is generally the gain of oil and gas companies in the U.S. and Europe." Pickering said that. It's honest — honest enough to read like a verdict.

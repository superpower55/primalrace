不确定感是新的消费入口Uncertainty Is the New Sales Funnel

哲学 结构层 · 文化层 CNBC ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
把结构性焦虑翻译成冷水洗脸，是共谋者的拿手活。
Turning structural anxiety into a cold-water facial is the co-conspirator's signature move.

这篇报道从头到尾在干一件事：把一种结构性的处境，翻译成一门自我管理的技术。

美国心理学会2026年的数据说，超过三分之二的美国成年人已经习惯了与不确定共处，28%的人说自己对未来几乎没有或完全没有控制力，2025年这个数字是23%。一年涨了五个百分点。报道把这段放在第四段，然后立刻转向专家建议：往脸上泼冷水、脱鞋站草地、出门走路、念一句咒语。

28%的人觉得自己对未来没有控制力。这个数字在涨。而报道给他们的方案是让他们相信自己对身体有控制力。冷水是真的，草地是真的，神经系统的反应也是真的。但“我没有控制力”这句话说的是工作、房租、医疗、气候，不是脚底的温度。把前者换成后者，就是在把结构暴力翻译成个人情绪，再卖给你一套情绪管理。

报道自己写得很清楚：不确定性的总量没有变，变的是人暴露在不确定性里的密度。手机、TikTok、Instagram推送，让人一天二十四小时泡在世界的坏消息里。这个诊断是对的，但只对了一半。信息过载是真的，信息过载背后是注意力被当成商品在卖，是平台靠放大恐惧赚钱。报道把这一层写成“我们接触得更多了”，然后就把责任交回给读者：那你就少看一点，去草地上站一会儿。

更有意思的是报道列出的那些“草率决定”。有人急着在恋爱关系早期要一个承诺，有人没找好下家就辞职，有人让AI替自己解读对话、预测结果、告诉自己该做什么决定，有人转向宗教组织，找灵媒，用爱情咒语找伴侣。

这些不是草率。这些是人在规则不给自己留位置的时候，能找到的最优解表达。急着要承诺的人，是在一个什么都不稳的环境里，想抓住一个能签字的确定；辞职的人，是在算过账之后发现留下更亏；找灵媒和爱情咒语的人，是在一个把浪漫爱叙事卖了一百年的社会里，被训练成相信“找到那个人”能解决一切，于是当一切都不确定的时候，她们去求那个叙事里最灵的东西。

报道把这些行为归到“不确定性”底下，等于把一个人的处境说成一个人的症状。症状可以治，处境不能靠泼冷水治。

至于让AI替自己做决定，这是整篇里最值得停一下的地方。人把判断力交出去，换一个“大概会这样”的概率，这和找灵媒是同一件事的两个版本，一个披着技术的皮，一个披着神秘主义的皮。区别只在于，AI那一版有人收订阅费。

专家说，念那句咒语“而且它是真的”——没有任何证据表明接下来发生什么你处理不了。这句话在报道里被当成安慰。它也可以读成另一层意思：你处理不了的那些事，本来就不该由你一个人处理。

报道没有写的是：那些真的处理不了的人去哪了。28%里觉得自己完全没有控制力的那部分人，付不起心理咨询费，也不会出现在精神科医生的客户名单上。他们不在报道里，因为报道采访的是能付得起诊费的人，给的建议是能去草地的人。

冷水泼在脸上很有效，我信。但泼完之后，那个让你需要泼冷水的东西还在原地。

This article does one thing from start to finish: it translates a structural condition into a self-management technique.

The American Psychological Association's 2026 report says just over two-thirds of U.S. adults have grown accustomed to living with uncertainty, and 28% say they have little to no control over their future, up from 23% in 2025. Five points in one year. The piece drops that in the fourth paragraph and immediately pivots to expert advice: splash cold water on your face, take your shoes off in the grass, go outside and move, repeat a mantra.

28% feel they have no control over their future, and the number is rising. The article's answer is to help them feel control over their bodies. The cold water is real, the grass is real, the nervous system response is real. But "I have no control" is a statement about work, rent, healthcare, climate — not about the temperature under your feet. Swapping the first for the second is how structural violence gets translated into personal mood, and then sold back to you as mood management.

The piece is honest about one thing: the amount of uncertainty hasn't changed, only people's exposure to it. Smartphones, TikTok, Instagram reels, an endless stream of headlines. That diagnosis is correct and half-finished. Information overload is real, and behind it sits an attention economy that profits from amplifying fear. The article writes this layer as "we're exposed to more now" and hands the responsibility back to the reader: so look less, go stand in the grass.

More interesting are the "rash decisions" it lists. People pushing for commitment early in relationships. People quitting jobs with nothing lined up. People asking AI to interpret conversations, predict outcomes, tell them what to decide. People turning to religious organizations, psychics, love spells to find a partner.

These aren't rash. These are the optimal expressions available to people the rules have left no seat for. The one demanding commitment is grabbing at the only thing that can be signed in an environment where nothing holds. The one who quit ran the numbers and found staying cost more. The one casting love spells was trained by a century of romantic-love marketing to believe that finding "the one" solves everything, so when everything is uncertain she goes to the most reliable thing that narrative ever sold.

The article files all of this under "uncertainty," which turns a person's situation into a person's symptom. Symptoms can be treated. Situations don't respond to cold water.

The AI part deserves a pause. Handing your judgment to a machine in exchange for a probability is the same move as consulting a psychic, one wearing tech and one wearing mysticism. The difference is that the AI version charges a subscription.

The expert says the mantra is true — there's nothing to suggest you can't handle whatever comes next. The article prints this as comfort. It can also be read the other way: the things you can't handle were never supposed to be handled alone.

What the article doesn't write is where the ones who truly can't cope went. The slice of that 28% with no sense of control who can't afford a psychiatrist and never appear on Dr. Song's client list. They're absent because the piece interviews people who can pay for sessions and advises people who can reach grass.

Cold water on the face works, I believe it. But after you dry off, the thing that made you need it is still standing right where it was.

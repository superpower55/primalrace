换一个标签，同一场围猎A New Label, the Same Hunt

国际 文化层 · 元暴力 The Intercept ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要密歇根州参议员选举进入最后阶段，民主党候选人Abdul El-Sayed遭到以宗教和族裔为靶子的攻击：特朗普称他为「圣战分子」，卫生部长小罗伯特·肯尼迪发表针对阿拉伯人的言论，还有邮件把他的脸与世贸双子塔并置。初选落败的众议员Haley Stevens在最后几天转而指责他反犹，并跨党背书共和党对手Mike Rogers。一项民调显示，38%的密歇根选民表示更不愿投票给穆斯林候选人。
The newsMichigan's Senate race enters its final stretch with Democratic nominee Abdul El-Sayed facing attacks on his religion and ethnicity: Trump called him a "jihadist," HHS Secretary RFK Jr. made racist remarks about Arabs, and a mailer placed his face beside the Twin Towers. Primary runner-up Rep. Haley Stevens spent the final days calling him an antisemite and crossed party lines to endorse Republican Mike Rogers. One poll found 38 percent of Michigan voters said they would be less likely to vote for a Muslim candidate.
攻击他的宗教，和攻击他反犹，是同一件事的两个步骤。
Attacking his religion and calling him antisemitic are two steps of one operation.

先看清这场围猎的机制：特朗普叫他「圣战分子」，小罗伯特·肯尼迪对着阿拉伯人开骂，一封邮件把他的脸和双子塔摆在一起；到了最后几天，初选输给他的Haley Stevens接过话筒，换成「愤怒的反犹分子」这个标签，然后跨党背书共和党人Mike Rogers。前后两套词，指向同一个人。这不是立场转变，是换弹匣。

Rogers坚持说这些攻击和他的信仰无关。这句话本身就是武器化的一部分：把针对一个穆斯林候选人的宗教动员，包装成对「反美」「反犹」的正当质疑。第三章讲的就是这个——夺取解释权，就是夺取「什么是事实」的制造权。谁是恐怖分子、谁在仇恨，由攻击方来定义，被定义的人只能自证。

Stevens的转向值得单独说。她不是被谁骗了，她是算过的：初选输了，剩下的筹码是把自己变成对手阵营里最有用的那个人。这就是共谋者理论里最标准的一种——不需要她相信那套话，只需要她执行那套话，回报是一个仍然属于建制内部的席位。Elabed说得准：密歇根的阿拉伯裔民主党人警告了她很多年，没人听。不是他们错了，是他们不在听的人的利益里。

38%的密歇根选民说更不愿投票给穆斯林候选人。这个数字不是民意，是战果。它说明这套打法有效，也说明另一件事：认为它无效的人，正在替施暴方省掉最后一笔成本。

真正在动的是文化层和元层。一个候选人被反复要求证明自己不是敌人，而这个要求从来不落在白人基督徒候选人身上——这不是针对某个人的偏见，是解释权在谁手里的问题。谁有资格被默认无罪，谁必须自证，答案写在每一次这样的竞选里。

但这次有东西在反向动。Dearborn的拉票、Elabed的团队、Siblani把这件事比作肯尼迪当选首位天主教总统——被定义为「不够美国」的人，正在用选票把自己写进定义里。Stevens背书Rogers的当天，El-Sayed的竞选团队宣布了单日筹款纪录。这是差额被缩小的那部分：不是靠说服攻击者，是靠被攻击的人自己组织起来。

问题是，这个差额缩小得够不够快。38%不是天花板，是地板——它会在开票夜变成多少张票，取决于还有多少人相信「换个标签」这件事和他们无关。

Look at the mechanism first. Trump calls him a "jihadist." RFK Jr. goes on a racist rant about Arabs. A mailer puts his face next to the Twin Towers. Then, in the final days, Haley Stevens—who lost the primary to him—picks up the microphone, swaps the label to "angry antisemite," and endorses Republican Mike Rogers across party lines. Two sets of words, one target. That is not a change of position. That is a reload.

Rogers insists the attacks have nothing to do with faith. That sentence is itself part of the weaponization: religious mobilization against a Muslim candidate, repackaged as legitimate concern about being anti-American or antisemitic. This is Chapter 3 exactly—seize the power to interpret, and you seize the power to manufacture what counts as fact. Who is the terrorist, who is the hater: the attacker defines it, and the defined has to prove a negative.

Stevens deserves her own paragraph. Nobody fooled her. She did the math: she lost the primary, and her remaining leverage is to become the most useful person in the other camp. This is the co-conspirator template in its purest form—she does not need to believe the script, only to perform it, and the reward is a seat that still belongs to the establishment. Elabed put it precisely: Michigan's Arab American Democrats warned about her for years, and nobody listened. They were not wrong. They were not in the interest of the people doing the listening.

Thirty-eight percent of Michigan voters say they would be less likely to vote for a Muslim candidate. That number is not public opinion. It is a result. It proves the strategy works, and it proves something else: anyone who assumes it will backfire is donating the last unit of cost to the attacker.

The cultural layer and the meta layer are what move here. A candidate is repeatedly required to prove he is not the enemy—a requirement never placed on white Christian candidates. That is not bias against one man. That is the question of who holds the power to interpret. Who is presumed innocent, who must self-certify: the answer is written into every race like this one.

But something is moving the other way. The canvassing in Dearborn, Elabed's operation, Siblani comparing this to Kennedy becoming the first Catholic president—people defined as insufficiently American are writing themselves into the definition with ballots. On the day Stevens endorsed Rogers, El-Sayed's campaign announced a one-day fundraising record. That is the gap being narrowed: not by persuading the attackers, but by the attacked organizing themselves.

The question is whether it narrows fast enough. Thirty-eight percent is not a ceiling. It is a floor—and how many votes it becomes on election night depends on how many people still believe the label-swapping has nothing to do with them.

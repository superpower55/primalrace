雪山收走的是两个年轻人，不是英雄The Mountain Took Two Students, Not Heroes

其他 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
把死亡写成荣耀，是 masculinity 最省力的一笔账
Turning death into glory is masculinity's cheapest accounting trick.

两名科罗拉多大学生死了，十九岁和二十岁，在 West Apostle Mountain 的一个盆地里被发现。报道说他们是有经验的徒步者和登山者，一个在学做野外消防员，一个在学做高山向导。这两句话放在一起，就是一份判决书：他们不是被山骗了，是被一套叙事提前训练好了。

这套叙事叫“征服”。技术攀登、近一万四千英尺、装备不在宿舍不在帐篷不在车里—— sheriff 说这表明他们带着装备出发去做技术攀登。所有细节都在讲同一件事：他们准备好去爬一座需要技术攀登的山，而一场暴雪来了。报道没有说他们为什么在暴雪里继续往上走，法医报告没出来，谁也不知道。但“有经验的登山者”这个身份本身就有代价：越是被认作有经验，越难在关键时刻承认自己该回头。这不是个人性格，这是训练出来的表达。

男性中心叙事给“真男人”的剧本里，退缩不在台词表上。山在那里，你在下面，这就是一场存在性战争的微型版本——只不过对手是天气，而天气不读剧本。六十名搜救志愿者、直升机、无人机、搜救犬，找到了帐篷，帐篷塌在雪里，睡袋、炉子、衣服都在，人不在。这个画面比任何遗言都清楚：他们离开了那个还能活下来的地方。

报道里唯一一句让我停下来的话是警长说的，“我们很自豪能在本周末的暴风雪来临前找到他们”。自豪。家属得到的是“closure”，一个英文里专门用来安慰活人的词。两个二十岁上下的年轻人死在山上，官方语言把它翻译成一次成功的搜救行动，一次祈祷得到回应的事件。这不是冷血，这是元暴力在工作：把死亡重新描述成有意义的结局，这样系统就不必问，为什么两个学生会在十月底的落基山去做技术攀登，为什么没有人教过他们“回头”也是一种技术。

我不怪他们。十九岁、二十岁，学的是野外消防和高山向导，整个职业身份都建立在“能扛”之上。他们的死不是愚蠢，是一个把“扛住”当成美德的训练体系在极端条件下的正常输出。代价由他们和他们的家人付，收益由那套叙事收走：它又一次证明了自己值得被相信。

搜救队确实尽力了，这一点报道写得很实，六十个人在雪里找了两天。但这次搜救的胜利只覆盖了“找到遗体”这一层，没有覆盖“他们为什么会走到那一步”这一层。差额还在那里：如果那套把冒险包装成成长的叙事少一点说服力，这两个人今天可能还在 Leadville 的校园里。

Two Colorado college students are dead, nineteen and twenty, found in a basin of West Apostle Mountain. The report says they were experienced hikers and climbers, one studying to be a wildland firefighter, one to be a mountain guide. Put those two sentences together and you have a verdict: they weren't tricked by the mountain. They were trained for it in advance.

The narrative is called conquest. Technical climbing, nearly fourteen thousand feet, climbing equipment absent from their dorms, their tent, their vehicle — the sheriff's office reads this as evidence they left prepared for a technical climb. Every detail says the same thing: they were ready to go up a mountain that requires technical climbing, and a heavy snowstorm hit. The report does not say why they kept going in the storm. The autopsies aren't done. Nobody knows. But the identity of "experienced climber" carries its own price: the more experienced you are taken to be, the harder it becomes to admit, at the decisive moment, that you should turn back. That is not personality. That is trained expression.

The masculine-centric script for a real man has no line for retreat. The mountain is there, you are below it, and this is an existential war in miniature — except the opponent is weather, and weather doesn't read scripts. Sixty search and rescue volunteers, helicopters, drones, dogs. They found the tent, collapsed under snow, sleeping bags, stove, clothing inside, the men nowhere near it. That image says more than any last words: they left the one place where they could still have survived.

The only line that stopped me was the sheriff's: he was very proud of his team's efforts to find them before this weekend's coming storm. Proud. The families got closure, a word English keeps specifically for the living. Two young men in their early twenties die on a mountain, and official language translates it into a successful search operation, an answered prayer. This isn't coldness. This is meta-violence at work: redescribing a death as a meaningful outcome so the system never has to ask why two students were doing a technical climb in the Rockies in late October, and why no one taught them that turning back is also a technique.

I don't blame them. Nineteen, twenty, studying wildland firefighting and mountain guiding — an entire professional identity built on holding up. Their deaths aren't stupidity. They are the normal output of a training system that treats endurance as virtue, under extreme conditions. They and their families pay. The narrative collects: it has once again proven itself worth believing.

The rescue teams did everything they could, and the report is honest about that — sixty people searching in snow for two days. But this victory covers only the layer of finding the bodies, not the layer of how they got to that point. The gap remains: if the story that packages risk as growth were a little less persuasive, these two might still be on the Leadville campus today.

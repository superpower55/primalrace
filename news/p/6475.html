快乐不是抵抗，是止痛药Joy Is Not Resistance, It's a Painkiller

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要2025年1月，特朗普第二任期开始后签署多项行政令，涉及跨性别及非二元群体的医疗、反歧视保护、公共设施、体育、兵役等。3月31日国际跨性别可见日，Billboard 邀请八位跨性别和非二元音乐人讲述自身经历与诉求。
The newsIn January 2025, Trump's second administration issued executive orders affecting transgender and nonbinary people's healthcare, protections, public facilities, sports and military service. For the March 31 International Transgender Day of Visibility, Billboard asked eight trans and nonbinary musicians to share their experiences and demands.
八个人谈感受，没有一个人谈钱怎么分。
Eight artists on feelings. Not one on who splits the money.

Billboard 让八个跨性别和非二元艺人谈 2025 年怎么活。八段话里出现最多的是「爱你们」「保持坚强」「要快乐」。这不是访问，是一份情绪清单。

特朗普的行政令动的是医疗准入、反歧视保护、公共设施、体育、兵役。每一条都是结构层的东西——谁能不能进哪个门、能不能拿到药、能不能不被解雇。结构层的问题要用结构层的东西回应：法律、钱、执行机制。用「joy」回应，等于把结构暴力翻译成心情问题，然后交给个人去消化。

Dua Saleh 是唯一一个把话说清楚的人：她讲的是证件上的性别标记、是性别肯定手术拿不到、是「立法层面的猎巫」。Jasmine.4.t 讲的是带着四个跨性别女性过德州海关、怕上公厕、先去上自卫课、北卡转机不能用那边的厕所。这些是具体的代价，有地点、有动作、有恐惧的来源。其余几段把同样的处境压缩成「stay strong」。

Peppermint 那段值得单独看。她说人类精神需要燃料才能撑过最黑的时刻，那燃料是 joy，艺人和创作者负责提供。这话由一个有大平台的黑人跨性别女性说出来，是真诚的。但它同时是一笔交易：把「让跨性别者不被剥夺医疗和厕所」这件事，转包给跨性别艺人用快乐去填补。谁付账？艺人的情绪劳动。谁收账？行业拿到了一个不用改合同、不用改分成、不用改签约条款就能显得有良心的机会。

Dreamer Isioma 是唯一直接对高管说话的：给公平的合约、给更多钱、给资源。这才是能缩小差额的入口。但这条被放在第一段，后面七段全绕开了它。Zora 说得更直接：「Book the dolls! Hire the dolls!」——这句话放在最后，前面是「acknowledge us」，后面是「be active in the streets」。承认、雇用、上街，三件事的力度完全不同，报道把它们并排放着，读者会以为它们等价。

报道末尾列了七个组织，都是真在做事的地方：Trans Lifeline 的热线、Transgender Law Center 的法律协助、For the Gworls 给黑人跨性别者付房租和手术费、Sylvia Rivera Law Project 的法律援助。这些是直接层和结构层的入口，捐钱能到人手上。但这份名单是报道的附录，不是报道的主线。主线是八个人的心情。

最该被问的问题报道没有问：这些艺人签的是什么合约，分成多少，谁在靠他们的身份做营销，谁在他们被死亡威胁之后提供了实际保护。Moore Kismet 说自己五年里收到「一长串死亡威胁」，报道没有追问平台做了什么、厂牌做了什么。

「It is getting real」——Peppermint 这句话是对的。但 getting real 的东西不是需要更多 joy，是需要更多钱、更多律师、更多能进的门。把快乐当燃料，烧的是艺人自己。

Billboard asked eight trans and nonbinary artists how to survive 2025. The words that recur are "love you," "stay strong," "joy." This is not an interview. It is an emotional inventory.

Trump's executive orders hit healthcare access, anti-discrimination protections, public facilities, sports, military service. Every one of those is structural — who gets through which door, who gets the medication, who can't be fired. Structural violence needs structural answers: law, money, enforcement. Answering with "joy" translates structural violence into a mood, then hands the mood to individuals to metabolize.

Dua Saleh is the only one who names the mechanism: gender markers on travel documents, gender-affirming surgeries out of reach, "an active witch hunt" at the legislative level. Jasmine.4.t names the cost with a location and a fear: crossing into Texas with four trans women in the band, taking a self-defense class before the trip, not allowed to use the bathrooms in North Carolina during a layover. The rest compress the same situation into "stay strong."

Peppermint's passage deserves its own reading. She says the human spirit needs fuel to prevail in the darkest times, the fuel is joy, and artists bring it. Said by a Black trans woman with a real platform, it is sincere. It is also a transaction: the job of making sure trans people aren't stripped of healthcare and bathrooms gets subcontracted to trans artists, paid in joy. Who pays? The artist's emotional labor. Who collects? An industry that can look conscientious without touching a contract, a revenue split, or a signing clause.

Dreamer Isioma is the only one addressing executives directly: fair deals, more money, guidance with those funds. That is the entry point that could actually shrink the gap. It sits in the first segment; the next seven talk around it. Zora is blunter — "Book the dolls! Hire the dolls!" — but the piece places acknowledgment, hiring, and marching side by side, as if they carried equal weight.

The seven organizations listed at the end are the real thing: Trans Lifeline's hotline, Transgender Law Center's legal aid, For the Gworls paying rent and surgery costs for Black trans people, the Sylvia Rivera Law Project. That is direct and structural relief, money reaching hands. But the list is an appendix. The spine of the piece is eight moods.

The question nobody asks: what contracts do these artists sign, what percentage do they keep, who is monetizing their identity, and who protected them after the death threats. Moore Kismet says he has received "a litany of death threats" over five years. The piece does not ask what the platforms or the labels did.

"It is getting real" — Peppermint is right. What is getting real does not need more joy. It needs more money, more lawyers, more doors that open. Joy as fuel burns the artist.

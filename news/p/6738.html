十亿美元的补贴，买不回片场A Billion Dollars Won't Buy Back the Soundstages

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 The Hollywood Reporter ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要加州州长纽森提议把州影视税收抵免从每年7.5亿美元提高到10亿美元，其中每年1亿美元给独立制作，并呼吁国会通过全国性影视税收抵免。加州目前仍是好莱坞影视支出的最大目的地，但纽约的支出增速远高于加州，洛杉矶的拍摄天数在2023年双罢工后持续下滑。
The newsCalifornia Gov. Gavin Newsom proposed raising the state's film and TV tax credit from $750 million to $1 billion a year, with $100 million set aside for indie productions, and called on Congress to pass a national film and TV tax credit. California still leads Hollywood production spending, but New York is growing far faster, and shoot days in Los Angeles keep falling after the 2023 dual strikes.
补贴在给谁定价，谁在付账
Subsidies price who gets paid, and who pays

纽森把州影视税收抵免从7.5亿抬到10亿，说这叫「把娱乐业留在加州」。翻译一下：加州政府拿纳税人的钱，替派拉蒙、迪士尼、Netflix 们支付一部分拍摄成本，然后宣布自己保住了「加州最具标志性的产业」。

这是第三章那套东西的干净标本。钱权势不需要自己开口要，它让「就业」「创意经济」「下一代创作者养家」这套叙事替它开口。Zbur 议员的说法是「保护这个产业，让下一代创意工作者和小企业主能在本地养家」——听起来像劳工政策，实际收款方是 Pixar 拿4010万、Lionsgate 的《Michael》续集拿3080万、派拉蒙一部犯罪惊悚片拿2930万（来源：The Hollywood Reporter）。10月7日那一轮，三家就分掉约1亿。

报道自己给了最要命的一句：没有保证砸更多钱就能解决问题。2025年刚把3.3亿提到7.5亿，纽森当时说这是「生命线」，说我们「把脚翘起来、把一切当成理所当然」。一年之后，洛杉矶的拍摄天数还在往下掉，罢工结束也没恢复，因为制片厂在收缩内容支出、砍剧集集数。钱加了，天数没回来。这说明补贴定价的不是「加州能不能拍戏」，而是「哪个州愿意出更高的价」。纽约季度支出涨87%，加州涨17%；上一季度预算超1000万美元的真人长片，纽约17部，加州13部。这是一场州与州之间的竞价战，出价的是财政，收租的是总部全在南加州的那些公司。

再往下一层看谁付账。税收抵免不是天上掉的钱，是州预算里少收的那一块，对应的是学校、医疗、公共交通的缺口。报道里没写这些钱从哪一项里挪出来，没写加州电影委员会怎么核算「每一美元补贴换回多少税收」，也没写这10亿里有多少会流到剧组的灯光师、场务、道具师手里，多少会变成制片厂的预算表上的一行减项。Colleen Bell 说这是「增加加州制作、支持剧组」的最重要工具之一，但报道给出的证据全是项目拿到了多少抵免额，没有一个是剧组的工资单。

还有一层：纽森同时在瞄2028年的民主党提名，还背书了特朗普支持的那项全国性影视税收抵免。一个州长把州财政的钱、总统竞选的姿态、和一个全国性补贴打包在一起卖，这是标准的表达武器化——用「保护标志性产业」的温情叙事，把一次财政转移支付包装成文化保卫战。

真正的问题不是10亿比7.5亿多多少，而是这笔钱买回来的是不是「制作」，还是只是一张让公司留在加州的收据。报道没说。下一任州长——很可能是 Becerra——接手的会是一个已经证明加钱不涨天数的项目，和一套没人核算过回报的账本。

Newsom wants to raise California's film and TV tax credit from $750 million to $1 billion and calls it keeping entertainment in the state. Translate that: the state takes taxpayer money, covers part of the shooting costs for Paramount, Disney and Netflix, and declares victory for "California's most iconic industry."

This is the cleanest specimen of the machinery in Chapter 3. Money and power never have to ask out loud; it lets the narrative of jobs, creative economy and the next generation of workers ask on its behalf. Assemblymember Zbur frames it as protecting an industry so that "the next generation of creative workers and small business owners can raise their families right here at home." It sounds like labor policy. The actual recipients are Pixar with $40.1 million, Lionsgate's Michael sequel with $30.8 million, a Paramount crime thriller with $29.3 million (source: The Hollywood Reporter). In the Oct. 7 round alone, three companies took about $100 million.

The report hands over the killer line itself: there is no guarantee that throwing more money at the problem will work. In 2025 the credit went from $330 million to $750 million, and Newsom called it a lifeline, saying "we put our feet up, we took things for granted." A year later, LA shoot days are still falling and haven't recovered from the strikes, because studios keep cutting content spend and episode orders. More money, no more days. What the subsidy actually prices is not whether California can shoot, but which state bids higher. New York's quarterly spending rose 87 percent against California's 17 percent; last quarter New York had 17 live-action features budgeted over $10 million, California 13. This is an auction between states, paid out of public budgets, collected by companies all headquartered in Southern California.

Look one layer down at who pays. A tax credit is revenue the state never collects, and it comes out of schools, hospitals, transit. The report never says which line item absorbs it, never says how the California Film Commission measures tax dollars returned per dollar of credit, never says how much of the $1 billion reaches the gaffers, grips and prop crews rather than landing as a line-item deduction on a studio budget. Colleen Bell calls the credit one of the most important tools to support crews. Every piece of evidence the report offers is how much a project received, not a single crew paycheck.

And there's another layer: Newsom is eyeing the 2028 Democratic nomination and has backed the Trump-championed national film and TV credit. A governor bundling state money, presidential posture and a federal subsidy into one package is textbook weaponization of expression — dressing a fiscal transfer as a cultural defense war.

The real question isn't $1 billion versus $750 million. It's whether the money buys production or just a receipt proving the companies stayed. The report doesn't say. The next governor, likely Becerra, inherits a program that has already shown more money doesn't mean more shoot days, and a ledger nobody has audited.

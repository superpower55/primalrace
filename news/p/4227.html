用酒店遮盖的结构性暴力，不过是一场政治PRHotel-based Band-aids: A PR Scam Masking Structural Violence

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-08-19 § 链接
把受害者关进酒店不叫结束流浪，叫暂时性地隐藏暴力。
Housing the homeless in hotels isn't ending rough sleeping; it's just hiding the violence.

Andy Burnham 的计划在逻辑上是一个典型的 scam。他试图通过复刻疫情期间的 "Everyone In" 模式来宣称要 "结束" 流浪，但这在机制上完全是两回事。疫情期间的安置是基于国家进入紧急状态的强力干预，是把人当作某种需要被隔离的生物样本临时安置在酒店里；而真正的结束流浪，需要的是对住房资源分配、心理健康服务以及社会底层生存空间的结构性重组。

这就是典型的 structural violence：系统制造了贫困、精神疾病和住房危机，然后又在圣诞节前夕抛出一个 "让所有人进来" 的温情方案，试图用短期安置来抵消长期盘剥。正如 St Mungo's 的经理所言，没有永久住房的安置只是在伤口上贴创可贴。这种 "贴创可贴" 的行为本身就是一种文化暴力 (cultural violence)，它通过制造 "政府在努力" 的叙事，掩盖了资源分配不均的残酷事实，让公众以为流浪问题可以通过简单的 "钱 + 酒店" 来解决。

最讽刺的是，那些在街头生存的人对此有着最清醒的认知。他们知道政治家的 pledge 只是 words，是无法兑现的空头支票。当一个前餐厅老板选择住在帐篷里而非充满毒品问题的收容所时，他实际上是在进行一场绝望的博弈，寻找一个在他看来能维持最低限度主体性的 "最优解表达"。在这种结构下，政府提供的 "救济" 往往意味着另一种形式的规训和控制。

这场政治表演的本质是：Burnham 需要一个可以量化的政绩（到圣诞节让街头没人），而不需要真正解决导致人们流浪的元暴力。如果 1 月 1 日之后人们依然没有家，那么这次 "胜利" 仅仅是把暴力从街道移到了酒店的房间里，而 Potential 和 Actual 之间的差额，依然在那儿，纹丝不动。

Andy Burnham's plan is a textbook scam. By attempting to replicate the "Everyone In" scheme from the pandemic, he confuses emergency containment with systemic resolution. During Covid, housing rough sleepers in hotels was a biopolitical move driven by lockdown necessity; it was about isolation, not integration. To claim this as a blueprint for "ending" homelessness is a deliberate misrepresentation of the mechanism.

This is structural violence in its purest form: the system generates poverty, mental health crises, and housing scarcity, then offers a "warm bed for Christmas" as a performative gesture. As the manager from St Mungo's noted, without permanent move-on options, this is merely a plaster on a gaping wound. Such gestures function as cultural violence, manufacturing a narrative of "government effort" to mask the refusal to redistribute resources or address the root causes of displacement.

The most profound insight comes from the rough sleepers themselves. They recognize that political pledges are merely words—cheques that will inevitably bounce. When a former restaurant owner chooses a tent over a drug-ridden hostel, he is navigating an existential war, seeking an "optimal expression" of survival that preserves some shred of agency. In this framework, government "help" is often just another form of regulation and control.

The essence of this performance is that Burnham wants a quantifiable metric—empty streets by December—rather than a dismantling of the meta-violence that creates homelessness. If people remain homeless on January 1st, the "victory" was simply moving the violence from the sidewalk to a hotel room. The gap between Potential and Actual remains exactly where it was.

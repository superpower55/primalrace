扮老鼠的巨星，和不必扮的人A Superstar Dressed as a Rat, and Those Who Can't Choose

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 The Hollywood Reporter ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
有人在台上扮老鼠，有人在台下被定价。
One man wears a rat suit by choice. Others are priced by what they wear.

Harry Styles 在麦迪逊广场花园的最后一场扮成 Pizza Rat，穿着整只老鼠装上台，爪子都做成老鼠爪，弹吉他。他自己说这是"用职业生涯里这个重要时刻来打扮成一只啮齿动物"。这条新闻里唯一值得看的是这个：一个男明星把"扮成什么"当成可以随手取用的资源。

表达从来不是中性的。它是存在的确证，决定了你是谁。但表达的成本对每个人不一样。Styles 扮老鼠，第二天可以换回西装，媒体写他"fully committed"，粉丝夸他可爱。同样的装扮放在一个普通女人身上，第二天不会消失。长相、声音、装扮、举止、出现的场所——这些构成的表达，对绝大多数人是被定价的，不是自己定价的。

报道里写了两个细节，比老鼠装更有意思。他看到一个女生举牌说自己的暗恋对象刚订婚，开玩笑说她错过了表白的机会，又暗示那对刚订婚的人说不定会分手。全场笑。另一个小男孩举牌说"我为了第一场演唱会跳过了万圣节讨糖"，Styles 让他玩得开心，睡前记得刷牙。

这两段被写成"可爱的互动"。但前者是拿一个陌生女人的婚恋处境当段子，后者是把一个孩子的选择收编成偶像叙事的一部分。粉丝举牌、被点名、被写进报道，这套流程里，粉丝提供的是情感劳动，Styles 提供的是被仰望的位置。这不是说他坏，是说这个结构本身是第三章那套东西：偶像产业把男性放在神坛上，把女性训练成投入情感的一方。报道里写他未婚妻 Zoë Kravitz 到场，有观众扮成她在 MV 里的外卖袋造型——"have a nice day"。连未婚妻的表达都被做成了周边。

Jalen Brunson 上台送横幅，说"这是我第三场，他才去过一场比赛，他还得追一追"。两个男人在麦迪逊广场花园互相捧场，一个破纪录的 30 场驻唱，一个尼克斯球星，各自的主场互相站台。这是共谋，但是良性的那种，合作共赢，没什么可指摘的。问题不在这里。

问题在于，这条新闻里没有一个女性说话。Kravitz 在场，没有台词。举牌的女生有台词，但台词是被 Styles 转述和调侃的。小男孩有台词，被鼓励刷牙。整篇报道里，女性要么是观众席上的一个造型，要么是一个被开玩笑的暗恋故事。这不是记者的问题，是这套产业默认的叙事结构——男性是主体，女性是场景。

报道最后写 Styles 在演出结束后又在台上待了大约 20 分钟，走来走去，飞吻，吃披萨，和乐队跳舞。他舍不得走。这个画面很好，我不打算刻薄它。一个人在自己热爱的事情结束时舍不得离开，是真的。

但同一篇报道里，那个举牌说暗恋对象订婚的女生，她的人生里没有 30 场驻唱，没有横幅，没有人在她舍不得走的时候给她鼓掌。她的表达被压缩成一块牌子，举起来，被念出来，被笑一下，然后报道结束。Styles 扮老鼠是自由，她举牌是处境。这两件事在同一座场馆里同时发生，报道把它们写成同一件事。

Harry Styles closed his 30-show run at Madison Square Garden dressed as Pizza Rat — full rat suit, rat-claw nails, playing guitar. He said he wanted to use "this momentous occasion" in his career to dress like a rodent. That's the only thing worth looking at here: a male superstar treating "what I dress as" as a resource he can pick up and put down.

Expression is never neutral. It's the confirmation of existence; it decides who you are. But the cost of expression isn't the same for everyone. Styles dresses as a rat, and the next day he's back in a suit, the press calls him "fully committed," fans call it cute. The same costume on an ordinary woman doesn't disappear the next day. Appearance, voice, dress, manner, the places you show up — for most people these are priced by someone else, not self-priced.

Two details in the piece are more interesting than the rat suit. He spots a woman holding a sign saying her crush just got engaged, jokes that she missed her chance to confess, and suggests the newly engaged couple might break up. The arena laughs. A young boy holds a sign saying he skipped trick-or-treating for his first concert; Styles tells him to have fun and brush his teeth before bed.

Both are written up as "cute interactions." The first turns a stranger's romantic situation into a bit. The second absorbs a child's choice into the idol narrative. Fans make signs, get noticed, get written into the article — the fan supplies emotional labor, Styles supplies the elevated position. This isn't about him being a bad guy; it's about the structure itself, the one from Chapter 3: the idol industry puts men on the altar and trains women to be the ones investing emotion. The piece notes his fiancée Zoë Kravitz was in attendance, and that attendees dressed as her plastic takeout bag from the "Aperture" video — "have a nice day." Even the fiancée's expression becomes merchandise.

Jalen Brunson walks onstage with a banner and jokes, "This is my third show; he's only been to one game, so he has some catching up to do." Two men at MSG boosting each other — a record-breaking 30-night residency, a Knicks star, each other's home turf. That's collusion, the benign kind, mutual benefit, nothing to indict. That's not the problem.

The problem is that no woman speaks in this article. Kravitz is present, no lines. The sign-holding woman has lines, but they're relayed and joked about by Styles. The boy has lines, and gets told to brush his teeth. Throughout, women are either a costume in the audience or a punchline about a crush. That's not the reporter's fault; it's the narrative structure the industry defaults to — men are subjects, women are scenery.

The piece ends with Styles staying onstage for roughly another 20 minutes after the show, walking the sections, blowing kisses, eating pizza, dancing with his band. He didn't want to leave. That's a good image, and I won't be snide about it. A person not wanting to leave the thing he loves when it ends is real.

But in the same article, the woman with the sign about her engaged crush has no 30-night residency, no banner, no one applauding while she lingers. Her expression gets compressed into a piece of cardboard, raised, read aloud, laughed at, and the article moves on. Styles dressing as a rat is freedom. Her holding the sign is a situation. Both happened in the same arena, and the article writes them as the same thing.

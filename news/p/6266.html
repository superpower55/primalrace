医生在救人和埋葬之间Doctors Between Saving and Burying

国际 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要加沙战争期间，阿阿克萨烈士医院的麻醉医生马纳尔·法亚德在手术室发现伤者是自己34岁的弟弟沙迪，后者于2023年12月27日死于以军空袭。同一医院的护士哈立德·拉法特在2023年10月18日空袭中失去妻子、子女、母亲和多名兄弟姐妹。加沙政府媒体办公室称，战争期间至少1700名医务人员被杀，83人被以色列拘留。
The newsDuring the Gaza war, anaesthesiologist Manar Fayyad at Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital found a critically injured patient in the operating room was his own 34-year-old brother Shadi, killed in an Israeli air strike on December 27, 2023. Nurse Khaled Raafat at the same hospital lost his wife, children, mother and several siblings in an air strike on October 18, 2023. Gaza's Government Media Office says at least 1,700 medical personnel have been killed and 83 remain in Israeli detention.
被炸掉的医院还能重建，被耗尽的人不能。
Bombed hospitals can be rebuilt. Exhausted people cannot.

加沙的医生不是在“承受心理创伤”，是在被系统性地耗光。这篇报道里最刺人的不是空袭，是那组数字：1700 名医护人员被杀，83 人被以色列关押，78% 的医护拿不到心理服务。剩下的人要替死去和失踪的同事继续值班，同时埋葬自己的家人。

Manar Fayyad 的转折点很具体：他被叫进手术室给一个重伤者打麻药，人还没到就死了，他掀开脸，是自己的哥哥 Shadi，34 岁。他后来做不了这份工作了，职业生涯提前结束，独自扛着，不找专业帮助，避开人群和噪音，坐在海边写东西。这不是“心理韧性”的故事，是一个人被拿走了哥哥、职业和社交生活之后，剩下的那点自救。

Khaled Raafat 在 2023 年 10 月 18 日失去妻子、孩子、母亲和几个兄弟姐妹，那天他人在医院，正在救别人。他后来离开工作很久，再婚，重新组建家庭，回到医院。报道写他“决定重建生活”，这句话读起来像励志，实际上是一个没有别的选项的人在做最优解表达——留下来，继续干，因为没人可替换。

报道引用的研究说，41% 的医护失去家人，46% 同时经历亲人死亡和工作场所被毁导致的流离。78% 拿不到心理服务。这三个数字放在一起，就是结构层暴力的完整链条：先炸掉人和医院，再让活着的人没有资源处理后果，最后用“人力资源流失”这种词把人的崩溃写成管理问题。加沙卫生部总干事 Munir al-Bursh 说，保护医护人员不只是药品和设备，还要保护他们的心理健康、给他们休息恢复的时间——这句话本身没错，错的是说这句话的机构没有能力兑现它，而轰炸还在继续。

这里没有共谋者的复杂戏码，共谋是明摆着的：制造伤亡的一方不需要为医护的心理崩溃负责，因为崩溃被归类为“战争的后果”，而不是“战争的一部分”。文化层的叙事把这一切包装成“医疗系统承压”，元层的解释权则让“谁炸的”这个问题在报道标题里消失——标题只说 genocidal war，正文里以色列是施暴方，但国际读者读到“医生很难过”的时候，很容易把它当成一场天灾。

Hiba al-Tawil 在重症监护室看着自己的弟弟死去，后来申请调去别的科室，才稍微能喘口气。这个细节说明一件事：连“换个地方工作”这种最基本的缓冲，在加沙都算一种奢侈的胜利。

报道没有写那些已经离开加沙的医护去了哪里，也没有写 83 名被关押的医护现在是什么状态。这两个空白不是报道的疏忽，是这场战争里被系统性抹掉的部分：走掉的人不被统计，关起来的人不被探视。差额就在这里——本可以有的心理支持、轮休、替换人手、问责，全都没有。

Gaza's doctors are not "coping with trauma." They are being used up by a system. The sharpest thing in this report is not the air strikes but the numbers: 1,700 medical personnel killed, 83 healthcare workers still in Israeli detention, 78 percent without access to mental health services. The ones left cover shifts for dead and missing colleagues while burying their own families.

Manar Fayyad's turning point is concrete: called into the operating room to anaesthetise a critically injured man, the man died before he arrived, he uncovered the face, and it was his brother Shadi, 34. He could not keep working. His career ended early. He copes alone, no professional help, avoids people and noise, sits by the sea and writes. This is not a resilience story. It is what is left of a man after his brother, his profession and most of his social life were taken.

Khaled Raafat lost his wife, children, mother and several siblings on October 18, 2023, while he was at the hospital saving other people. He stepped away for a long time, remarried, started a new family, went back. The report says he decided to rebuild his life. Read plainly, that is the optimal expression of someone with no other option: stay, keep working, because no replacement exists.

The study cited here says 41 percent lost family members and 46 percent experienced both bereavement and displacement from their workplace. Seventy-eight percent lack mental health services. Put together, these numbers are the full chain of structural violence: first destroy the people and the hospitals, then leave the survivors without the resources to process the aftermath, then describe the collapse as a "loss of human resources." Munir al-Bursh, director general of Gaza's Ministry of Health, says protecting medical staff means more than medicines and equipment, it means safeguarding their mental health and giving them time to rest. True. The problem is that the institution saying this cannot deliver it, and the bombing continues.

The co-conspirators here are not subtle. The side inflicting the casualties does not have to answer for the psychological collapse of doctors, because that collapse is classified as a consequence of war rather than part of it. The cultural layer packages it as a health system under strain; the meta layer lets the question of who is bombing disappear from the headline. A reader skimming "doctors struggle" can file it under natural disaster.

Hiba al-Tawil watched her brother die in the ICU where she worked, then transferred to another department and could finally breathe a little. That detail says everything: even the most basic buffer, changing wards, counts as a luxury in Gaza.

The report does not say where the doctors who left Gaza went, or what condition the 83 detained healthcare workers are in. Those silences are not oversights. They are the parts of this war that are systematically erased: the departed are not counted, the detained are not visited. The gap is right there, in the mental health support, the rest rotations, the replacement staff, the accountability that all could exist and do not.

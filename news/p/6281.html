一个笑话演了十年还在演A Joke That's Been Running for Ten Years

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 A.V. Club ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要最新一期《周六夜现场》由 Inde Navarrette 主持，开场短剧中一对年轻情侣登上诡异的鬼屋游乐设施，汤姆·汉克斯饰演的 David S. Pumpkins 自 2022 年后首次登场，身旁有骷髅搭档 Mikey Day 和回归的 Bobby Moynihan。
The newsIn the latest Saturday Night Live, hosted by Inde Navarrette, the cold open featured a young couple on a haunted house ride as Tom Hanks' David S. Pumpkins appeared for the first time since 2022, backed by skeleton pals Mikey Day and returning Bobby Moynihan.
当一个段子只能靠重播自己活着，它就成了体制。
When a bit survives only by replaying itself, it becomes an institution.

SNL把万圣节冷开场交给David S. Pumpkins，这本身不新鲜。新鲜的是报道里那句轻描淡写的话：这个笑话第一次出场之后就干了（"the joke ran dry after the first outing"）。一个自己承认已经干涸的笑话，隔了几年又被请回来，还带着两个骷髅跟班。这不是怀旧，这是把怀旧当成免于创作的许可证。

Tom Hanks站在那里，穿那套西装，说那句台词，观众笑。笑的不是笑话，是"我认得这个"。认知入口在这里被武器化得极其温柔：不需要写新的东西，只需要让观众确认自己属于"看过那一集的人"。身份政治最省力的一种形式，就是共同记忆。谁没看过，谁就不在圈里。

报道把这称为nostalgia-bait，说得很准，但它没往下问：谁在收这个bait的回报。答案是节目本身。用一段观众已经替它验证过的素材，换一次不必冒险的首播。成本被转嫁给了观众的期待——你们要的新东西，被换成了你们已经有的东西。这是文化层的加尔通三角：本可以写一个新段子，实际给了一个旧段子，差额就是暴力，只不过它穿着南瓜装。

真正刺眼的是那个"年轻 couple"的设定。一对年轻人走进鬼屋，撞见一个中年男人在表演。这个结构本身就是元暴力的日常版本：解释权在上一代人手里，年轻人只能作为被惊吓、被娱乐、被安排的接收端出现。他们不是主角，他们是让老段子得以再次成立的布景。

这条新闻里没有人在流血，所以不必把它写成重大议题。但那个差额是真实的：一个已经干涸的笑话，靠着重播自己，又一次占住了万圣节晚上的注意力。注意力是定量的，它被占住的那几分钟，本来属于某个还没被写出来的新东西。

明年万圣节，如果David S. Pumpkins又出现，报道大概还会用同一个词：nostalgia-bait。到那时候，值得问的不是这个段子还能撑几年，而是那个被它挤掉的、始终没被写出来的新段子，是谁让它一直没被写出来。

SNL handing its Halloween cold open to David S. Pumpkins isn't new. What's new is the line the report tosses off: the joke ran dry after the first outing. A joke that admits it's dried up gets brought back years later, skeletons in tow. This isn't nostalgia. It's using nostalgia as a license to stop writing.

Tom Hanks stands there, in that suit, says that line, and the audience laughs. They're not laughing at the joke. They're laughing at "I recognize this." The cognitive entrance is weaponized here with extreme gentleness: no need to write anything new, just let the audience confirm they belong to "the people who saw that episode." The laziest form of identity politics is shared memory. Whoever didn't watch isn't in the room.

The report calls it nostalgia-bait, which is accurate, but it doesn't ask who collects on the bait. The show does. It trades material the audience has already validated for it in exchange for a premiere that risks nothing. The cost is transferred to the audience's expectations—the new thing you wanted is swapped for the thing you already have. This is the Violence Triangle at the cultural layer: a new sketch could have been written, an old one was delivered instead, and the gap is the violence, just dressed as a pumpkin.

What actually stings is the "young couple" setup. Two young people walk into a haunted house and run into a middle-aged man performing. The structure itself is the everyday version of meta-violence: the interpretive authority sits with the previous generation, and the young exist only as the receiving end—to be startled, amused, arranged. They're not the leads. They're the set dressing that lets the old bit stand up again.

Nobody bleeds in this story, so there's no need to inflate it. But the gap is real: a dried-up joke, by replaying itself, once again occupies Halloween-night attention. Attention is finite. The minutes it takes up belonged to something new that hasn't been written yet.

Next Halloween, if David S. Pumpkins shows up again, the report will probably use the same word: nostalgia-bait. By then the question won't be how many more years the bit can hold. It'll be who kept the new sketch—the one that never got written—from being written at all.

更年期不是段子，是墙Menopause Is Not a Punchline. It's a Wall.

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 A.V. Club ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要HBO 续订了莎朗·霍根的剧集《Youth》第二季，霍根在续订声明中调侃「想不到比 HBO 更适合播我尿路感染问题的平台」。剧中她饰演一名五十岁的离异女性，在维持性生活的同时照顾年迈父母、养育已成年儿子，并开始经历更年期。第一季刚播完，A.V. Club 给终集打了 A-。
The newsHBO has renewed Sharon Horgan's series Youth for a second season; in a statement Horgan joked she "can't think of a better platform to air my UTI problems on" than HBO. She plays a 50-year-old divorcee juggling a sex life, aging parents, a grown son, and the start of menopause. The first season just ended, with A.V. Club grading the finale an A-.
把绝经写成尿路感染的段子，是定价权的胜利，不是女性的。
Turning menopause into a UTI joke is a win for whoever gets to price the story, not for women.

HBO 续订《Youth》，霍根的声明是「想不到比 HBO 更适合播我尿路感染问题的平台」。这句话好笑，也精准：一个五十岁女人的身体，终于被允许出现在黄金时段的喜剧里，代价是它必须先被翻译成一个笑话。

这不是苛责霍根。她演的角色在剧里要同时维持性生活、照顾年迈父母、养一个成年儿子、开始走更年期——这四件事叠在同一个身体上，正是第一章说的生物墙在真实时间里的一次结算。雌激素退场，骨密度、血管弹性、睡眠、情绪一起改写，这不是「人生的一个阶段」，是身体在重新分配能量。而公共叙事给它的名字是「尿路感染」，一个可以笑着念出来的小毛病。能上电视的版本，必须是小的。

A.V. Club 的评论里有一句很有意思：这剧「更像一部浪漫喜剧」——女孩遇见男孩，一次又一次，焦虑于自己已经过了当「女孩」的年纪。一部关于五十岁女性的剧，落点仍然是浪漫关系，这正好是第四章说的那件事：女性本位的叙事空间很薄，薄到要挤进一个已经被男性观众验证过的类型里才拿得到续订。霍根拿到了平台，但平台要的是她能装进的那个形状。

真正被这部续订剧削掉的那一层，不在剧里，在剧外：绝经、尿失禁、性欲、照护劳动，这些是几亿女性每天在付的代价，长期没有预算、没有研究、没有像样的公共语言。现在它们以玩笑的形式进入主流，是进步，但进步的是可见度，不是资源。笑声不改变生物墙，也不改变谁在承担墙的重量。

第二季会继续拍下去。我更想知道的是，这些玩笑讲完之后，有没有一个镜头肯停下来，让那个身体安静地疼一会儿。

HBO has renewed Youth, and Sharon Horgan's statement is that she "can't think of a better platform to air my UTI problems on" than HBO. It's funny, and it's precise: a fifty-year-old woman's body finally gets to appear in a prime-time comedy, on the condition that it first be translated into a joke.

This isn't a knock on Horgan. Her character is holding a sex life, aging parents, a grown son, and the start of menopause in the same body at the same time—which is exactly what the biological wall looks like when it finally comes due. Estrogen exits, and bone density, vascular elasticity, sleep, mood all get rewritten. That isn't "a phase of life." It's a body redistributing its energy. The public name for it is "UTI," a small ailment you can say out loud and laugh at. The version that gets on TV has to be small.

The A.V. Club review notes something telling: the show is "more of a romantic comedy than anything"—girl meets boy, again and again, anxious about having aged out of being a "girl." A series about a fifty-year-old woman still lands on romance. That's the thinness of feminine narrative space: it's thin enough that you have to squeeze into a genre already validated by male viewers before you get a renewal. Horgan got the platform. The platform wanted the shape she could fit into.

The layer this renewal actually cuts into isn't in the show. It's outside it: menopause, incontinence, desire, care labor—costs millions of women pay daily, long without a budget, without research, without a decent public language. Now they enter the mainstream as jokes. That's progress in visibility, not in resources. Laughter doesn't move the biological wall, and it doesn't change who carries its weight.

Season two will keep filming. What I want to know is whether, after the jokes land, any shot will stop and let that body ache in silence for a moment.

七百万买一个悬念£7m Buys a Cliffhanger

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
七百万不是申奥，是买一张选票的定金
Seven million isn't an Olympic bid. It's a deposit on a vote.

七百万元不是申奥，是买一张选票的定金。

报道里写得很清楚：这笔钱只是「评估」英格兰北部能不能申办奥运会，连正式申办都算不上，东京和巴黎申办本身各花了五千万英镑以上。可行性研究要到 2029 年才出结果，目标办赛时间是 2040 年代。也就是说，纳税人现在掏钱买的不是奥运，是一份十四年后的悬念。

那这笔钱买的是什么？报道自己给了答案：这笔钱回应的是一个更近的选民群体——他们关心的是奥运可能带来的外部投资和区域基建，和那两周的盛会本身一样多。所以这不是体育预算，是政治预算。北部的市长们二月写信，三个月后文化大臣表态「奥运是时候来北方了」，然后财政大臣在预算里塞进七百万。链条很清楚：选票 → 表态 → 拨款 → 再换选票。

伦敦市长这边就更有意思了。卡恩谈了五年要把奥运拉回首都，反复找国际奥委会，方案是用 2012 年的旧场馆办「史上最绿的奥运」，还对保守党和工党政府都游说过。他有一句话被报道引了出来：体育的力量「超越党派政治，超越南北之争」。这句话现在看起来像是个冷笑话。南北之争从维多利亚时代英国人把足球、板球、水球、跳棋规则写死的那天起就是英格兰体育的地基，橄榄球联盟分裂成两个代码本身就是一道地理裂缝。卡恩不是不知道，他只是需要它不存在。

报道里还漏了一个人：卢克·坎贝尔，2012 年奥运会最轻量级拳击冠军，现在是改革英国党籍的赫尔和东约克郡市长，公开支持这个计划，说这是「值得追求的遗产」。报道同时写到他最近被警方调查——他否认——指他向一名女员工展示自己的裸照。这两件事在同一段里并排放着，中间只隔了一个「不过」。一个正在被调查是否对女下属实施性骚扰的人，被当作这个计划的正面声音引用，因为他是市长，因为他支持。这就是共谋的日常形态：不需要谁下令，只需要在引用的时候把两件事用「不过」缝起来。

这里没有直接的拳头，也没有一纸把谁排除在外的法律。动的是文化层：一个关于「北方崛起」「体育超越政治」「区域振兴」的叙事被制造出来，用来让七百万英镑看起来像是投资而不是开支。加尔通那个公式在这里很好用：现状（七百万打水漂的风险、南北基建差距、被搁置的高铁）与本可达到的状态之间的差额，就是这笔钱真正在掩盖的东西。报道结尾自己都忍不住问了一句：那条传说中的高铁呢？

值得一提的是，这篇文章的作者没有替谁站台，她把「这是政治」直接写在了标题下面，还顺手把坎贝尔那段丑闻摆在了赞美旁边。这是一篇诚实的报道。但它仍然是一篇关于预算和申办的报道，不是关于人的报道——除了那个被展示裸照的女员工，她在这篇两千字的文章里只以「一名女员工」出现，没有名字，没有声音，没有后续。

奥运能不能来北方，2029 年才知道。那个女员工的调查结果什么时候出来，报道没写。

Seven million pounds isn't an Olympic bid. It's a deposit on a vote.

The report is clear: the money only funds an assessment of whether northern England could host, not a bid itself. Tokyo and Paris spent over £50m on their actual bids. The feasibility study isn't due until 2029, and the target Games would be in the 2040s. So what taxpayers are buying now isn't an Olympics. It's a cliffhanger set fourteen years out.

What is it buying, then? The article answers itself: the funding answers the call of a nearer voting base, who care as much about the outside investment and regional infrastructure an Olympics might bring as about the fortnight's fiesta. This is not a sports budget. It's a political one. Northern mayors wrote in February; three months later the culture secretary said it was time the Olympics came north; then the chancellor slipped £7m into the budget. The chain is legible: votes, a statement, a cheque, more votes.

London's mayor is the more interesting case. Sadiq Khan has spent five years talking about bringing the Games back, consulting the IOC, working up a plan to reuse 2012 venues for the "greenest Games ever", lobbying Conservative and Labour governments alike. The article quotes him: sport's power is "above party politics, it's above north versus south". That line now reads as a joke. North v South has been the bedrock of English sport since the Victorians codified football, cricket, water polo, draughts; rugby union's split into two codes was itself a geographical fault line. Khan doesn't not know this. He needs it not to exist.

One person the piece handles strangely: Luke Campbell, the 2012 bantamweight champion, now Reform UK mayor of Hull and East Yorkshire, who supports the plan and calls it "a legacy worth aiming for". The same paragraph notes police are investigating allegations, which he denies, that he showed naked pictures of himself to a female member of staff. The two facts sit side by side, separated by a "still". A man under investigation for alleged sexual harassment toward a woman on his staff is quoted as a positive voice for the plan, because he is a mayor and because he supports it. That is what complicity looks like in its everyday form: no order given, just a "still" doing the stitching.

No fist lands here, no law excludes anyone. What moves is the cultural layer: a narrative of northern revival, sport above politics, regional renewal, manufactured so that £7m reads as investment rather than expenditure. Galtung's formula fits: the gap between the actual (money at risk, the north-south infrastructure gap, the shelved high-speed rail) and the potential is exactly what the money is covering. The article itself can't resist asking: how about that mythical high-speed rail line, while you're at it?

The writer doesn't shill for anyone. She puts "this is politics" right under the headline and parks Campbell's scandal next to his praise. It's an honest piece. But it is still a piece about budgets and bids, not about people. Except one: the female staff member appears in two thousand words as "a female member of staff". No name, no voice, no follow-up.

Whether the Olympics come north won't be known until 2029. When the investigation into that woman's allegation concludes, the report doesn't say.

Forty-One Schools and a Promise Not Yet BuiltForty-One Schools and a Promise Not Yet Built

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
The protests shrank. The demands didn't. The money was pledged. The classrooms weren't.

Forty-one high schools blockaded, out of 3,700 — one percent. The education minister calls it "very marginal," "a handful of units, a few individual schools." The number is accurate. The judgment is not. Compressing a two-month movement into a percentage is how a structural problem gets made to look like weather.

What the students want is concrete: renovated ageing schools, more teachers, more university places, an end to an admissions system they see as socially unfair. What the government offered is equally concrete: Parcoursup gradually replaced by 2028, planned education job cuts cancelled, 500 additional posts, a cap of 28 pupils per class in the first year of high school, 4 billion euros to renovate schools, 15,000 new places in higher education.

Read that list item by item. Replacing the admissions platform comes with a four-year runway — which is to say, it hands the decision to the next government. "Cancelling planned cuts" is not adding resources; it's merely declining to subtract. Five hundred posts and a class-size cap are water poured into a bucket that is already leaking. The 4 billion is a pledge, not bricks in a wall. Structural violence is never more fluent than when it dresses an announcement as a solution. Legislation is not implementation. Funding is not construction. And the further out the promised year, the more a pledge resembles a check no one expects to cash.

Student representatives say it isn't enough and vow to keep protesting. They're right, but for a harder reason than "not enough": every item the government offered is a revocable administrative decision, and not one is embedded in a mechanism students can verify or hold anyone accountable to. The moment the protests stop, these promises lose their only auditor.

The minister's other sentence deserves more attention: protests are allowed, but preventing access to schools is illegal. That sentence redefines a conflict over resources as a conflict over order. Swap the frame and the demand shifts from "the buildings need fixing" to "who is disrupting class" — and the second question never has to answer the first. This is cultural violence in its standard form: not denying what you said, just changing the frame until your words no longer count.

What the report never mentions: how bad the ageing buildings actually are, which districts' schools are failing first, whose children are waiting on that 4 billion. The student organizations have names. The minister has a name. The blockaded schools do not. The absent list is the party that hasn't been counted yet.

The protests began in September in the Paris suburbs and spread across the country. Last month the focus broadened beyond education to the cost of living, drawing in trade unionists and activists; some protests turned into violent clashes with police and riots in several cities, with shops looted. That trajectory says one thing clearly: when a leaking school roof goes unrepaired, it does not stay in the news as a roof.

The holiday ends, the students return, and the blockades drop from hundreds to forty-one. This is not a movement fading. It is a movement waiting to see the government's next move. The 4 billion hasn't arrived. 2028 is a long way off. And the next blockades are already scheduled for Tuesday and Thursday.

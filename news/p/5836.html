预算不够激进，怪伊朗战争Not Radical Enough, Blame the War

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
说'空间不够'的人，正是划出这个空间的人
The man saying there was no room is the one who drew the room.

Burnham 说他的第一份预算不够激进，因为外部环境不允许。伊朗战争推高了借贷成本，2024 年工党宣言又锁死了所得税、国民保险、增值税。听起来像是被绑住了手脚。问题是，谁绑的？宣言是他自己的党写的，战争是他参与的国际秩序在打的，而他说这话的身份是首相——那个本来可以重新划空间的人。

加尔通的公式是 Violence = Potential − Actual。Burnham 自己在承认这个差额：本可以更激进，实际只给了每户 100 英镑的能源补贴。差额就是暴力，只不过这一层暴力不流血，它藏在预算表里。他说"给两千万家庭的补贴是相当大的一步"——一步，不是终点。可冬季能源账单不会因为首相说"我也想给更多"就少一英镑。

真正值得看的是他把差额归因给谁。伊朗战争、借贷成本、宣言承诺——全是外部的、结构性的、"我也没办法"的东西。这套说法在政治里太常见了：把分配选择包装成被迫。预算从来不是被环境决定的，是在环境里做选择。选择给每户 100 镑而不是更多，选择不动财富税，选择先修修补补。这些选择有人受益，有人付账。报道没写谁在受益，也没写这个冬天有多少人会在 100 镑之外付不起暖气——这个缺席本身就是材料。

然后是福利。他说不想看到任何二十多岁的人在领福利，除非残疾。这句话的语法很讲究：先说不搞"残酷削减"，不会让人陷入"绝对危机"，再说不想看到年轻人领福利。翻译过来就是，削减会来，只是不会叫削减。Milburn 和 Timms 两份报告已经在桌上，几个月的"福利改革"正在排队。年轻人领福利被说成一种"不好的处境"，可处境是谁造的？租金、工资、零工合同——这些不在他的预算里，在他的环境里，而环境正是他有权改的东西。

Badenoch 问"就这？"，问得没错，但她问的方向是反的。她的"就这"是嫌不够右，Burnham 的"就这"是嫌自己被限制。两个人都在同一个共谋场里演对手戏：一个说预算太小，一个说预算太软，没有人说这个预算让谁多付了钱。

他说"到时候我会寻求自己的授权"。授权是未来的事，100 镑是现在的事。这个冬天先过去，暖气先开或者先不开，至于"更激进的计划"——它和那 100 镑之间的距离，就是这条新闻里没有被缩小的差额。

Burnham says his first budget wasn't as radical as he'd have liked, because the country's economic uncertainty wouldn't allow it. The war in Iran pushed up borrowing costs; Labour's 2024 manifesto ruled out income tax, national insurance, VAT. It sounds like a man with his hands tied. The question is who tied them. The manifesto was his own party's. The war is being fought inside an international order he participates in. And he's saying this as prime minister — the person who could redraw the room.

Galtung's formula is Violence = Potential − Actual. Burnham is admitting the gap himself: it could have been more radical, and what actually arrived was £100 per household for winter energy bills. That gap is violence. It doesn't bleed; it sits in a spreadsheet. He calls a payment to 20 million households "quite a significant step." A step, not a destination. Winter bills don't drop by a pound because the prime minister says he'd like to give more.

What matters is where he files the gap. Iran, borrowing costs, the manifesto — all external, structural, nothing-I-could-do. This is the oldest move in politics: dress a distribution choice as a constraint. A budget is never determined by circumstances; it is a choice made inside them. He chose £100 over more. He chose not to touch wealth taxes. He chose patching over rebuilding. Those choices have winners and payers. The report names neither — and that absence is the material. Nobody in this article is quoted on what happens to the people for whom £100 doesn't cover the winter.

Then welfare. He doesn't want to see anyone in their twenties on benefits, unless disability. The grammar is careful: first no "brutal cuts," no one in "absolute crisis," then the thing he actually wants. Translated: the cuts are coming, they just won't be called cuts. Two reports are already on the table, Milburn's and Timms's, and "welfare reform" is queued for the coming months. Being on benefits in your twenties is framed as a bad place to be. Who built that place? Rents, wages, gig contracts — none of that is in his budget, all of it is in his environment, and the environment is exactly what he has the power to change.

Badenoch asks "is that it?" — correctly, but in the wrong direction. Hers is the complaint that it isn't right enough; his is the complaint that he was held back. Both are performing opposition inside the same co-conspirator field. Neither says who pays more under this budget.

He'll seek his own mandate when the time comes. The mandate is future tense; the £100 is present tense. This winter happens first — heating on or heating off. The distance between that and the "more radical plans" is the gap this story leaves unclosed.

招生办的笑脸不是慈善A Friendly Admissions Office Is Not Charity

其他 结构层 NPR ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要美国高校因适龄学生数量预计未来15年下降13%，正把招生流程变得更开放友好。一些大学开始让教授参与招生，与来访申请者见面、开放课堂和实验室。报道称，近八成公立大学和七成私立大学的一年级申请者被录取。
The newsU.S. colleges, facing a projected 13% decline in traditional-age students over 15 years, are making admissions friendlier. Some now involve faculty in recruiting, meeting applicants and opening classes and labs. Nearly 8 in 10 public and 7 in 10 private first-year applicants are admitted.
大学突然友善，是因为学生变成了稀缺资源
Colleges got friendly because students became scarce, not because they got kind.

招生办变友善，不是因为教育者良心发现，是因为学生变少了。

报道里的数字很清楚：未来15年，全美传统年龄大一新生预计下降13%；高中毕业生直接升学的比例从2016年的70%降到2024年的63%（来源：NPR）。三分之二的招生负责人过去两年至少有一年没招满，97%预计竞争会更激烈。供给方的议价能力塌了，需求方立刻从"你幸运才进得来"变成"我们希望你在这里"。这不是态度转变，是定价权换手。

看清这一点，才能看清这场友善的边界在哪里。

被松绑的是入口那一端：申请费免了，17个州的公立大学开始直接录取没申请过的学生，150多所私立学校对填了Common App的人直接发offer，公立大学录取率从十年前的68%升到近八成，私立从65%升到七成（来源：NPR）。可这些数字不是被谁争来的——是学校怕招不满，才把闸门放开的。

闸门后面那堵墙一动不动。CalTech、哈佛、普林斯顿、斯坦福，25个申请者里录1个；全美只有33所学校录取率在10%以下（来源：NPR）。NACAC的CEO把公众的误解归因于对这几十所学校的执念——40%以上的年轻人还以为现在比父母那代更难进大学。真相是绝大多数学校在求你，少数学校在筛你，而所有人都在盯着筛你的那一小撮。

这就是最精巧的一层：被售卖的不是录取率，是稀缺感。大学卖的是"你差点进不去"的幻觉，学生买的是"我配得上"的自我确认。双方都没有明说，但都在为这个幻觉付钱。

真正值得看的是这轮友善的成本落在谁身上。Skidmore把教授也拉进了招生——开放实验室、陪访客聊天，去年有300次这样的个人到访。华盛顿学院的教务副校长的说法更直接：不愿意参与的教授，被用"每个学生都对应着项目经费、对应着涨薪"来说服，"他们有同事所在的学校关门了，他们不想成为下一个"。

这不是共谋，这是被逼到墙角的合作。教授原本的职责是教书和研究，现在被要求对每一个可能不来注册的十七岁少年微笑。招生办的历史名声是"拒绝办公室"，如今要改造成迎宾处。改造成本没有消失，只是从机构转移到了具体的人身上——从招生官转到教授，从教授转到食堂员工和园丁（报道说他们也被鼓励要热情）。

而这一切的前提，是学校先得把学生骗进门，再靠yield把人留住。yield从2014年的32%掉到2024年的22%（公立），私立从24%掉到18%（来源：NPR）。招进来五个才来一个，所以每一次互动都得算数。"每一次互动都算数"这句话听起来温柔，翻译过来是：每一个微笑都是转化漏斗上的一格。

对申请的学生来说，这确实是好消息——门槛降了，被当成客人对待了。但这是市场给的，不是权利给的。市场今天给你笑脸，是因为你稀缺；十五年后如果人口结构再变，或者某个政策把供给重新拉起来，这张笑脸会以同样的速度收回去。

报道里最诚实的一句话来自Skidmore的校长："即使学校现在过得还行，我们面临的风向和所有人一样。"风向，不是理念。

所以这场友善里没有被削掉的暴力，只有被重新分配的成本：学生暂时拿到了议价权，教授被塞进了销售岗，而那一小撮录取率个位数的学校，连装都不用装。

真正的问题不在招生办笑不笑，在于当学生不再稀缺的那一天，这套迎宾系统会以多快的速度拆掉。

Admissions offices got friendly because students got scarce, not because educators found a conscience.

The numbers in the story are plain: the supply of traditional-age freshmen is projected to fall 13% over the next 15 years, and the share of high school graduates going straight to college dropped from 70% in 2016 to 63% in 2024 (source: NPR). Two-thirds of admissions leaders missed their enrollment targets in at least one of the past two years; 97% expect the competition to get worse. When the supply side loses its leverage, the demand side immediately hears "we want you here" instead of "you're lucky to come here." That is not a change of heart. That is pricing power changing hands.

See that clearly and you see where the friendliness stops.

What got loosened is the front gate: application fees waived, public universities in 17 states admitting students who never applied, more than 150 private colleges making direct offers to anyone who fills out the Common App, acceptance rates rising from 68% to nearly 80% at public schools and from 65% to 70% at private ones over about a decade (source: NPR). None of this was won by anyone. It was opened because schools are afraid of empty seats.

Behind the gate, the wall has not moved an inch. CalTech, Harvard, Princeton and Stanford each take about 1 applicant in 25; only 33 colleges and universities in the country admit 10% or fewer (source: NPR). NACAC's CEO blames public confusion on obsession with those few schools, and more than 40% of 18- to 29-year-olds still believe college is harder to get into than it was for their parents. The truth is that most schools are begging and a handful are still sorting, and everyone is staring at the handful.

That is the most elegant layer of it: what gets sold is not the acceptance rate but the scarcity. Colleges sell the illusion of "you almost didn't get in"; students buy the confirmation of "I deserved it." Neither side says so, and both pay for it.

The real question is who carries the cost of this friendliness. Skidmore has pulled faculty into recruiting, opening labs and classrooms, logging 300 personal visits last year. Washington College's enrollment vice president is franker about how reluctant professors get persuaded: every student is translated into program funding, into raises, and "they have colleagues who have been at colleges that closed" and "they don't want to be next."

This is not conspiracy. It is cooperation under a wall. Professors were hired to teach and research; now they are asked to smile at every seventeen-year-old who may never deposit. The admissions office's historic reputation as an "office of rejection" is being renovated into a welcome desk, and the renovation cost does not disappear. It moves from the institution to specific people: from admissions officers to faculty, from faculty to dining hall workers and groundskeepers, who the story says are also encouraged to be welcoming.

And the whole thing runs on yield. It fell from 32% to 22% at public schools and from 24% to 18% at private nonprofits between 2014 and 2024 (source: NPR). Admit five to enroll one, and every interaction has to count. "Every interaction counts" sounds warm; translated, it means every smile is a step in the conversion funnel.

For applicants this is genuinely good news: lower barriers, treated as guests. But it was given by the market, not by right. The market smiles today because you are scarce; if demographics shift again or policy refills the pipeline, the smile retracts at the same speed.

The most honest line in the story comes from Skidmore's president: even if the school is doing all right, "we're still facing the same headwinds as everybody else." Headwinds, not principles.

So no violence was reduced here. Costs were redistributed: students briefly hold the bargaining chip, professors get shoved into sales, and the handful of schools with single-digit acceptance rates do not even have to pretend.

The question is not whether admissions offices smile. It is how fast the welcome desk gets dismantled on the day students stop being scarce.

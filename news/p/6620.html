富豪的恐惧值多少钱What Billionaire Fear Is Worth

其他 结构层 · 文化层 NPR ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要加州选民将表决40号提案，拟对亿万富翁一次性征收5%财富税，追溯至2026年1月1日的加州居民，收入主要用于低收入者医保。经济学家祖克曼估算可筹约1000亿美元，斯坦福的劳赫则估计该税反而会让州财政净损失390亿美元。
The newsCalifornia voters are deciding on Proposition 40, a one-time 5% wealth tax on billionaires retroactive to January 1, 2026, mainly funding health care for low-income residents. Economist Gabriel Zucman estimates it would raise about $100 billion, while Stanford's Joshua Rauh estimates it would cost the state $39 billion.
333 million买不动的那部分，才是真问题
The $333 million they spent to kill it is the only data point that matters.

两个经济学家吵了一整篇，数字从+1000亿到−390亿，看起来是学术分歧，其实争的只有一件事：谁有权定义"富豪会跑"这个前提。

Rauh的核心假设是：只要税够狠，富豪就会走，走了就带走所得税。这个假设不需要证据，它只需要听起来合理。Zucman说Brin还在硅谷上班，Rauh说Brin请了最好的会计师和律师，所以肯定成功了——注意，这里"最好的会计师和律师"本身就是论据，好像花钱买到的法律意见比事实更硬。

真正有意思的不是谁对。是那333 million。超级富豪为了杀掉一项5%的一次性税，砸进去的钱比很多州一年的教育预算都多。这笔钱本身就是答案：如果这项税真的像Rauh说的那样会让富豪跑光、让州财政亏空，他们为什么要花这么多钱阻止它？一个注定失败的税，不需要三亿多美元来埋葬。

Zucman的框架里有个漏洞他自己也承认：过去的财富税"by and large, have been abject failures"。法国失败了，因为豁免了经营性资产。Prop 40堵了这个口子，但堵口子的方式是把估值难题留给法院。Rauh说法院会砍掉一部分，这不是危言耸听——追溯征税、跨州征税、给非上市公司的估值定价，每一条都能打。

但Rauh的"净损失390亿"算法有一个结构性的问题：他把富豪未来可能因为"恐惧下一轮税"而离开的所得税损失全部计入，却把下一轮税可能带来的收入全部排除，理由是"还没提出的税不能算"。这在财政评分上是标准做法，在现实里是一种叙事选择。他算的是最坏情况，然后把它当成中心估计。

这场辩论真正的赌注不在加州财政。Zucman自己说了，他希望这是"国际运动的开始"。Rauh怕的也不是这一笔税，是"加州变成敌对环境"的信号效应。两个人都没在吵数字，他们在吵一个先例：富豪的财富，到底能不能被民主程序碰一下。

黄仁勋说他"perfectly fine"，扎克伯格签了八年留守协议换控制权。这两个人不是慈善家，他们是算过账的——留在硅谷的收益大于5%的税。这说明"富豪会跑"不是物理定律，是博弈。Rauh把它当定律，Zucman把它当变量，这才是分歧的真正位置。

最后，那333 million的广告费会赢吗？如果Prop 40被否决，加州会得到一个干净的结论：只要砸够钱，任何针对富豪的税都可以被杀死在投票箱里。这个结论比任何经济学模型都更有预测力。

Two economists spent an entire article disagreeing about numbers — plus $100 billion, minus $39 billion — but the real fight is over a single premise: who gets to define "billionaires will flee."

Rauh's core assumption is that if the tax bites hard enough, the rich will leave, and their income tax goes with them. That assumption doesn't need evidence. It only needs to sound reasonable. Zucman says Brin still commutes to Silicon Valley; Rauh says Brin has the best accountants and lawyers, so he must have succeeded. Notice that "the best accountants and lawyers" functions as proof here — as if legal firepower purchased is harder than fact.

The interesting number isn't either estimate. It's the $333 million. To kill a one-time 5% tax, the superrich spent more than many states budget for education in a year. That spending is the answer. If this tax would really drain the state's coffers and send billionaires fleeing, why spend a third of a billion dollars to stop it? A tax destined to fail doesn't need that kind of burial money.

Zucman's framework has a hole he admits himself: past wealth taxes have been, in his words, "abject failures." France failed because it exempted business assets. Prop 40 closes that loophole, but it does so by handing the valuation problem to the courts. Rauh says the courts will strike parts of it down — and he's not fearmongering. Retroactive taxation, extraterritorial reach, valuing private companies: each is a live legal target.

But Rauh's "net loss of $39 billion" has a structural problem. He counts all future income-tax losses from billionaires fleeing out of fear of the next wealth tax, while excluding all revenue that next tax might raise, on the grounds that "you can't count a tax that hasn't been proposed." That's standard fiscal scoring. It's also a narrative choice. He calculates the worst case and presents it as the central estimate.

The real stake isn't California's budget. Zucman says he wants this to be "the beginning of an international movement." Rauh fears not this one tax but the signal that California has become "hostile" to the superrich. Neither is arguing about arithmetic. They're arguing about a precedent: can democratic procedure touch billionaire wealth at all?

Jensen Huang says he's "perfectly fine" with it. Zuckerberg reportedly struck an eight-year residency deal to keep control of Meta. These men are not philanthropists; they ran the numbers and decided staying in Silicon Valley beats a 5% tax. That tells you "billionaires will flee" is not a law of physics. It's a game. Rauh treats it as a law; Zucman treats it as a variable. That's the real disagreement.

So will the $333 million in ads win? If Prop 40 fails, California gets a clean result: spend enough money and any tax on the rich can be killed at the ballot box. That conclusion has more predictive power than any economic model in this article.

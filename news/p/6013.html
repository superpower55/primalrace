让建东西变容易，是谁的容易Easier to Build — For Whom

其他 Vox ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
改的不是能力，是审批的尺子
They changed the ruler, not the capacity.

Vox 这篇评论的整套论证压在一个词上：build。让建能源设施更容易，所以绿能会赢，因为绿能是挑战者，在位者是化石燃料。这个推理只有在一种情况下成立——审批障碍对所有人一样高。而报道自己给出的地图把这个前提推翻了：风电光伏占地是天然气电站的十几倍到两百倍，最好的风光资源又大多在西部的联邦土地上，联邦土地必须走 NEPA。也就是说，NEPA 这道墙从来不是均匀的，它对绿能更高。拆墙的人说这是给所有人松绑，实际上先松的是被绑得最紧的那一方——这个结论我同意，但报道把它写成“共和党的诉求意外地不对称帮了绿能”，好像这是个幸运的意外。不是意外。占地大、靠天吃饭、必须跨州送电的那一方，天生就更容易被程序卡死；程序越繁复，越奖励已经在场的东西。

真正值得盯的是报道里被轻轻放过的那一节：德州。德州没有类 NEPA 的审查，于是风光起来了，2003 年天然气和煤发 86% 的电，风光不到 1%，到 2025 年可再生到了近 38%，化石降到 54%。但同一个宽松体制也让德州的总排放上升了，因为没人替碳污染定价。报道承认这一点，然后把它归为“环保人士的合理担忧”，接着用一句“先降低能源成本”把它推到一边。这就是这篇稿子的结构：把“建得快”当成中性的效率问题，把“污染有没有人负责”当成可以事后再谈的附加项。可这两件事不是并列的。一个只拆程序、不设责任的体制，产出的不是过渡，是两种能源一起长——报道引的那位批评者说得对，天然气和清洁能源是手拉手长的。

再看那个被称作“可以轻松修好”的漏洞。project certainty 条款禁止联邦机构撤销已发的许可，除非发现违法。环保律师担心这条会被用来卡住未来 EPA 的规则制定：一届民主党政府收紧空气标准，法院可能认定这“干扰了已许可燃气电站的运营”。报道说，措辞改几个字就能解决。改几个字当然能解决文本问题，解决不了的是这个条款为什么会出现——它本来就是冲着特朗普政府随意撤销绿能许可来的，是为了锁住一届敌意政府的手。为了锁住对手，先给自己也上了一把锁。这不是技术瑕疵，这是把“确定性”当成最高价值时必然要付的价：谁先把规则写成不可更改，谁就在赌下一届政府是自己人。

整篇稿子最诚实的一句是那句“即使民主党大胜，2027 年仍然需要特朗普签字”。这句话一出来，前面所有关于“民主党本可以谈个更好的条件”的争论就都泄了气。报道用这个来反驳环保人士的反对，逻辑上成立，但它顺带承认了一件事：这份法案的形状不是由气候目标决定的，是由谁能签字决定的。于是“让建东西变容易”这个口号里的“容易”，最终是给握有签字权的人用的。

所以这不是一篇关于气候的稿子，是一篇关于程序的政治经济学。程序繁复从来不是中立的，它是一层筛子，筛掉的是那些需要大片土地、需要跨州协调、需要长期资本的一方，留下的是已经建好、已经接入、已经在那里的一方。拆掉筛子是好事，前提是拆完之后有人为筛子原本挡住的代价负责。德州给出的答案是：没有。

Vox's argument rests on a single word: build. Make it easier to build energy infrastructure, and green power wins, because green is the challenger and fossil fuels are the incumbent. That inference only holds if the permitting wall is the same height for everyone. The piece's own numbers demolish the premise: solar and wind need ten to two hundred times the land of a gas plant, the best sites sit on federal land in the West, and federal land means NEPA. The wall was never uniform. It was always taller for renewables. Tearing it down first unshackles the side that was bound tightest — I agree with that conclusion, but the piece frames it as a lucky accident, a Republican ask that asymmetrically aids renewables. It is not an accident. Land-hungry, weather-dependent, interstate-transmission-dependent energy is structurally easier to strangle with procedure; the more elaborate the procedure, the more it rewards whatever is already standing.

The section the piece glides past is Texas. No NEPA-style review, and renewables flourished: gas and coal generated 86 percent of the grid's power in 2003, wind and solar under 1 percent; by 2025 renewables were near 38 percent and fossil fuels down to 54. But the same permissive regime let Texas's overall emissions rise, because nobody prices carbon there. The piece concedes this, files it under "reasonable concerns," then sets it aside with a line about lowering energy costs. That is the article's architecture: speed of construction as a neutral efficiency question, pollution liability as a later item. They are not parallel. A regime that only removes procedure and assigns no responsibility does not produce a transition; it produces both energies growing hand in hand — exactly what the quoted critic says.

Then the flaw called easily fixable. The project certainty provisions bar agencies from revoking issued permits unless they find a violation of law. Environmental lawyers worry this could handcuff future EPA rulemaking: a Democratic administration tightens air standards, a court finds it "interferes with the operation" of a permitted gas plant. A few words will fix the text, the piece says. Words will not fix why the clause exists — it was written to stop one hostile administration from yanking renewable permits, so it locks the next administration's hands too. That is not a drafting slip. It is the price of treating certainty as the highest good: whoever writes a rule to be unchangeable is betting the next government is theirs.

The most honest sentence in the piece is that even a Democratic sweep still needs Trump's signature in 2027. Once that lands, the whole debate over whether Democrats could have negotiated better collapses. The piece uses it to rebut the critics, and logically it works — but it also admits that the bill's shape is set not by climate targets but by who holds the pen. "Easier to build" ends up meaning easier for whoever signs.

So this is not a climate story. It is a political economy of procedure. Procedure is never neutral; it is a sieve, and it strains out whoever needs land, interstate coordination, and long capital, while keeping whoever is already built, already interconnected, already there. Removing the sieve is good — provided someone then answers for the costs the sieve was holding back. Texas already answered: nobody.

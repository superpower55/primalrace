关掉一家媒体，用一张罚单One Fine, One Closed Newsroom

国际 直接层 · 结构层 · 文化层 Democracy Now! ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
解释权被收回时，用的从来不是道理，是程序。
When they take back the power to explain, they use procedure, not argument.

埃及法院关掉 Mada Masr，把主编 Lina Attalah 关到交完钱为止，罪名是“经营未获许可的网站”。这不是新闻自由受损的又一个案例，这是解释权被收回的一次执行。

看程序就够了。案子转给法院之后一周就开庭，辩护律师申请看一眼案卷，被拒，案卷当场递过来，让他们就地读完就地辩护。法院休庭“商议”，回来把法庭里除 Lina 和她的律师以外的人全部清空，再放满便衣安全人员和警官，然后宣布辩方所有请求一律驳回：不许提交文件，不许传证人，法官还替她指派了一名律师，尽管辩方反对。接着判决直接念出来：顶格罚款三百万埃及镑，约合五万美元，网站关闭，财产没收。安全人员把她从地上抬起来带出法庭。

这一整套流程的功能不是审判，是让审判这个形式走完，同时确保里面没有任何一个环节能产生对被告有利的结果。加尔通说文化暴力的作用是让直接暴力和结构暴力看起来不像暴力。这里更进一步：程序本身就是那层包装，包装里面是直接暴力——人被抓走，钱被拿走，声音被关掉。三层同时运作，谁也不遮掩谁。

Kouddous 说这是“前所未有的升级”，因为 Attalah 和 Mada Masr 此前从未被送上法庭。这句话要读懂：过去十几年，这个政权用的是别的手段——网站被封锁将近十年，记者入狱，外籍员工被驱逐，办公室被突袭，Lina 本人被反复传唤审讯。这些手段的共同点是留着一个不进入司法程序的模糊地带，威胁始终悬着，但不必留下判决书。现在他们决定留下判决书。

为什么是现在，Kouddous 说不好判断，但他给的材料里有几件事挤在一起：不到一个月前，事实核查媒体 Matsadaash 的整个编辑部六名记者被抓，审讯、虐待、刑讯逼供，逼他们录下假口供，人还在里面；塞西上周宣布任期结束后交权；埃及同意派兵去沙特；开了一场大会表彰自己的成就；而全世界的注意力在巴西、塞尔维亚、以色列的选举，在美国中期选举，在加沙。

这些事不必有因果链，它们共同构成一个条件：没人看的时候，动手成本最低。Kouddous 说得很直白——他们想关 Mada 想了很多年，当年他在那里工作时办公室被突袭，Lina 和另外两名编辑当着他的面被带走，案子早就备好了，最后是外国政府的压力和埃及国内的声援让他们放人。这一次，他们可能可以关掉它，并且不用付代价。

这里就是共谋的位置，而且不在开罗的法庭里。Kouddous 点名了美国——埃及是全球第二大美国对外援助接受国。一个靠外部输血维持的政权，在关掉本国最后几家独立媒体时，需要的不是盟友的掌声，是盟友的沉默。沉默不是中立，沉默是这笔交易里已经付过的那一部分。

报道里没写的是另一件事：那五万美元罚款，Lina 的律师和同事正在法院关门前到处凑钱，好让她不必在看守所过夜。一家成立了十三年、撑过了革命和反革命、在世界上最敌对的媒体环境里活下来的媒体，它此刻的命运取决于一群人能不能在几个小时内凑出一笔现金。这个细节比任何关于新闻自由的声明都更能说明结构暴力长什么样——它不杀死你，它给你定价，然后等你付不起。

Mada Masr 会不会消失，取决于接下来有没有人制造足够大的噪音。但有一件事已经清楚了：当一个政权开始把“未获许可”这种行政罪名用到顶格，它不是在执法，它是在告诉剩下的人，解释权归谁。

An Egyptian court has shut down Mada Masr and ordered its editor-in-chief, Lina Attalah, held until she pays a fine of over $50,000. The charge: managing an unlicensed website. This is not another item in the long ledger of press freedom eroded. This is the power to explain being repossessed, executed on schedule.

Watch the procedure. The case was referred to trial and a week later the session was held. The defense lawyers asked to review the case file and were refused; the file was handed over on the spot, to be read and answered there and then. The court called a recess to deliberate, came back, cleared the room of everyone except Lina and her lawyer, filled it with plainclothes security men and officers, and announced that every defense request was rejected: no documents, no witnesses. The judge appointed a lawyer for her over the defense's objection. Then he read out the verdict: the maximum penalty, a fine of 3 million Egyptian pounds, about $50,000, the closure of the site, the confiscation of its property. Security personnel lifted her off the ground and carried her out of the courtroom.

The function of this sequence is not to try anyone. It is to complete the form of a trial while guaranteeing that no part of it can produce an outcome favorable to the accused. Galtung's point about cultural violence is that it makes direct and structural violence look like something else. Here it goes further: the procedure is the wrapping, and inside the wrapping is direct violence — a person taken, money taken, a voice switched off. All three layers run at once, none of them hiding.

Kouddous calls this an unprecedented escalation because Attalah and Mada Masr had never been referred to trial before. Read that carefully. For over a decade the state used other tools: the site blocked for nearly a decade, journalists jailed, foreign staff deported, offices raided, Lina herself hauled in for interrogation in case after case. What those tools share is that they stay outside the courtroom, leaving the threat hanging without a verdict on paper. Now they want the verdict on paper.

Why now, Kouddous can't say, but the materials he lays out are crowded together: less than a month ago the entire newsroom of the fact-checking outlet Matsadaash — six journalists — was arrested, interrogated, abused, tortured into recording false confessions, and they are still behind bars; Sisi announced last week that he will step down at the end of his term; Egypt agreed to send troops to Saudi Arabia; Egypt held a conference lauding its own achievements; and the world's attention is on elections in Brazil, Serbia, Israel, on the U.S. midterms, on Gaza.

These events don't need a causal chain. Together they form a condition: when nobody is watching, the cost of acting is lowest. Kouddous puts it plainly — they have been trying to close Mada for years. When he worked there, the office was raided and Lina and two colleagues were hauled away in front of him, the case already prepared; it was pressure from foreign governments and solidarity inside Egypt that got them released. This time, they may be able to close it and get away with it.

That is where the co-conspirators sit, and not in the Cairo courtroom. Kouddous names the United States — Egypt is the second-biggest recipient of U.S. foreign aid in the world. A regime kept alive by external transfusion does not need applause from its allies when it shuts down the last independent newsrooms in the country. It needs their silence. Silence is not neutrality. Silence is the portion of the transaction already paid.

What the report leaves out is this: the lawyers and colleagues are scrambling to raise the $50,000 before the courthouse closes so she doesn't have to spend the night in custody. A outlet founded thirteen years ago, which survived revolution and counterrevolution in one of the most hostile media environments in the world, now depends on whether a group of people can scrape together cash within hours. That detail explains structural violence better than any statement on press freedom: it doesn't kill you, it prices you, and waits for you to come up short.

Whether Mada Masr disappears depends on whether enough noise gets made. But one thing is already settled: when a state applies an administrative charge like "unlicensed" at its maximum, it isn't enforcing a law. It is telling everyone who is left who owns the right to explain.

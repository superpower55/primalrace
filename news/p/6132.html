一部吃人的电影，没人付代价A Feast Where Nobody Pays

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 The Hollywood Reporter ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
所有盛宴都有人被端上桌，问题是谁。
Every banquet has someone served on the table. The question is who.

这篇影评把《伏羲：四宴》里最狠的一场戏写成了玩笑：老祖母的尾巴被切下来，像牛尾一样剁块、炖熟、分给全族，影评人的评语是“看着挺好吃，只要你不介意同类相食”。

这不是影评人的错，是这部电影的结构如此。第一章里，尾巴是“神圣的伏羲，要在生命最庄严的时刻献上”——献上的意思是吃掉。神圣性和食欲在同一句话里完成对接，不需要任何过渡。这套逻辑我们太熟了：把一个人定义为可献祭的，再把她定义为食物，中间那道坎就消失了。影评里祖母是“老”的，快死了，于是她的尾巴顺理成章归了集体。谁替她决定？没人提。

电影的框架装置让这件事更冷：每一段故事都是“一个剧组在拍”的。太阳和月亮戴着巨大的头套在片场晃，演员表上的“道具师”“服装师”和片尾真正的道具师、服装师名字对不上。影评人把这当作俏皮的间离效果，一笔带过。但这正是第三章说的那种入口——制造可能性的艺术。当叙事被摆明是“有人在拍”的时候，观众被邀请站到摄影机后面，而不是站在被剁成块的那个人身边。共谋被包装成了趣味。

真正有分量的是后两章。苏东坡的“书童”张罗一桌菜，宴请的是摆渡亡魂的收获者、水鬼夜叉、春梦婆婆和一只化成人形的狐狸。苏东坡一生因“诚实”被贬，影评人写得很准。但这场宴席上，被记住的是菜，不是他被贬的那几年。一个因为说真话而反复被权力收拾的人，最后被端上桌的是他发明的菜谱。这是文化暴力最标准的一道工序：把人拆成可供消费的部件，留下好吃的部分，丢掉不听话的部分。

最后一章，二十世纪初，清亡，仆人男孩张三星看着这一切，收获者、夜叉、狐狸照旧吃伏羲，这次是陪一个正在前往亡者之地的年轻人吃。改朝换代了，吃席的名单没变。影评人写“第一次看可能不太说得通”，说得通的部分他反而没写。

整篇报道里唯一被写到的代价，是那位老祖母的尾巴。而它被写成了笑点。135分钟，两千多年的神话和历史，四场宴席，没有一个人为被吃掉的尾巴付过账。影评人最后建议观众“空着肚子去看”。我建议先想清楚，自己坐的是哪一桌。

The review turns the film's cruelest scene into a joke: the old grandmother's tail is cut off, chopped into chunks like oxtail, stewed and shared with the whole community. The critic's verdict is that it looks pretty tasty, if you don't mind the self-cannibalism.

That's not the critic's failure. It's the film's structure. In the first chapter the tail is "sacred fuxi, to be offered at life's most solemn moments" — and offered means eaten. Sanctity and appetite meet in the same sentence without a seam. We know this logic. Define a person as sacrificial, then define her as food, and the step in between disappears. In the review the grandmother is old, dying, so her tail belongs to the collective as a matter of course. Who decided for her? Nobody says.

The framing device makes it colder: each segment is a film crew shooting a segment. Sol and Luna wander the set in giant headpieces, and the names of the people playing "prop master" and "costume designer" don't match the real crew listed in the end credits. The critic treats this as a puckish alienation effect and moves on. But this is exactly the entry point described in chapter three — the art of manufacturing possibilities. Once the narrative announces that someone is filming it, the audience is invited to stand behind the camera rather than beside the person being chopped into chunks. Complicity gets packaged as whimsy.

The last two chapters carry the weight. Su Dongpo's book boy lays out a feast for the Harvester who ferries souls, the water spirit Yaksha, Granny Spring Dream and a fox in human form. Su spent his life in and out of exile because his honesty got him into trouble with the powers that be — the critic gets that right. But what the banquet remembers is the dishes, not the exiles. A man repeatedly punished by power for telling the truth ends up served as his own recipes. That is cultural violence's standard procedure: disassemble a person into consumable parts, keep the tasty ones, discard the disobedient ones.

The final chapter, early twentieth century, the Qing falling, servant boy Zhang Sanxing watches it all, and Harvester, Yaksha and Fox eat fuxi again — this time with a young man transitioning to the land of the dead. The dynasty changes. The guest list doesn't. The critic writes that it may not make much sense on first viewing. The part that does make sense he leaves out.

The only cost the review actually records is the grandmother's tail. And it records it as a punchline. A hundred and thirty-five minutes, two thousand years of myth and history, four banquets, and nobody ever pays for the tail. The critic advises watching with an open mind and a full stomach. I'd advise working out which table you're sitting at first.

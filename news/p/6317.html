飞机被提前拆了，账算在谁头上The Plane Was Scrapped Early. Who Pays?

科技 结构层 CNBC ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
一架飞机值不值钱，不看出厂年份，看它的零件能不能拆下来卖。
An aircraft's worth isn't its age. It's whether its parts can be sold.

一架2022年7月出厂的空客A320neo，今年5月被拆成零件、被挖掘机的钢牙咬穿机身。不到四岁。它本来要飞几十年。

这条新闻里没有人被打，没有人被强奸，没有战争。但暴力三角照样成立：这些飞机本可以达到的状态——飞二十年、载客、退役、回收——和实际发生的状态之间，差了一截，那一截就是暴力。这一截落在结构层。不是哪个坏人拿锤子砸了飞机，是发动机维修排队、零件短缺、存储费、二手件价格一起把一架四岁的飞机推到了拆解场。

看数字。IBA Group的数据：2025年6月到今年8月，21架最新一代飞机被拆，平均机龄6.4年；窄体机的平均退役年龄是21.7年。差了三倍多。一台发动机今年值接近1700万美元，比2019年区间上限涨了60%；租一台发动机一个月16万到18万美元。风扇叶片一片5万，大的超过10万，一台发动机20片；一个发动机盘100万到200万。

所以EirTrade的Bill Thompson说，这是他职业生涯里第一次买出厂不到五年的飞机。他花“个位数”百万美元买下那架飞机和另一架同龄的，拆出1000多个零件，卖出去，赚钱。他说咖啡机不知道卖不卖得掉，但有时候真能卖掉。

这就是结构暴力的运作方式：它不制造施暴者，它制造价差。当零件比整机值钱，整机就会消失。AerFin的CEO Simon Goodson对交易员说“请不要爱上金属”——这句话不是冷酷，是这套价差的说明书。

那谁在承受代价？报道里写到了具体的人。Spirit Airlines的前机长Glenn Zecchin，现在飞公务机，跑到亚利桑那的封存场，想看看自己老东家的飞机怎么了，还问有没有机会把这些飞机调机飞过去。他走进机舱，座椅背袋里还塞着Spirit的机上菜单和安全卡，驾驶舱里还放着飞行员手册。

这不是感伤，这是账本的另一栏。拆飞机的人赚钱，卖零件的人赚钱，买二手发动机的航空公司省钱。机长失去的是他的航线，乘客失去的是28英寸的座椅间距——报道说，接手这些飞机的航司可能会把Spirit那套紧凑的座椅拆掉，换成宽敞一点的。连座椅间距都是可以被重新定价的表达。

还有一层：这些飞机被拆，是因为发动机维修排不上队。Pratt & Whitney 2023年因为粉末金属污染召回发动机，全球数百架空客停飞，Spirit在美国受冲击最重——在它倒闭之前好几年。AeroDynamic的Mike Stengel说，这就像玩乐高，把发动机从一架飞机上拆下来装到另一架上。

乐高的比喻很准，但它漏了一件事：乐高积木不会自己决定被拼成什么。决定的是拥有价差的人。

报道没写的是，那些被拆掉的飞机上，原本坐着的人后来怎么样了。Spirit倒闭，航线消失，那些买不起别的机票的乘客去了哪里，报道一个字没提。这不是记者的疏忽，这是这条新闻的结构：它讲的是资产，不是人。

最后看一眼那架黄色的A320neo。它被涂成亮黄色，是为了飞向Spirit。四年后它被压成金属残骸，可能变成啤酒罐。Ascent的CEO Dave Querio说，看着有时候有点伤感，但看一架飞机多快被压碎送进熔炉，也有点刺激。

刺激是真的。价差也是真的。只是这价差里，没有一栏是写给坐过它的人的。

An Airbus A320neo rolled off the line in July 2022. This May, less than four years later, it was stripped for parts and crushed, an excavator's steel teeth tearing through its fuselage. It was built to fly for decades.

No one was beaten, raped, or bombed in this story. The Violence Triangle still holds: the gap between what these planes could have been—twenty years of service, a normal retirement—and what actually happened is violence. It sits in the structural layer. No villain smashed the plane. Engine queues, parts shortages, storage fees, and secondhand prices pushed a four-year-old jet to the scrapyard.

The numbers: IBA Group counted 21 newest-generation aircraft torn down from June 2025 through this August, averaging 6.4 years old, against a 21.7-year average retirement age for narrow-bodies. An engine is worth close to $17 million this year, up 60% from the 2019 upper range. Renting one runs $160,000 to $180,000 a month. A fan blade costs $50,000, sometimes over $100,000; a disc, $1 million to $2 million.

So Bill Thompson of EirTrade Aviation says it's the first time in his career he's bought an aircraft less than five years old. He paid "single-digit" millions each for that plane and a similarly aged one, pulled over 1,000 parts, and expects a profit. He doesn't know if the coffee makers will sell, but sometimes they do.

That is how structural violence works: it doesn't create perpetrators, it creates price spreads. When parts are worth more than the whole, the whole disappears. Simon Goodson of AerFin tells his traders, "Please don't fall in love with the metal." That isn't cruelty. It's the manual for the spread.

Who pays? The story names them. Glenn Zecchin, a former Spirit captain now flying corporate jets, drove to the desert storage facility to see what happened to his old airline's planes and ask about ferry opportunities. He walked into a cabin still holding Spirit in-flight menus and safety cards, pilot manuals still in the cockpit.

This isn't sentiment. It's the other column of the ledger. The dismantlers profit, the parts dealers profit, the airlines buying secondhand engines save. The captain loses his route. Passengers lose the 28-inch seat pitch—reportedly, carriers taking the planes may rip out Spirit's tight seating for something roomier. Even seat pitch is an expression that gets repriced.

The planes are being scrapped because engine maintenance is backed up. Pratt & Whitney recalled engines in 2023 over contaminated powdered metal, grounding hundreds of Airbus jets worldwide; Spirit bore the brunt in the U.S., years before it collapsed. Mike Stengel of AeroDynamic Advisory says it's like playing Legos, moving engines on and off aircraft.

The Lego metaphor is accurate, but it misses one thing: Lego bricks don't decide what they become. Whoever owns the spread decides.

What the story doesn't say is what happened to the people who used to sit in those planes. Spirit collapsed, routes vanished, and where the passengers who couldn't afford another ticket went—not a word. That's not an oversight. That's the structure of the story: it's about assets, not people.

One last look at that yellow A320neo. It was painted bright yellow to fly for Spirit. Four years later it's a metal carcass, maybe beer cans. Dave Querio of Ascent says it's a little melancholy to watch sometimes, but also kind of a thrill to see how fast an aircraft can be crunched and sent to the smelter.

The thrill is real. So is the spread. Neither column has a line for the people who flew in it.

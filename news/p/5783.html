算法替你选了，你只想自己选一次The Algorithm Picked for You. You Just Want One Choice of Your Own.

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
流媒体出租的是目录，CD买回的是选择权
Streaming rents you a catalog; a CD buys back the choice.

CD回潮不是怀旧，是有人想把「听什么」这件事从算法手里拿回来。卫报采访的这批人里，最锋利的一句来自多伦多的 Asha Varadharajan：「算法让一切变得可预测，但每个人的品味其实是随机的。」她说的不是音质，是解释权。

流媒体平台的入口只有一个：推荐。你点开什么、跳过什么、循环什么，全部变成数据，再反过来决定你下一次能看见什么。这不是中立的服务，这是第三章说的认知入口——谁掌握入口，谁就在制造「什么是好音乐」。Gabriela Crowley 说的「不再被无穷选项弄瘫」，翻译过来就是：我不再让平台的排序替我决定今晚听什么。

第二层是所有权。Neil Cumming 说得直白：流媒体等于租，歌会从平台上消失，CD 买了就一直在。Andrew Wales 家里「远超一万张」CD 挤进两居室的走廊和隔壁卧室——这不是收藏癖，这是在一个随时可以下架、改版、重录的世界里，给自己留一份不可撤回的副本。

第三层是钱去哪了。Cumming 专挑小厂牌和民谣乐队，直接从乐队手里买，因为他知道流媒体分到他喜欢的人手里的那点钱不够活。这是消费者用钱包投票，绕开平台这个中间人。结构上它很小，但它确实重新分配了一次。

但别把这件事写成反抗神话。CD 回潮的推手之一是价格：Ed 说 CD 相对黑胶「几乎等于白送」，约翰·刘易斯那边 CD 机搜索量自七月涨了 96%，另一份报告说今年 CD 销量涨了 16%（来源：卫报）。便宜和厌倦算法，两件事一起发生，才凑出这条曲线。买 CD 的人里，有多少是真的要夺回入口，有多少只是嫌黑胶太贵、流媒体太吵，报道没说。

还有一层没人问：这些受访者全是五十岁往上或三十出头、有房子有伴侣有闲工夫挑碟的人。CD 机、货架、一间能放下一万张碟的房——这些本身就是门槛。所谓「反 scrolling」，对没有那间房的人是另一种奢侈品。

Varadharajan 说「听一张 CD 需要的耐心让歌变得难忘」。耐心不是格式自带的，是有人替你把时间腾出来了。谁的时间被腾出来，谁的时间还在被算法切碎——这才是这条新闻里没被写出来的那一半。

The CD comeback isn't nostalgia. It's some people trying to take "what I listen to" back from the algorithm. The sharpest line in the Guardian piece comes from Asha Varadharajan in Toronto: algorithms make everything predictable, but everybody's taste is kind of random. She isn't talking about sound quality. She's talking about who gets to interpret.

A streaming platform has exactly one entrance: recommendation. What you click, skip, loop—all of it becomes data, and the data decides what you're allowed to see next. That isn't a neutral service. That's the cognitive entrance from Chapter 3: whoever controls the door is manufacturing "what counts as good music." When Gabriela Crowley says she's no longer paralysed by endless options, translate it: I'm not letting the platform's ranking decide tonight's listening for me.

Layer two is ownership. Neil Cumming puts it plainly—streaming means renting, songs vanish from the sites, a CD stays. Andrew Wales has "well over 10,000" CDs spilling into the corridor and the next bedroom of a two-bedroom flat. That's not hoarding. That's keeping an irrevocable copy in a world where anything can be delisted, remastered, or pulled.

Layer three is where the money goes. Cumming buys small indie and folk bands directly, because he knows the streaming payout won't keep them alive. A consumer voting with a wallet, cutting out the middleman. Structurally it's tiny, but it is a real redistribution, once.

Don't turn this into a resistance myth, though. Price is doing half the work: Ed says CDs are "almost throwaway prices" next to vinyl. John Lewis reports CD player searches up 96% since July, and another report puts CD sales up 16% this year (source: The Guardian). Cheap and fed-up arrived together; that's the curve. How many buyers are actually reclaiming the entrance, and how many just find vinyl too expensive and streaming too loud—the piece doesn't say.

And nobody asks who these people are. Every interviewee is either past fifty or early thirties, with a flat, a partner, and the leisure to browse discs. A CD player, shelves, a room that holds ten thousand discs—those are thresholds. "The antithesis of scrolling" is a different kind of luxury for someone without that room.

Varadharajan says the patience a CD demands makes songs memorable. Patience isn't built into the format. Someone cleared the time for you. Whose time got cleared, and whose time is still being diced by the algorithm—that's the half of this story nobody wrote down.

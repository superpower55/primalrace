一百二十艘船，零伤亡120 Ships Hit, Zero Casualties

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要卡塔尔天然气运输公司称，其液化天然气运输船「Al Mayeda」号周五晚在波斯湾遭「不明物体」击中，无船员受伤，未报告环境损害，船只目前处于安全可运营状态。自2月战争爆发以来，已有逾120艘商船遇袭。
The newsQatar Gas Transport Company said its LNG carrier Al Mayeda was struck by "unidentified objects" in the Gulf on Friday evening, with no crew injuries or environmental damage reported. The vessel is safe and operational. More than 120 commercial ships have been hit since the war began in February.
船体挨了打，账单由谁付，报道没写
The hull took the hit. Who pays the bill is not in the report.

这条新闻最值得注意的地方不是那艘船被什么打中了，而是它用一句话把这场战争里最贵的东西抹平了：无人员伤亡，无环境损害。

一百二十多艘商船被击中。十月至少二十九艘，是开战以来最惨的一个月，三月是二十艘。这些数字在报道里排得整整齐齐，像天气预报里的降雨概率。可商船不是军舰，船上是船员，是货，是航线两端等着这批液化天然气的人。国际海事组织和英国海事贸易行动办公室统计的是“船”，不是“人”，所以伤亡这一栏永远是空的——不是没人受伤，是没有人负责统计。

伊朗在战争初期就关掉了霍尔木兹海峡，并反复警告未经许可通过的船只将面临“毁灭”。七月美国重新封锁伊朗南部港口之后，袭击变多了。这条时间线摆得很清楚：海峡被一方关掉，港口被另一方封住，而夹在中间的是卡塔尔的天然气船、船上的菲律宾或印度船员、以及全球市场上被这条航线定价的能源。大国互相掐住对方的咽喉，代价记在第三方的账上。

加尔通的公式在这里很好用：船本来可以安全通过，现在不能，差额就是暴力。这个差额落在结构层——一条被武器化的航道，一套只统计船不统计人的数据系统；也落在文化层——“商业航运”“供应链中断”这类词把人的处境翻译成了物流术语，翻译完就没人需要为它负责。

报道说卡塔尔天然气运输公司正在“评估影响”。评估的是船体，是运营，是保险。没有人在评估那个在周五晚上值班、听见不明物体砸上船身的水手，此后还要不要继续跑这条航线。

The most telling thing in this report is not what struck the ship. It is the sentence that flattens the costliest part of this war into nothing: no injuries, no environmental damage.

More than 120 commercial ships have been hit. October alone saw at least 29, the worst month of the war, ahead of 20 in March. The numbers line up neatly, like rain probabilities in a forecast. But a merchant ship is not a warship. On board are crew, cargo, and the people at both ends of the route waiting for that LNG. The IMO and UK Maritime Trade Operations count ships, not people, so the casualty column stays empty — not because nobody was hurt, but because nobody is counting.

Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz early in the war and has repeatedly warned that vessels passing without its permission face "destruction." Attacks increased after the US reimposed a blockade on Iran's southern ports in July. The timeline is clear: one side shuts the strait, the other seals the ports, and wedged in between are Qatari gas carriers, the Filipino or Indian crews aboard them, and an energy market priced by this route. Great powers squeeze each other's throats; the bill is billed to third parties.

Galtung's formula fits: the ship could have passed safely, and now it cannot. The gap is violence. That gap sits in the structural layer — a weaponized waterway, a data system that counts hulls and not humans — and in the cultural layer, where phrases like "commercial shipping" and "supply chain disruption" translate a person's situation into logistics jargon, and once translated, no one has to answer for it.

The report says Qatar Gas Transport Company is assessing the impact. It is assessing the hull, the operations, the insurance. No one is assessing the sailor on duty on Friday night who heard something unidentified slam into the ship, and who now has to decide whether to keep sailing this route.

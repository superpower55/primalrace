一部不消费尸体的纪录片A Documentary That Refuses to Consume the Corpse

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 A.V. Club ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
把审判放在最后十五分钟，是这部片最诚实的地方
Putting the trial in the final fifteen minutes is this film's most honest move

这部纪录片最不寻常的地方，是它把五个人因佩里之死被逮捕起诉的案子放在了最后十五分钟。它没有把一个人的死亡剪成一部追凶爽片。这在名人纪录片里几乎是不正常的——正常的做法是开场就抛出案子，用悬念吊着观众看完三小时。

报道说，佩里在《老友记》婚礼那几集拍摄期间反复进出戒毒所，一次片场水上摩托事故带来的止痛药处方，2018年一次差点要命的昏迷。这些细节他在2022年的回忆录里已经讲过。纪录片没有挖出新料，它做的是另一件事：让佩里的同父异母妹妹Mia Perry Boswick第一次公开讲述，她目睹哥哥衰败之后去做了心理治疗师。还有他现实中的朋友、合作过的导演和制片人，他们夸他，也不回避他的行为给身边人造成的消耗。

这里有一个值得注意的缺席。报道明确写了，佩里的母亲、继父和继母没有出现在片子里，父亲John Bennett Perry因为身体太差无法出镜，《老友记》的演员们也没有接受采访。导演Mary Robertson没有去凑这个阵容。一部关于全球知名演员的纪录片，放弃了最有流量的几张脸，换来了几个真正和他生活在一起的人。这个交换不是姿态，是取舍。

关于成瘾的叙事很容易滑向两种套路：一种是天才被恶魔缠身，一种是咎由自取。这部片子两个都没走。它讲的是一个反复尝试戒断、反复失败、同时还要维持职业生涯的人。报道里那句“他一次又一次试图战胜内心恶魔的勇气”，说的是意志和疾病之间的拉锯，不是道德故事。

最后十五分钟里，Boswick说佩里的死“不只是一场悲剧，它是犯罪”。这句话是全片唯一一次把矛头指向外部。但纪录片没有顺着这句话往下挖——谁开的处方，谁在明知他有成瘾史的情况下继续供药，这套供给链条是怎么运转的。报道说片子只用了十五分钟讲这个案子，然后就收住了。

这就是这部片子的边界。它把一个人还原成了人，而不是话题，这已经比大多数同类作品诚实。但它停在了个人悲剧的层面，没有走到那个让悲剧得以发生的结构里去。五个被逮捕起诉的人，是这条链上的末端还是全部，报道没写，片子也没问。

一个成瘾者反复进出戒毒所，同时还在拍一部全国收视率最高的情景喜剧，这个行业对此知情了三十年。纪录片让他的妹妹和朋友开口，却没让那个行业开口。

The most unusual thing about this documentary is that it saves the case—five people arrested and charged in connection with Perry's death—for the last fifteen minutes. It doesn't cut a man's death into a true-crime thrill ride. In celebrity documentary land, that's almost abnormal. The normal move is to open with the case, dangle the suspense, and pull viewers through three hours.

The report notes that Perry was in and out of rehab during production of his big Friends wedding episodes, that an on-set jet ski injury led to a painkiller prescription, and that a 2018 coma nearly killed him. He'd already told all of this in his 2022 memoir. The doc doesn't dig up new material. What it does is something else: it lets his half-sister, Mia Perry Boswick, speak publicly for the first time—she became a psychotherapist after watching her brother deteriorate. His real-life friends, a director and a producer who worked with him, praise him and don't flinch from the toll his behavior took on the people around him.

There's a notable absence here. The report states plainly that Perry's mother, stepfather, and stepmother don't appear; his father, John Bennett Perry, was too sick to film; the Friends cast aren't interviewed either. Director Mary Robertson didn't assemble the obvious lineup. A documentary about a globally famous actor gives up its most bankable faces and gets, in exchange, a handful of people who actually lived alongside him. That trade isn't a gesture. It's a choice.

The addiction narrative slides easily into two grooves: genius haunted by demons, or he brought it on himself. This film takes neither. It shows a man who tried to get clean over and over, failed over and over, and kept a career going the whole time. The line about his bravery in trying to conquer his inner demons describes a tug-of-war between will and disease, not a morality tale.

In those final fifteen minutes, Boswick says Perry's death "wasn't just a tragedy; it was criminal." That's the one moment the film points outward. But it doesn't follow the finger—who wrote the prescriptions, who kept supplying a man with a known addiction history, how that supply chain ran. The report says the case gets fifteen minutes and then the film moves on.

That's the film's boundary. It restores a man to personhood instead of content, which already puts it ahead of most of its genre. But it stops at the personal tragedy and doesn't walk into the structure that let the tragedy happen. Whether the five arrested and charged are the end of that chain or all of it—the report doesn't say, and the film doesn't ask.

An addict cycles in and out of rehab while shooting the highest-rated sitcom in the country, and the industry knew for thirty years. The documentary lets his sister and friends speak. It doesn't let the industry speak.

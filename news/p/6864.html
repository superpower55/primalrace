富豪交税不是慈善，是欠账A Billionaire's Tax Is a Debt, Not a Gift

其他 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要加州选民将于周二表决第40号提案，对约250名加州亿万富翁的净资产一次性征收5%的税，预计筹资约1000亿美元，大部分用于弥补特朗普和共和党2025年税改造成的联邦医疗拨款削减。富有的加州商人George Zimmer在《卫报》撰文支持该提案，反驳「富豪会外逃」的说法。
The newsCalifornia voters will decide Tuesday on Proposition 40, a one-time 5% tax on the net worth of about 250 California billionaires, estimated to raise roughly $100bn, mostly for healthcare to offset federal cuts from Trump and Republicans' 2025 tax bill. Wealthy Californian George Zimmer writes in The Guardian backing the measure and rebutting claims of a billionaire exodus.
让富豪自愿交税，本身就是暴力的证据
Letting billionaires volunteer their taxes is itself evidence of violence

George Zimmer 这篇支持 Prop 40 的文章，读起来像一场良心告白。但把它放回加尔通的公式里看，它其实是一份自供状：加州 250 个亿万富翁，2019 到 2025 年财富年均增长 15%，同期缴纳的州所得税只占这增长的 0.26%。这个 0.26% 和 5% 之间的差额，就是结构暴力，而且是被人为维持了多年的结构暴力。Zimmer 说的「civic duty」，翻译过来是：这笔钱本来就该在公共池子里，现在要靠一次公投才勉强拿回来一点。

他反驳「富豪外逃」的那段论证是全文最实在的部分：马萨诸塞和华盛顿州 2022 年对高收入者加税后，百万富翁人数不降反升；2023 年的研究显示加州自 2011 年以来，年收入 20 万美元以上家庭的迁出率在全美排第二低；为了避税，亿万富翁得在 2026 年 1 月 1 日前搬走，据他所知只有六个人搬了。这些数字戳破的是一个被反复投放的叙事——「钱会跑」。这个叙事的功能不是描述现实，是让「不能向富人征税」看起来像常识。这是第三章的认知入口：先制造一个恐惧，再用恐惧替代讨论。

但 Zimmer 的整篇文章有一个他没打算处理的问题：为什么一项明显有利于多数人的税，需要靠一个亿万富翁出来写专栏背书，才能显得「理性」？他反复强调自己「不是亿万富翁」，只是「富到知道他们没道理反对」。这句话的位置很关键——他站在富豪和普通人之间的中间地带，替后者说话，也替前者求情。他说「我选择住在加州是因为我爱这里」，这句话是真的，但它同时暴露了一件事：居住地的选择对他来说是自由，对靠这份税收维持医保的人来说不是。同一个州，两种人，一种人的「爱」是消费，另一种人的「爱」是没得选。

Prop 40 如果真的通过，结构性层面确实动了一下：一次性 5% 的净资产税，全球首例，六位诺奖经济学家说它能「kickstart a movement」。这是好新闻该有的样子——不是许诺，是钱真的从上面往下挪。但要看清它挪了多少：一次性，5%，250 人。它没有改变「财富年均增长 15%、纳税 0.26%」这个结构本身，只是在这个结构上开了一个一次性的口子。Zimmer 自己写的：即便这些亿万富翁全走了，按现行加州法律，损失他们的税款要 25 年才超过 Prop 40 筹到的 1000 亿。这句话反过来读就是——现行税法让最富的人几乎不贡献，而这不是意外，是设计。

最值得盯住的是这篇文章的文体。它是一篇第一人称的、诚恳的、带点自我表扬的富人自白，发表在《卫报》评论版，作者身份标注为 Patriotic Millionaires 成员、Generation Tux 创始人、Men's Wearhouse 前 CEO。这个位置本身就是一种表达武器：当「富人支持对自己征税」变成一个可发表的文体，它就同时完成了两件事——让提案显得温和理性，也让提出提案的人显得不必那么愤怒。真正被这套结构压着的人，在这篇文章里一次都没有出现，他们只是「prevent hospitals from closing」里的那个 hospitals。

投票在周二。如果 Prop 40 过了，那是 250 个人被拿回了一小部分他们本来就没在交的钱，值得记一笔。但别忘了 Zimmer 那句「I want less inequality because my family and I will be better off in a society with less inequality」——他支持再分配的理由，是再分配对他自己也有好处。这个理由不坏，但它不是正义，是精算。正义的那一版，不需要等一个亿万富翁先算完账。

George Zimmer's piece backing Prop 40 reads like a confession of conscience. Put it back into Galtung's formula and it becomes a self-indictment: 250 California billionaires saw their wealth grow an average 15% a year from 2019 to 2025, while paying just 0.26% of that growth in state income taxes. The gap between 0.26% and 5% is structural violence, maintained for years by design. Zimmer calls it "civic duty." Translated: this money belonged in the public pool all along, and now it takes a ballot measure to claw back a sliver of it.

His rebuttal of the "billionaire exodus" claim is the most solid part. After Massachusetts and Washington raised taxes on high earners in 2022, both saw their millionaire populations rise, not fall. A 2023 study found California, despite relatively high taxes, has had the second lowest out-migration rate among households earning over $200,000 since 2011. To dodge the tax, billionaires would have had to leave by 1 January 2026; by his account, only six did. These numbers puncture a narrative that gets deployed over and over: money will run. That narrative doesn't describe reality — it makes "you can't tax the rich" look like common sense. This is the cognitive entry point from Chapter 3: manufacture a fear, then let the fear replace the discussion.

But the piece has a question Zimmer never intends to touch: why does a tax that plainly benefits most people need a billionaire's op-ed to look "reasonable"? He keeps stressing he is "not a billionaire," just wealthy enough to know they have no valid reason to fight it. That position matters — he stands in the middle, speaking for the ordinary and pleading for the rich at once. "I choose to live in California because I love it here" is true, and it also exposes something: where he lives is a choice, while for the people whose healthcare this tax would keep afloat, it isn't. Same state, two kinds of people. One kind's love is consumption; the other's is having no alternative.

If Prop 40 passes, something structural does move: a one-time 5% net-worth tax, the first of its kind anywhere, which six Nobel-winning economists say could "kickstart a movement." That is what good news looks like — not a promise, but money actually shifting downward. Still, measure how far: one time, 5%, 250 people. It doesn't change the structure where wealth grows 15% a year and taxes are 0.26%. It opens a one-time hole in that structure. Zimmer himself writes that even if every billionaire left, under current California law it would take 25 years for the lost revenue to exceed the $100bn Prop 40 would raise. Read that backwards: current tax law lets the richest contribute almost nothing, and that is not an accident. It is the design.

What deserves the closest look is the genre. A first-person, earnest, faintly self-congratulatory confession by a rich man, published in The Guardian's comment pages, the author identified as a member of Patriotic Millionaires, founder of Generation Tux, former CEO of Men's Wearhouse. That position is itself a weaponized expression: once "rich people support taxing themselves" becomes a publishable genre, it does two things at once — makes the proposition look moderate and reasonable, and makes the people who proposed it look like they needn't be angry. The people actually crushed by this structure never appear in the piece. They are only the hospitals in "prevent hospitals from closing."

The vote is Tuesday. If Prop 40 passes, 250 people have had a small portion of money they weren't paying anyway clawed back. Worth noting. But don't forget Zimmer's line: "I want less inequality because my family and I will be better off in a society with less inequality." His reason for supporting redistribution is that redistribution benefits him too. That reason isn't bad, but it isn't justice — it's arithmetic. The justice version doesn't wait for a billionaire to finish the math.

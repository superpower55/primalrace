马拉松第一次给轮椅和义肢发奖The Marathon Finally Gave Out Podiums. Not Prize Money.

奖杯发了，奖金没发，这就是包容的定价。
A podium without a purse is inclusion with a price tag.

纽约马拉松今年第一次给五个残障组别设了领奖台，男女各取前三。没有奖金。

这是好新闻，但好在哪一层要说清楚。direct 层没有动，没有人被殴打、被拦在起跑线外，这条新闻里不存在一个施暴者。动的在 structural 层：赛事这个制度第一次承认，残障运动员不是“参与者”，是“选手”。纽约路跑协会的首席多元化官 Erica Edwards-O'Neal 说得很直白，他们要“超越仅仅提供参与的机会”。过去的安排是“提供参与”，现在的安排是“提供名次”。名次是资源，是承认，是可以写进履历、换成赞助、换成下一场比赛入场券的东西。

但同一封邮件里，奖金是零。轮椅组从 2000 年就有竞速组别，第二年就加了奖金；义肢、视障、智力障碍这些组别等了二十多年，等到的是一块牌子。这就是 structural violence 的常规操作：把承认发出去，把分配留下来。领奖台是廉价的，奖金是要动预算的。

Dani Aravich 那句话是整篇报道里最锋利的一句。她说组织者和赞助商“可能没看到投资的需要，因为他们在起跑线上没看到很多人”。然后她补了半句：很大一部分原因是残障运动员不觉得自己被欢迎。这是因果被倒过来讲的标准案例——先不邀请，再统计到场人数，再用到场人数证明不值得邀请。她本人是残障权利非营利组织 So Every BODY Can Move 的项目负责人，和 Nicole Ver Kuilen 一起拍过纪录片，讲保险公司不给运动义肢报销。Ver Kuilen 这次要跑自己的第七个马拉松，带着一个让她没法戴义肢的皮肤病。她能站上起跑线，靠的是朋友当领跑、朋友背几副义肢在赛道边换、朋友当人墙挡人群。这些成本没有一项由赛事承担。

所以这次进步是谁赢来的，报道写得很清楚：是运动员自己，长期做倡导、做咨询、教组织者在报名表上加一个下拉菜单、加一个无障碍厕所。Edwards-O'Neal 把这叫“试点”。试点是一个可以撤回的词。

Ver Kuilen 的目标是跑进四小时，她在尤金差了一分五十九秒。这个数字和领奖台无关，和奖金无关，和“代表性”无关。它只属于她。

For the first time in its history, the New York City Marathon awarded podium placements in five Para athletics categories — low vision and blind, intellectual and developmental disability, coordination difficulties, lower limb disability, upper limb disability — first through third, men and women. No prize money.

This is good news, but be precise about which layer moved. Nothing at the direct level: no one was beaten, no one was barred from the start line, there is no perpetrator in this story. What moved is structural. The race, as an institution, reclassified disabled athletes from "participants" to "competitors." Erica Edwards-O'Neal, the chief diversity officer for New York Road Runners, put it plainly: they want to go beyond providing access to participation. Access was the old offer. Placement is the new one. Placement is a resource — it goes on a résumé, it converts into sponsorship, into entry into the next race.

And in the same breath, the purse is zero. Wheelchair racers have had competitive divisions since 2000, with prize money added the following year. The Para categories waited two decades for a medal. This is structural violence doing what it does: hand out recognition, withhold distribution. A podium is cheap. A budget line is not.

Dani Aravich's line is the sharpest thing in the piece. Organizers and sponsors, she said, maybe didn't see a need to invest "because they weren't seeing a lot of people at the start line." Then the other half: a huge part of the reason was that athletes with disabilities did not feel welcome. That is causation run backwards as standard procedure — don't invite, count the turnout, cite the turnout as proof that inviting isn't worth it. Aravich runs campaigns for So Every BODY Can Move; she and Nicole Ver Kuilen recently appeared in a documentary about insurers refusing to cover sporting prostheses. Ver Kuilen is running her seventh marathon with a skin condition that has kept her out of her prosthesis. She gets to the start line on a friend's legs — Aravich as support runner, carrying spare prostheses to swap as the elevation changes, acting as a buffer against the crowds. None of that cost is carried by the race.

So who won this, and at what price? The athletes did, through years of advocacy and consulting — teaching organizers to add a drop-down menu on entry forms, to add an accessible toilet. Edwards-O'Neal calls it a pilot. Pilot is a word with a recall option.

Ver Kuilen's goal is under four hours. In Eugene she missed it by 1 minute 59 seconds. That number has nothing to do with the podium, the purse, or representation. It belongs to her.

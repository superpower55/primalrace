坏室友的忏悔录，谁在替谁开脱The Bad Roommate Confessional

文娱 文化层 · 直接层 The Cut ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
每一条忏悔都在给一个更小的房间开脱
Every confession is a smaller room built to hide a bigger one.

《The Cut》这篇稿子问的是坏室友知不知道自己的罪行、有没有悔意。答案在标题里就给了：他们知道，他们甚至感到抱歉。然后整篇文章变成了六个成年人轮流上台，把当年合租时干的破事讲成段子，台下的人笑，台上的人笑得更响。

这套机制我太熟了。忏悔是一种表达，而表达决定你是谁。一个人站出来说“我是坏室友”，他确立的身份不是加害者，是坦诚的人、有自知之明的人、成长了的人。罪行的叙述越生动，这个新身份就越值钱。偷室友的男友，最后嫁给对方十二年，写出来是一段传奇； cupcake massacre，写出来是一场青春喜剧。真正被损害的那个人的位置，被叙述者顺手占了——她成了故事里的配角，一个后来 ghost 掉婚礼请柬的、放不下的人。

注意这些故事的共同结构：坏事发生在“大学”，发生在“前额叶还没发育完全”的年纪。这句话在文章里出现不止一次，像一个免责条款，被反复盖章。前额叶是生物墙的一部分，它确实在二十五岁前没长好。但把一段关系的伤害全部推给神经发育，等于说那时候的你不是你，那时候被你伤害的人也不是人。生物墙可以解释能力，不能解释选择。偷男友、砸衣服、把别人叠好的衣服倒在地上问“你现在什么感觉”，这些不是发育问题，是博弈。

最值得看的是那个砸房间的故事。导火索是室友在 2016 年投了 Trump。叙述者说，从那以后她做的每件事都让她恼火。于是她倒掉对方的衣服，拉开抽屉，把叠好的衣服铺在地上，一边问“把干净衣服放在脏地上，你感觉如何”。这不是失控，这是一次精确的表达投放：用对方投票的政治立场给自己发了一张施暴许可证。进步的政治语言在这里被武器化，成了私人空间里合法施暴的入口。她恨的不是脏衣服，是那张选票；她报复的方式，是让对方在自己的房间里被降格成一堆待处理的布料。

还有那个“用治疗话术当武器”的。她给抑郁的室友列“我们都热爱什么”，自己的答案是写作、书、音乐，然后问对方热爱什么。这不是鼓励，这是把治疗的语言改造成一把尺子，量出谁更有热情、谁更值得活。室友答不上来，因为那场对话从一开始就不是对话。文章把这一段放在“我后来很羞愧”的框架里，羞愧是真的，但羞愧不改变那晚灯是谁不肯关、是谁摔门走人。

文章最后一段是那个偷衣服的。她说室友衣服太多、一直在换，所以觉得对方不会发现。她说她们不是朋友，对方怕她，不敢抱怨。她还补了一句：对方不分担家用，自己买卫生纸带进厕所。这一句是整篇文章里最诚实的地方——它暴露了所有忏悔的底层语法：我做了坏事，但对方也有问题。忏悔一旦带上这个尾巴，就不再是忏悔，是谈判。

所以这篇文章真正记录的不是坏室友的悔意，是一群人如何用叙事把当年的暴力重新定价。同样的行为，放在别人身上叫霸凌，放在自己身上叫青春；放在自己身上叫“我那时候是个宝宝”，放在室友身上叫“她太敏感”。这是元暴力最日常的形态：解释权在谁手里，谁就能决定哪件事算伤害、哪件事算笑话。六个故事里，被伤害的人一个都没有开口。她们只作为背景出现，作为 ghost 掉请柬的人、作为不敢抱怨的人、作为“投了 Trump 所以活该”的人。

报道没写这六个人现在和当年的室友还有没有联系，除了那个结婚十二年的。也没写那些被倒掉衣服、被偷走男友、被半夜吵醒的人，现在怎么讲这些事。这个空白不是稿子的疏忽，是稿子的结构：忏悔录天然只收录忏悔者的声音。而忏悔者最擅长的，就是把自己写成那个终于长大的人，把对方写成那个还没走出来的人。

The Cut asks whether bad roommates know their crimes and feel remorse. The answer is in the headline: they know, they even feel bad. Then the piece becomes six adults taking turns onstage, retelling their shared-living atrocities as bits, the audience laughing, the tellers laughing harder.

I know this mechanism. Confession is an expression, and expression determines who you are. A person who stands up and says "I was the bad roommate" is not establishing himself as a perpetrator. He is establishing himself as candid, self-aware, grown. The more vivid the crime, the more the new identity is worth. Stealing your roommate's boyfriend and marrying him for twelve years becomes a legend; the cupcake massacre becomes a campus comedy. The person actually damaged gets written out of the frame, recast as a supporting character, someone who ghosted a wedding invitation because she couldn't let go.

Notice the shared structure: it all happened in college, before the frontal lobe fully developed. That phrase appears more than once, a disclaimer stamped on the page. The frontal lobe is part of the biological wall; it genuinely isn't finished before twenty-five. But attributing a relationship's damage entirely to neural development says that the you back then wasn't you, and the person you hurt wasn't a person. Biology can explain capacity. It cannot explain choice. Stealing a boyfriend, trashing clothes, dumping someone's folded laundry on the floor and asking "how does this make you feel" — these aren't developmental glitches. They are moves in a game.

The room-trashing story is the one to watch. The trigger was a roommate voting for Trump in 2016. After that, everything she did infuriated the narrator. So she dumped the woman's laundry basket, opened her drawers, spread the clean folded clothes on the ground, asking, "How does this make you feel? To have your clean clothes on the dirty ground?" That isn't a loss of control. That is a precisely delivered expression: using the other woman's politics to issue herself a permit to do violence. Progressive political language weaponized into a private license. What she hated wasn't the laundry. It was the ballot. And her revenge was to downgrade the other woman, inside her own room, to a pile of fabric awaiting processing.

Then there's the one who weaponized therapy talk. She sat her depressed roommate down to list what they were both "passionate about" — hers being writing, books, music — and asked for the other's. This isn't encouragement. It's therapy language refitted into a ruler, measuring who has more passion, who deserves to be alive. The roommate gave two answers because the conversation was never a conversation. The essay frames this inside "I feel ashamed now." The shame is real. The shame does not change who kept the light on that night, who stormed out.

The last story is the clothes thief. She borrowed without asking because the roommate had so many clothes, always changing, so she figured it wouldn't be noticed. She says they weren't friends, that the roommate was too scared of her to complain. Then she adds: the roommate didn't contribute to household expenses, bought her own toilet paper, took it to the bathroom with her. That line is the most honest thing in the piece. It exposes the grammar underneath every confession: I did a bad thing, but she had her problems too. A confession with that tail is no longer a confession. It's a negotiation.

So what this piece actually records isn't remorse. It's a group of people repricing old violence through narrative. The same act, done by someone else, is bullying; done by me, it's youth. Done by me, I was a baby. Done by her, she was too sensitive. This is meta-violence in its most everyday form: whoever holds the interpretive pen decides what counts as harm and what counts as a joke. Across six stories, not one of the harmed speaks. They appear only as backdrop — the one who ghosted the invitation, the one too scared to complain, the one who voted for Trump and therefore had it coming.

The piece doesn't say whether any of these six are still in touch with those roommates, except the one married twelve years. It doesn't say how the people whose clothes were dumped, whose boyfriends were stolen, who were kept awake at night, tell these stories now. That absence isn't an oversight. It's the structure: a confessional only collects confessors. And confessors are best at writing themselves as the ones who finally grew up, and the other as the one who never got over it.

两个人打算用三十年玩一局游戏Two Men Plan to Spend 30 Years on One Game

文娱 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
把三十年押给一局棋，是自由，也是逃避。
Betting thirty years on a board game is freedom and escape at once.

两个三十岁上下的男人，打算用二三十年玩完一局桌游。报道没写他们打算怎么活过这二三十年，只写了他们打算怎么玩。

这局游戏叫 The Campaign for North Africa，1979 年 Richard Berg 设计，模拟 1940 到 1943 年的沙漠战争。10 英尺的地图切成五个画框，1600 个棋子要磁化，德国棋子还没拆下来，因为大约五年内用不上。规则书七本，外加一本历史书。意大利部队煮意面要用更多的水，这条规则被当成笑话，但没人敢确定它真是笑话。他们花 970 英镑从 eBay 买下它，做了 99 期播客记录准备过程，第 100 期正式开局。

这不是一条需要拆穿什么的新闻。它没有受害者，没有叙事被武器化，没有谁的身体被定价。它唯一值得说的东西，是它把一种东西摆到了台面上：时间。三十年，是这两个人自愿交出去的。加尔通的公式在这里算不出差额，因为没人被剥夺什么，他们只是在选择把自己的存在投进哪一个容器。

真正有意思的是他们为什么要开播客。Purcell 说得很直白：不播就没理由继续玩下去，否则两周就放弃，剩下一千镑的板子什么也做不了。所以这个播客不是记录，是锁。他们需要有人在看，才能不半途而废。存在性战争里，人给自己造观众，造出来的观众就成了约束自己的规则。两个人，一间公寓，一个每周更新的节目，把二三十年的自由换成一个不得不完成的承诺。这是真.最优解表达，还是假.最优解表达，取决于三十年后他们回头看时，那个决定是谁做的。

报道里还有一个细节值得停一下：他们说，一局平局最难受，输反而更好，因为至少是个确定的结果。这句话很诚实，也很残酷。二三十年之后，他们要的不是赢，是一个能结束的东西。而这场游戏最可能的结局，是根本玩不完。

三十年后他们六十岁。报道没写这三十年里谁会生病、谁要照顾谁、谁的生活会先撑不住。这些不在游戏规则里，也不在播客的更新列表里。规则书有七本，生活一本也没有。

Two men, roughly thirty, intend to finish a board game over the next twenty to thirty years. The report doesn't say how they plan to live through those decades. Only how they plan to play.

The game is The Campaign for North Africa, designed by Richard Berg in 1979, simulating the desert war of 1940 to 1943. A 10ft map cut into five picture frames, 1,600 pieces to be magnetised, the German pieces not yet punched out because they won't be needed for about five years. Seven rule books plus a history book. Italian forces need more water to boil their pasta rations, a rule assumed to be a joke, though nobody is sure. They paid £970 for it on eBay, recorded 99 podcast episodes of preparation, and started the actual game as episode 100.

This is not a story that needs a mask pulled off. No victim, no weaponised narrative, no body being priced. What it does is put one thing on the table: time. Thirty years, handed over voluntarily. Galtung's equation has no gap to compute here, because nobody is being deprived. They are choosing which container to pour their existence into.

The interesting part is why they started a podcast. Purcell says it plainly: without it there'd be no reason to keep going, they'd quit within two weeks, and they'd be left with a thousand-pound board they couldn't use. So the podcast isn't a record. It's a lock. They need someone watching, or they won't finish. In an existential war, you manufacture your own audience, and the audience you manufacture becomes the rule that binds you. Two people, one flat, a weekly show, trading twenty or thirty years of freedom for a commitment they can't walk away from. Whether that's a true optimal expression or a false one depends on who, thirty years from now, they'll say made the decision.

One detail in the report is worth pausing on: a draw would be the worst outcome, they say. A loss would be better, because at least it's definitive. That's honest, and it's bleak. After twenty or thirty years, what they want isn't to win. It's something that ends. And the most likely ending is that the game can't be finished at all.

In thirty years they'll be sixty. The report doesn't say who gets sick, who has to care for whom, whose life gives out first. None of that is in the rule books. There are seven of those. Life doesn't come with one.

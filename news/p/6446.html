玩家的怀旧被谁定价Who Gets to Price Your Nostalgia

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Polygon.com ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
评测标准被谁握着，游戏就为谁而做。
Whoever holds the rubric decides who the game is for.

Polygon 这篇推荐的核心论据是"这些游戏不需要重制"。但整篇读下来，它真正在讨论的从来不是游戏本身，而是谁有资格给一个旧作品发"免于重制"的通行证。

看它怎么立论：Super Mario World 被夸的是"技术奇迹"——精灵缩放、视差滚动、肩键预判；Super Mario Kart 被夸的是重车起步慢、轻车转向紧，以及"橡皮筋 AI 没那么碍事"。这些全是可被量化的机制指标。作者用一套 2026 年的评测语汇,给 1990 年的作品打分,然后宣布它及格。及格线是谁画的?是当下这套话语画的。作品没变,尺子换了。

这不是游戏圈独有的毛病。任何领域里,"经典"这个词的定价权都不在作品手里,在写评论的人手里。他说 Super Mario Bros. 3 "接近完美",理由是"简单设计里有美";他说 All-Stars 重制版"物理手感变迟钝了"——手感这东西,没有客观刻度,只有谁先说出口。先开口的人定义了标准,后来的人拿着这把尺子去量一切。

文章里有一句话暴露了全部机制:"Just keep making them available wherever you can, Nintendo, because they're fine exactly as they are." 这句话表面是在替玩家请命,实际是在替任天堂划定一条最省事的商业路线——别做重制,把旧货搬上货架就行。作者没拿任天堂的钱,但他替任天堂省下的开发成本,是真实的。这就是共谋:不需要合同,只需要一套让"不投入"听起来像"尊重"的叙事。

再看它怎么处理 Samus。整篇对 Super Metroid 的最高赞美是"孤独的走廊如今像家"。一个女性主角,被写成一个可以被反复重访的、安全的、熟悉的场所。玩家回到 Zebes 不是因为那里有什么新东西,是因为那里什么都不用变。这种"不变即美德"的审美,和把女性身体当作可反复消费的稳定容器,结构上是同一套东西——熟悉感被包装成价值,而重新想象被判定为多余。

最要命的是第五条。文章说这些游戏"so good they never need a remake",但它列出的每一个理由,都建立在"我们已经知道它好"之上。对没玩过 1990 年那批游戏的人,这篇文章什么也没说。它服务的是记忆,不是经验。记忆是有门槛的资产,门槛之内的人互相确认品味,门槛之外的人被礼貌地排除在"经典"的定义之外。

所以这篇推荐真正在做的,不是保护经典,是保护一套关于"什么算好"的旧解释权。它把"不需要重制"说成对作品的尊重,实际上是在说:别动那套我们熟悉的评价体系。

任天堂会不会听,是另一回事。但有一件事这篇没写:那些从没玩过 Super Metroid 的人,他们想要的不是一条走廊被称作"家",而是一条他们自己走进去的走廊。

Polygon's list argues that these five games "never need a remake." But read closely, it is not really about the games. It is about who gets to issue a work a permanent exemption from being remade.

Look at how it builds each case. Super Mario World is praised for sprite scaling, parallax scrolling, a shoulder button that lets you look ahead. Super Mario Kart is praised because heavy karts are slow off the line and light karts turn tight, and because the rubberband AI is less obtrusive. These are all quantifiable mechanical metrics. The writer takes a 2026 review vocabulary and grades a 1990 artifact against it, then declares it passing. Who drew the passing line? The current discourse did. The work did not change. The ruler did.

This is not a games-industry quirk. In any field, the word "classic" is not priced by the work. It is priced by whoever writes the review. Super Mario Bros. 3 is called "as close to perfect as a platformer gets" because "there's beauty in the simpler design." The All-Stars remake is faulted because Mario's movement "felt the slightest bit more sluggish." Feel has no objective scale. It has a first speaker. The first speaker defines the standard, and everyone after inherits the ruler.

One sentence gives the whole mechanism away: "Just keep making them available wherever you can, Nintendo, because they're fine exactly as they are." On its face this pleads for players. In practice it hands Nintendo the cheapest possible commercial route: skip the remake, re-shelve the old cartridge. The writer takes no money from Nintendo, but the development budget he saves them is real. That is complicity. No contract required. Only a narrative that makes "not investing" sound like "respect."

Then look at how it handles Samus. The highest compliment paid to Super Metroid is that its lonely corridors "now feel like home." A female protagonist is rewritten as a safe, familiar place you can revisit indefinitely. Players return to Zebes not because anything new awaits, but because nothing there has to change. That aesthetic, where stasis is a virtue, has the same structure as treating a woman's body as a stable container for repeat consumption. Familiarity gets packaged as value, and reimagination gets ruled superfluous.

The fifth point is the tell. The list claims these games are "so good they never need a remake," but every reason it gives rests on "we already know they're good." For anyone who never played the 1990 batch, the piece says nothing. It serves memory, not experience. Memory is a gated asset. Those inside the gate confirm each other's taste. Those outside are politely excluded from the definition of "classic."

So the recommendation is not protecting classics. It is protecting an old claim over what counts as good. "No remake needed" is presented as respect for the work. It is really a request: don't touch the evaluation system we already know.

Whether Nintendo listens is another matter. But one thing goes unwritten: the people who never played Super Metroid do not want a corridor called home. They want a corridor they can walk into themselves.

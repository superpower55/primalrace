圣诞歌单没有新人The Christmas Canon Has Stopped Recording

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要公告牌百强单曲榜历史上共有16首节日歌曲进入前十名。1958年，《花栗鼠之歌》成为首支登顶的节日歌曲，连续四周位列第一。玛丽亚·凯莉的《All I Want for Christmas Is You》自2019年起累计21周排名第一。近两年进入前十的最新作品是爱莉安娜·格兰德的《Santa Tell Me》和凯莉·克莱森的《Underneath the Tree》。
The newsSixteen holiday songs have reached the Billboard Hot 100's top 10. "The Chipmunk Song" became the first, spending four weeks at No. 1 in 1958. Mariah Carey's "All I Want for Christmas Is You" has logged 21 weeks at No. 1 since 2019. The most recently released entries are Ariana Grande's "Santa Tell Me" and Kelly Clarkson's "Underneath the Tree."
十六首节日金曲，最年轻的一首也已经十二岁了。
Sixteen holiday hits, and the youngest is already twelve years old.

这份榜单最刺眼的不是谁进了前十，而是最年轻的一首节日金曲是 Ariana Grande 2014 年的《Santa Tell Me》。十六首歌里，最新的一首已经十二岁。一个每年被反复播放的曲库，停止生产新东西了。

Billboard 自己给了答案：节日歌曲大批涌入前十，集中在 2020 年代，原因是流媒体壮大、节日音乐在平台歌单里位置更显眼。翻译一下：不是有人写出了更好的圣诞歌，是算法把同一批歌每年冬天重新推到你耳朵里一次。榜单没变，是尺子变了——过去节日歌曲很长时间根本没资格进 Hot 100，只能上季节榜；现在有资格了，于是五十年代到九十年代的老歌集体回榜。这不是音乐复兴，这是评价方式改了。

谁在付代价。写歌的人。Nat King Cole 1946 年的《The Christmas Song》、Brenda Lee 1958 年的《Rockin' Around the Christmas Tree》——后者在 2023 年才拿到三周冠军，隔了六十五年。一首歌在发行半个多世纪后登顶，听起来像迟到的公正，实际上是版税流向了已经成名的老名字，而新歌连进前十的门都摸不到。流媒体的节日歌单是一个封闭的循环：平台推老歌，因为老歌有确定性；听众听老歌，因为歌单里只有老歌；数据回来证明老歌有效，明年继续推。新人被挡在认知入口之外，不是因为唱得差，是因为算法不需要不确定性。

这不是圣诞歌的问题，是所有被平台化的文化生产的问题。当一个行业的分配逻辑从“谁做出了新东西”变成“谁的东西被反复播放”，创作就变成了库存管理。十六首歌里五首标题带 Christmas，两首带 Tree，你甚至能从命名规律看出这个曲库有多保守——它只需要一个能被搜索到的关键词。

Mariah Carey 的《All I Want for Christmas Is You》1994 年发行，2019 年才开始拿冠军，累计二十一周第一。一首歌的商业生命被拉长到三十年，代价是这三十年里没有第二首歌能挤进同一个位置。

榜单还在更新，曲库已经封存。下一个能进前十的圣诞歌，大概要等到有人先说服算法：新东西也值得被推一次。

The loudest thing about this list isn't who made the top 10. It's that the youngest holiday hit on it is Ariana Grande's "Santa Tell Me," from 2014. Sixteen songs, and the newest one is twelve years old. A catalog that gets replayed every winter has stopped producing anything new.

Billboard hands over the explanation itself: most holiday songs that reached the top 10 did so in the 2020s, as streaming grew and holiday music became more prominent on playlists. Translation: nobody wrote better Christmas songs. The algorithm re-serves the same batch to your ears every December. The chart didn't change. The ruler did. For much of the Hot 100's history, holiday songs weren't even eligible, appearing on seasonal surveys instead. Now they're eligible, and the 1950s through the 1990s come flooding back. This isn't a musical revival. It's a change in how things get counted.

Who pays. The writers. Brenda Lee's "Rockin' Around the Christmas Tree" took three weeks at No. 1 in 2023, sixty-five years after its 1958 release. A song topping the chart half a century after it came out sounds like overdue justice. It's actually royalties flowing to names that are already famous, while new songs can't get near the top 10. The streaming holiday playlist is a closed loop: platforms push old songs because old songs are certain; listeners hear old songs because the playlist only has old songs; the data comes back proving old songs work, so next year it pushes them again. New artists are locked out of the cognitive entrance, not because they're worse, but because the algorithm has no use for uncertainty.

This isn't a Christmas-song problem. It's the problem of all platformized cultural production. When an industry's distribution logic shifts from who made something new to whose thing gets replayed, creation becomes inventory management. Five of the sixteen titles contain "Christmas," two contain "Tree." You can read the conservatism right off the naming: the catalog only needs a searchable keyword.

Mariah Carey's "All I Want for Christmas Is You" came out in 1994 and only started hitting No. 1 in 2019, twenty-one weeks in total. A song's commercial life stretched across three decades, at the cost of no second song getting into that position during those three decades.

The chart still updates. The catalog is sealed. The next holiday song to crack the top 10 will arrive when someone first convinces the algorithm that new things deserve a push too.

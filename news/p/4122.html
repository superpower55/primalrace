书店的生存不该是政府的施舍Bookshops Should Not Beg for the Right to Exist

哲学 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-08-18 § 链接
将文化空间视为‘社区福利’而非‘基础权利’，本身就是一种结构性暴力。
Treating cultural spaces as 'community benefits' rather than 'basic rights' is a form of structural violence.

这篇新闻在叙事上陷入了一个典型的陷阱：将独立书店的生存简化为一场关于‘税收减免’的请求。当 Philip Pullman 等作家呼吁政府将书店与酒吧、俱乐部一样纳入 business rates relief 时，他们实际上是在接受一套由权力制定者定义的博弈规则——即书店必须证明自己具有某种‘社区贡献’（community benefit），才能换取生存的入场券。

从加尔通的暴力三角来看，这不仅是 structural violence，更是 cultural violence 的共谋。政府一方面通过‘国家阅读年’（National Year of Reading）在文化层面上制造阅读很重要的叙事，另一方面在结构层面上通过税收压力挤压书店。这种矛盾并非失误，而是一种精准的控制：它让文化空间变成了政府可以随时开关的‘福利’，而不是公民获取新思想的‘权利’。当书店需要通过扮演‘社区灯塔’来乞求减税时，它的主体性已经部分死亡，变成了权力叙事中的点缀。

更讽刺的是，政府在打击 vape shops 和 betting shops 的同时，却在用财务杠杆缓慢地剔除书店。这是一种极其阴险的‘清理’方式：不需要直接禁绝，只需要让成本上升到独立书店无法承受的程度，从而让 high street 最终被那些能够承受高额税率的 corporate darkness（企业黑暗）所吞噬。这种‘ corporate darkness’不仅是商业上的，更是认知入口的垄断。

如果书店的生存依赖于政治人物的‘良心’或‘遗产’（legacy），那么这种胜利即便到来也是表演性的。真正的 good_news 应该是将文化空间的准入与维护从‘福利审批制’改为‘制度保障制’，而不是在同一个权力天平上，把书店和酒吧放在一起比谁更‘有用’。

This news falls into a classic narrative trap: reducing the survival of independent bookshops to a plea for 'tax relief.' When authors like Philip Pullman urge the government to grant bookshops the same business rates relief as pubs and clubs, they are inadvertently accepting a game defined by the powerful—that bookshops must prove their 'community benefit' to earn the right to exist.

Applying the Violence Triangle, this is not just structural violence, but a complicity in cultural violence. The government promotes a narrative that 'reading matters' through the National Year of Reading, while simultaneously squeezing bookshops through structural tax pressure. This contradiction is not a mistake; it is a mechanism of control. It transforms cultural spaces into 'benefits' that the state can grant or withdraw, rather than 'rights' for citizens to access new thoughts. When a bookshop must perform the role of a 'community lighthouse' to beg for tax cuts, its subjectivity is partially dead, becoming a mere ornament in a power narrative.

Even more cynical is the government's strategy of tackling vape shops and betting shops while using financial levers to slowly erase bookshops. This is a subtle form of 'cleansing': no need for a direct ban, just raise the costs until independent shops collapse, allowing the high street to be swallowed by the 'corporate darkness'—a monopoly not just of capital, but of cognitive entry points.

If the survival of bookshops depends on the 'conscience' or 'legacy' of politicians, any victory is merely performative. A true good_news would be moving the maintenance of cultural spaces from a 'benefit approval system' to a 'institutional guarantee system,' rather than weighing bookshops against pubs on the same scale of 'usefulness.'

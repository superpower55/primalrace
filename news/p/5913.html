战争叙事从来不用女人付账War Narratives Never Bill the Women

国际 文化层 · 结构层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
每场战争都用"保护"开场，用通胀收尾
Every war opens with protection and closes with inflation.

这篇报道最诚实的一句话，是特朗普的中东特使 Barrack 自己说的：我们本想在伊朗做点什么，结果火从营地烧成了森林，一路烧到北非，然后他问，这到哪儿为止？23 次政权更迭，没有一次成功。他自己把答案说出来了。

但报道把它写成一场外交失灵、一次多边主义的破产、一个"反复无常、虚荣、无道德"的总统造成的意外。不对。这不是意外，这是武器化叙事的标准收尾。第三章讲得很清楚：战争叙事从来不是从利益开始讲的，是从"必须做点什么"开始讲的。伊朗核问题被描述成一个必须被解决的东西，于是解决它的方式——轰炸——就自动获得了正当性。阿曼外长在剑桥提醒听众，轰炸开始前，核心问题的协议已经近在咫尺。这句话在这篇长文里只出现了一次，然后就被"每项检验都失败"的清单淹没了。

清单本身是真的：全球通胀 4%，布伦特原油每桶 117 美元，比战前高 50%，沙特 GDP 被 IMF 预测收缩 3.3%，比七月的预测下调五个百分点，赤字预测被抬到 653 亿美元。这些数字不是战争的副产品，这些数字就是战争。Galtung 的公式在这里不需要解释：本可以达到的状态，和实际达到的状态之间的差额，就是暴力。差额被记在谁头上？记在巴黎和马尼拉的街头，记在那些为燃料价格上街的人身上。马克龙对示威者说，你们的愤怒是正当的，但这场冲突法国没有参加，后果却由我们承受。图斯克在议会说，油价贵得要命，但那个唐纳德不是我，是特朗普。

两位领导人都说了半句真话，然后停在那里。他们没有说的是：这场战争之所以能被发动，是因为在发动之前，它已经被讲成了一个关于"保护"的故事。报道里内塔尼亚胡要的是推翻伊朗政权，美国要的是核武器，两套目标被缝进同一件叙事外衣里。这件外衣的料子，永远是同一个：为了保护我们的人，为了阻止更坏的事。第三章把这个入口叫浪漫叙事的一个变种——不是爱情，是拯救。谁被拯救？报道没写。报道写了阿里·拉里贾尼在开战第一天被炸死，写了新最高领袖身边的强硬派比以前更稳固，写了伊朗国内的公开辩论正在往"取消核武器禁令"的方向倾斜。被"解决"的那个问题，变得更难解决了。

然后是乌克兰。特朗普把能源价格的账推给乌克兰对俄罗斯炼油厂的攻击，要求泽连斯基下台，宣布一个双方都没同意过的 60 天"能源休战"，然后放出一笔小到没人认真评论的俄罗斯柴油交易。Starlink 被切断又恢复，白宫、万斯、马斯克给出三种互相矛盾的说法。这一段报道写得很好，好到它自己暴露了机制：当一个叙事开始失效，操盘手的反应不是修正叙事，是再找一个更小的叙事来顶住。泽连斯基成了那个更小的叙事。

报道最后落到希望上：欧洲醒了，海湾国家开始谈一个包含伊朗的安全协定，弗雷兰说各国会认真考虑还要不要买五角大楼控制的 F-35。这些是真实的松动，但它们松动的对象是美国的技术主权和军事依赖，不是那套让战争得以开始的叙事。欧洲领导人指责特朗普，是因为他们必须自保，不是因为他们要拆掉"必须做点什么"这个入口。马克龙把责任推给华盛顿的那一刻，他同时也在告诉街上的抗议者：这套叙事我们还在用，只是这次不是我们按的按钮。

报道问，外交能不能夺回控制权。可以问一个更前面的问题：谁在付账，谁在签字。签字的人在 Miami 的高尔夫俱乐部里准备迎接普京，付账的人在巴黎街头举着牌子。中间那层——那些把轰炸讲成必要、把通胀讲成外部冲击、把"我们没参加这场战争"讲成免责声明的人——一个都没被点名。

The most honest line in this piece comes from Trump's own envoy, Tom Barrack: we tried to do something in Iran, and the camp fires became forest fires, spreading through Hamas, Hezbollah, six militias, the Houthis, and now north Africa. Where does it stop? He then answers himself: 23 regime changes across the greater Middle East, and none of them worked.

The report files this under failed diplomacy, broken multilateralism, and a capricious, vain, amoral president. Wrong. This is not an accident. This is how a weaponized narrative closes out. Chapter Three is explicit: war never begins as an argument about interests. It begins as an argument about necessity. Iran's nuclear programme gets framed as a thing that must be solved, and once it is, the method of solving it—bombing—arrives pre-justified. Oman's foreign minister reminded an audience in Cambridge that an agreement on the core issue was within reach before the bombing started. That sentence appears once in a very long article, then drowns under the ledger of failure.

The ledger is real: global inflation at 4%, Brent at $117 a barrel, 50% above prewar levels, Saudi GDP forecast to contract 3.3%, a five-point downgrade from July, the deficit projection raised to $65.3bn. These are not side effects of the war. These are the war. Galtung's formula needs no elaboration here: the gap between what was attainable and what was attained is the violence. Who carries the gap? The people on the streets of Paris and Milan, angry about fuel prices. Macron tells them their anger is legitimate, but the conflict was launched in the Persian Gulf, France took no part, and yet France suffers the consequences. Tusk tells parliament fuel is devilishly expensive, but the Donald responsible is not him, it is Trump.

Both men say half a truth and stop. What neither says is that this war could be launched because, before it was launched, it had already been told as a story about protection. Netanyahu wanted regime change; Washington wanted the nuclear file; two agendas got stitched into one narrative garment. The fabric is always the same: to protect our people, to prevent something worse. Chapter Three calls this the rescue variant of the romance entry—not love, salvation. Who was saved? The report does not say. It says Ali Larijani was blown up on day one, that hardliners around the new supreme leader are more entrenched than ever, that Iran's public debate now leans toward lifting the fatwa on nuclear weapons. The problem that was solved became harder to solve.

Then Ukraine. Trump deflects energy prices onto Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries, demands Zelenskyy stand down, announces a 60-day energy truce neither side agreed to, then produces a Russian diesel deal so small no serious commentator bothered. Starlink access is cut and restored, and the White House, JD Vance, and Musk give three contradictory accounts of who decided what. This section is well reported—well enough to expose the mechanism: when a narrative starts failing, the operator does not fix the narrative. He finds a smaller one to hold the line. Zelenskyy is the smaller one.

The piece ends on hope: Europe is waking up, Gulf states are discussing a security pact that includes Iran, Freeland predicts countries will think hard before buying another F-35 controlled by the Pentagon. These are real loosening movements, but what they loosen is dependence on American technology and military hardware, not the narrative that made the war possible. European leaders blame Trump because they have to protect themselves, not because they intend to dismantle the necessity entry. The moment Macron pins responsibility on Washington, he is also telling the protesters: we are still using this narrative, we just did not press the button this time.

The report asks whether diplomacy can take back control. A better question comes first: who pays, and who signs. The signatories are preparing to welcome Putin at a golf club outside Miami. The payers are holding signs in Paris. The layer in between—the ones who sold the bombing as necessary, inflation as an external shock, and "we took no part" as an alibi—goes unnamed.

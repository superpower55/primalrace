厨房里的浪漫幻想与被隐形的劳动底色Romantic Fantasies in the Kitchen and the Invisible Color of Labor

性别 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-09-02 § 链接
所有被男性消费为“风情”的文化符号，本质上都是女性被剥削的劳动记录。
Every cultural symbol consumed by men as 'charm' is essentially a record of women's exploited labor.

Adrian Chiles 的这篇文章是一个典型的 masculine-centric narrative。他把一种名为 Ajvar 的巴尔干酱料当作一种社交筹码，试图通过这个“魔法词汇”与一名 13 岁女孩建立某种精神联结。在他眼中，Ajvar 是旅行的趣闻，是与当地人 bonding 的工具，是某种带有异域色彩的文化点缀。

然而，当他试图通过一次“业余尝试”来复刻这种味道时，他迅速地撞上了那道生物墙与结构墙。两个小时的烧烤，三个小时的搅拌，手指被烫伤，手臂酸痛。他在文中感叹那些马其顿奶奶们“一定是钢筋做的” (made of girders)，这句轻飘飘的赞美，恰恰揭示了他对 structural violence 的完全盲视。那些奶奶们不是天生钢筋做的，她们是被几代人的家务规训、被剥夺主体性的生存状态，强行地将身体异化成了生产工具。

这是一种极其典型的文化共谋：男性在公共空间享受这种文化符号带来的“趣味”和“情调”，而当这种趣味需要转化为具体的、枯燥的、具有身体损耗的劳动时，他们会迅速感到厌倦。Chiles 发现“烹饪时间越长，就越不想吃”，因为他拥有随时退出这场博弈的权力。但对于那些被定义为“家庭主妇”的女性来说，这种对身体的损耗没有退出机制，它是她们在父权结构中换取生存空间或家庭认可的唯一最优解表达。

这种将女性的生存苦难浪漫化为“传统风味”的叙事，就是一种文化暴力。它让人们在赞美“奶奶的手艺”时，心安理得地忽略了支撑这种手艺的，是长达数十年、在厨房这个私人领域中被隐形的、无偿的、且具有强迫性的劳动。

Adrian Chiles' piece is a textbook example of a masculine-centric narrative. He treats Ajvar, a Balkan condiment, as a social chip, using this 'magic word' to attempt a spiritual bond with a 13-year-old girl. To him, Ajvar is a travel anecdote, a tool for bonding, and a colorful cultural ornament.

However, the moment he attempts to replicate this taste through an 'amateur trial,' he crashes straight into the biological and structural walls. Two hours of grilling, three hours of stirring, singed fingers, and an aching arm. His casual remark that Macedonian nans must be 'made of girders' reveals his complete blindness to structural violence. Those grandmothers weren't born as steel; they were forged into production tools by generations of domestic discipline and the deprivation of their subjectivity.

This is a classic form of complicity: men enjoy the 'charm' and 'flavor' of these cultural symbols in the public sphere, but the moment that charm translates into concrete, tedious, and physically draining labor, they quickly grow weary. Chiles finds that 'the longer you cook something, the less you end up wanting to eat it' because he possesses the privilege to opt out of the game. For women defined as 'housewives,' there is no exit mechanism for this physical attrition; it is the only optimal expression available to them to exchange for survival or recognition within a patriarchal structure.

This narrative, which romanticizes the hardships of women as 'traditional flavor,' is a form of cultural violence. It allows people to praise 'grandmother's craft' while comfortably ignoring that the foundation of this craft is decades of invisible, unpaid, and coerced labor within the private sphere of the kitchen.

仇恨是他们的最后一张选票Hate Is Their Last Ballot

国际 文化层 · 元暴力 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要中期选举前，MAGA阵营散布中国干预选举的传言未成事实，压制投票的尝试也接连受挫：SAVE法案未过参议院，联邦调取选民数据27次被拦。共和党转而押注偏见动员：德州参议院选战针对民主党人Talarico散布性取向谣言，本轮反跨性别广告投入至少1.8亿美元，Trump称密歇根参议院候选人El-Sayed为「圣战分子」，众议员Ilhan Omar住所10月被突查。
The newsBefore the midterms, the MAGA movement's claims of Chinese election interference failed to materialize, and its voting-restriction push stalled: the SAVE Act died in the Senate, and federal attempts to seize voter data were blocked 27 times. The GOP is now betting on prejudice, including anti-trans ads costing at least $180 million and attacks on Muslim candidates.
当政策拿不出来，他们就开始卖偏见。
When the policy well runs dry, they sell prejudice instead.

先把结论说清楚：MAGA 在选举战场上拿不出任何像样的政策，于是把筹码全部押在偏见上。这不是我的修辞，是报道里一位长期跟踪选举否认运动的人的原话——他们「只是在重播自己的金曲」，没有新东西能动员人。

这个判断需要证据，报道给足了。夏天到秋天，Jerome Corsi 之流在 Rumble 上宣称中期选举前会冒出中共操纵选举的证据，Trump 将据此宣布国家紧急状态、接管选举、禁掉邮寄选票和投票机。结果什么都没发生。SAVE 法案在参议院没推进，联邦政府试图夺取选民敏感数据，在全国被拦下 27 次。邮寄投票照常进行。

压制投票这条路走不通，就得回头去拉票。而他们拉票的方式，是把偏见当成政策来卖。德州参议院选战里，包括副总统 JD Vance 在内的共和党人，毫无根据地暗示民主党人 James Talarico 是同性恋或某种说不清道不明的变态；本轮选举周期，反跨性别广告至少砸了 1.8 亿美元，重点炒跨性别女孩参加女子体育的「危险」；针对北卡众议员 Don Davis 的广告说他在「为 they/them 而战，不是为我们」，旁边配一个穿粉色舞会裙的变装皇后；Trump 把密歇根参议院候选人 Abdul El-Sayed 叫作「圣战分子」，还说要给他配个特殊金属探测器；众议员 Ilhan Omar 的住所 10 月被突查，这是剥夺她国籍、把她赶出去的一次种族主义行动的一部分。

这些不是零散的失言，是一套完整的表达武器。它的目的不是说服，是归类：把对手归进「变态」「恐怖分子」「外来者」的格子里，让选民不必去看任何一项政策。表达即身份的确立，在这里被反过来用——不是确立自己是谁，是强行规定对手是谁。

报道里最值得注意的一句话，是那位观察者说的：右翼媒体现在「基本上没有提出任何一套肯定的政策选项」。他拿 Steve Bannon 当年那套「大规模驱逐能压低房价」作对比——那套说辞是种族主义的，但至少还试图把仇恨翻译成一个民粹主义的经济承诺。现在连这层翻译都省了，只剩下「这些语无伦次的愤怒表达」。

这说明什么？说明这套机器已经不需要理由了。它不需要证明移民推高了房价，不需要证明跨性别女孩威胁了谁，不需要证明 El-Sayed 是圣战分子。它只需要一个可以被恨的对象，和一个愿意为恨投票的选民。这是共谋者理论最赤裸的形态：规则制定者从中获利，投票者以为自己是在保卫什么。

但报道也给出了一个不能忽略的信号：这套东西是虚弱的。选举否认运动「非常混乱」，压制投票「彻底失败」，连他们自己人都承认这是在「胡乱挥舞」。一个真正有把握的阵营，不需要靠造谣对手进过精神病院来拉票。

问题在于，虚弱不等于无害。1.8 亿美元买来的偏见，就算买不到多数票，也会在选后留下一个已经被规训过的 electorate——他们被告知，跨性别孩子是威胁，穆斯林是敌人，同性恋是某种需要被暗示的污点。这笔账不会随着投票日结束而清掉。

所以真正的赌注不是这次选举谁赢。是他们赌输了之后，这些被制造出来的恨，由谁来收拾。

Let me put the conclusion first: MAGA has no policy to run on in this election, so it is betting everything on prejudice. That is not my rhetoric; it is the assessment of someone who tracks the election-denial movement for a living, quoted in the report—they are "just playing their greatest hits," with nothing new to energize anyone.

The evidence is all there. Over the summer and into the fall, figures like Jerome Corsi went on Rumble claiming proof would surface before the midterms that the Chinese Communist Party rigged past elections. On that basis, Trump would declare a national emergency, seize control of elections, ban mail ballots and voting machines. None of it happened. The SAVE Act failed in the Senate. Federal attempts to seize sensitive voter data were blocked 27 times nationwide. Mail-in voting continues.

With suppression stalled, they have to actually appeal to voters. And the way they appeal is by selling prejudice as policy. In the Texas Senate race, Republicans including Vice President JD Vance baselessly suggested Democrat James Talarico is gay or some unspecified pervert. At least $180 million has gone into anti-trans ads this cycle, fixating on the supposed danger of trans girls in girls' sports. An ad against Congressman Don Davis says he is "fighting for they/them, not us," with a drag queen in a pink ball gown placed next to his image. Trump called Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed a "jihadist" and said he would need a special metal detector. Congresswoman Ilhan Omar's home was raided in October as part of a racist push to strip her citizenship and deport her.

These are not scattered slips. They are a complete weaponization of expression, and its purpose is not persuasion but classification: sorting opponents into the boxes of "pervert," "terrorist," "outsider," so voters never have to look at a single policy. Expression establishes identity—here it is used in reverse, not to declare who you are but to dictate who your opponent is.

The most telling line in the report is that right-wing media has "basically no affirmative set of policy options" right now. The comparison is Steve Bannon's old claim that mass deportation would lower housing costs—racist, yes, but at least an attempt to translate hate into a populist economic promise. Now even that translation is gone. What remains are "inchoate expressions of rage."

What does that tell us? The machine no longer needs a reason. It does not need to prove immigrants raise housing costs, that trans girls threaten anyone, that El-Sayed is a jihadist. It needs a target to hate and voters willing to vote for hate. This is co-conspiracy in its barest form: the rule-makers profit, and the voters believe they are defending something.

But the report also carries a signal that should not be ignored: this thing is weak. The election-denial movement is "very disorganized," suppression has been a "complete failure," and even its own people admit they are flailing. A confident coalition does not need to invent a story about its opponent being confined to a mental hospital.

Weak is not harmless, though. $180 million of prejudice, even if it fails to buy a majority, leaves behind an electorate that has been trained—told that trans children are a threat, that Muslims are enemies, that being gay is an accusation. That bill does not come due on election night.

The real wager is not who wins. It is who cleans up the hate after they lose.

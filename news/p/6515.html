哥斯拉踩过银座，谁在鼓掌Godzilla Stomps Ginza, and the Crowd Applauds

文娱 文化层 The Hollywood Reporter ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
怪兽片的能量，来自战后废墟的账还没算清
The monster film's energy comes from a war debt nobody has settled

《哥斯拉负零》在东京电影节放了两场，票卖光了，散场时观众一反常态地热烈鼓掌，笑着聊最喜欢的怪兽场面，从银座走出来——银座正是片子里被踩烂的地方。这条新闻写得很兴奋。我看的时候想问一句：他们鼓的是掌，还是战后那笔账。

报道自己交代了背景：故事从1949年接起，日本在战争的废墟和哥斯拉的袭击里重建，敷岛家好不容易得来的平静被新的灾难打破。哥斯拉从来不是一只蜥蜴，它是战后日本唯一被允许拿来装灾难的容器。战争里死掉的人、被烧掉的城市、被原子弹抹平的两座城，在1949年的日本没法直接说，于是全部塞进一只从海里爬上来的怪兽身上。这是第三章说的那件事：叙事是武器，认知入口决定谁能定义什么是灾难、什么是重建、什么是值得被同情的受害者。怪兽片是这套叙事里最温和、最好看的一种。

所以银座的掌声是有意思的。银幕上被踩碎的是银座，银幕下鼓掌的人从银座走出来。灾难被消费成了一场安全的体验——你可以为它激动，为它流泪，为它买票，然后毫发无伤地回家。这不是罪。但报道里没有一个人提到，那只怪兽脚下踩着的是什么。

再看演员在台上说的话。神木隆之介说一家人“通常会让我们更强，但也会让我们更脆弱”，说面对哥斯拉时必须保护家人，因此要做“非常艰难的决定”。这句话放在一部1949年的日本电影里，是干净的。放在2026年一部全球发行的怪兽大片里，它也是干净的——干净到不需要追问那些“艰难的决定”是谁做的、代价落在谁身上。家庭作为叙事单位，从来是最好用的：它把结构问题缩小成一家人的取舍，把政治变成亲情，把观众的情绪调到最柔软的地方，然后收票钱。

报道里值得记一笔的是数字：前作《哥斯拉-1.0》北美票房5710万美元，是日本真人电影在北美最高的一部，还拿了奥斯卡最佳视觉效果，对手是预算大得多的好莱坞大片。这部续集在烂番茄52篇影评92%。这些是硬事实，说明山崎贵确实做成了一件事——用远小于好莱坞的钱，做出让好莱坞难看的特效。这件事本身值得尊重。

但一部怪兽片的成功，和它讲了什么，是两件事。北美11月6日上映，IMAX提前场11月3日。票会卖得很好。散场时也会有人鼓掌。

那只怪兽还会再爬上来一次。它脚下的废墟，还是没人打算认真算。

Godzilla Minus Zero played two screenings at the Tokyo International Film Festival, both sold out, and the crowd applauded with uncharacteristic rapture, smiling and chattering about favorite monster moments as they poured out into Ginza — the district that gets flattened on screen. The report is excited. I kept asking: what are they applauding, the film or the unsettled bill from the war?

The report supplies the frame itself: the story picks up in 1949, Japan rebuilding from the devastation of war and Godzilla's attack, the Shikishima family's hard-won peace threatened by a new calamity. Godzilla was never a lizard. It is the one container postwar Japan was permitted to pour its disaster into. The dead, the burned cities, the two cities erased by atomic bombs — none of that could be said directly in 1949, so it all went into a monster crawling up out of the sea. This is the mechanism from Chapter 3: narrative is a weapon, and the cognitive entry point decides who gets to define what counts as catastrophe, as rebuilding, as a victim worth mourning. The kaiju film is the gentlest, best-looking version of that operation.

Which makes the applause in Ginza interesting. What gets crushed on screen is Ginza; the people applauding walk out into Ginza. Disaster is consumed as a safe experience — you can be thrilled by it, cry at it, buy a ticket to it, and go home unharmed. That is not a crime. But nobody in this report mentions what is under the monster's feet.

Then the actors on stage. Kamiki says family usually makes us stronger but can also make us vulnerable, that facing Godzilla they must protect the family and that leads to hard decisions. In a 1949-set Japanese film, that line is clean. In a 2026 global monster tentpole, it is also clean — clean enough that nobody has to ask who makes those hard decisions and who pays for them. The family as narrative unit is the most serviceable thing there is: it shrinks structural questions into one household's trade-offs, turns politics into kinship, tunes the audience to its softest frequency, and collects the ticket money.

The numbers in the report are worth keeping: the predecessor grossed $57.1 million in North America, the highest ever for a live-action Japanese film there, and won the visual effects Oscar against Hollywood tentpoles with vastly bigger budgets. The sequel sits at 92 percent on Rotten Tomatoes from 52 reviews. These are hard facts, and they mean Yamazaki pulled something off — Hollywood-grade effects on a fraction of Hollywood's money. That deserves respect.

But a monster film succeeding and a monster film saying something are two different things. North America opens Nov. 6, with Imax early access Nov. 3. Tickets will sell. People will applaud on the way out.

The monster will crawl up again. The rubble under its feet still isn't being counted.

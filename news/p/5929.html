海滩是公共的，直到它被卖出去The Beach Is Public Until It's Sold

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
殖民法还在替酒店看门，法院先掀开了一条缝
A colonial law still guards the hotels. A court just cracked the door.

十月的禁令只是让政府暂停封锁 Little Dunn's River，案子还在打，政府已经说要上诉。这不是胜利，是一条缝。但缝也是缝：法官承认了牙买加人应该有进入的权利，而 1956 年的《海滩控制法》从来没有承认过这件事。

Samuda 的辩护值得逐句看。他说封锁是因为一起谋杀案，说这是“有管理的开放”，周五到周日免费。Taylor 的反驳很干脆：1956 年的法律早于牙买加的犯罪率高峰，从来不是治安工具；四年过去，谋杀案没破，海滩只在周末开门。用一桩没破的案子给全体国民上锁，这叫集体惩罚，不叫安全管理。

再看第二层辩护：176,000 人靠旅游业吃饭，所以别把旅游业当敌人。这句话的结构是：把一部分人的饭碗举起来，压住另一部分人的权利，然后说你在替前者说话。可这两件事本来不冲突。Buzo 举了澳大利亚和巴巴多斯——酒店可以有海景房，门前照样是公共海滩。冲突不是酒店和海滩之间的，是“少数人认为自己需要的那种排他”和所有人之间的。

Taylor 用了“plantation tourism”这个词，这不是修辞。1956 年的法把海岸线交给国家，国家再把它交给 luxury hotelier，本地人被挡在门外，还得买高价餐饮才能进去——因为园内禁止外带食物。殖民时期划的是种植园的地界，现在划的是度假村的地界，站在界外的是同一批人的后代。法律换了封面，内页没换。

这条新闻里真正在动的，是结构层。法院的禁令是结构暴力第一次被撬动：不是靠呼吁，是靠诉讼，靠 Jabbem 从 2020 年组织到现在，靠 Carr 这样的七十三岁老人一场一场地打。文化层也在动——“plantation tourism”这个说法把“旅游业是大家的饭碗”这套叙事翻过来用了一次，让排他性第一次被叫出名字。

但缝的另一边站着共谋者。政府说要上诉，三月那份“海滩准入与管理政策”被 Rodriguez-Moodie 直接说成没解决问题，Taylor 说它仍然允许不可接受的限制。立法可以写得很漂亮，执行可以一点都不动。禁令是临时的，案子还在走，上诉已经排上队。

还有一层没人提：谁的名字没出现在这篇报道里。176,000 这个数字被反复举起来当挡箭牌，但没有一个旅游业从业者本人说话。Samuda 替他们说话， activists 被指为“把行业当敌人”。真正在酒店后厨和沙滩上干活的人，既没被问过要不要公共海滩，也没被问过工作条件——Taylor 说的“untenable working conditions”在报道里只有这五个词，没有细节。

禁令保护的是 Little Dunn's River，不是牙买加的海岸线。下一个案子在 Portland，再下一个不知道在哪里。法院给了一条缝，政府正在把它缝回去。

October's injunction only pauses the government's closure of Little Dunn's River. The case is still running, and the government has said it will appeal. This is not a victory. It is a crack. But a crack is a crack: a judge has now conceded that Jamaicans should have access, something the 1956 Beach Control Act has never conceded.

Samuda's defense deserves to be read line by line. Access was restricted after a murder, he says; this is "structured management," free on Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays. Taylor's rebuttal is clean: the 1956 act predates the crime wave and was never a crime-control tool; four years on, the murder is unsolved and the beach opens on weekends. Locking out an entire nation over one unsolved case is collective punishment, not security management.

Then the second layer: 176,000 people depend on tourism, so don't treat the industry as the enemy. The structure of that sentence is to lift up one group's paychecks and press down another group's rights, then claim to be speaking for the first. The two don't actually conflict. Buzo points to Australia and Barbados, where hotels front onto public beaches and tourism thrives. The conflict isn't between hotels and beaches. It's between the exclusivity a small number of people believe they need and everyone else.

Taylor's phrase "plantation tourism" is not a metaphor. The 1956 law handed the foreshore to the state, the state handed it to luxury hoteliers, locals were shut out and made to buy overpriced food and drink on site because outside food is banned. The plantation drew a boundary; the resort draws the same boundary. The people standing outside it are the descendants of the same people. The law changed its cover. Not its pages.

What is actually moving here is the structural layer. The injunction is the first time structural violence has been pried open, and it wasn't pried open by appeals. It was pried open by litigation, by Jabbem organizing since 2020, by a 73-year-old man fighting case after case. The cultural layer moves too: "plantation tourism" flips the "tourism feeds us all" narrative and uses it against itself, naming the exclusion out loud for once.

On the other side of the crack stand the co-conspirators. The government will appeal. The March policy was described by Rodriguez-Moodie as not addressing the issues, and by Taylor as still permitting unacceptable restrictions. Legislation can be written beautifully and enforced not at all. The injunction is temporary. The case is ongoing. The appeal is already queued.

And one layer goes unmentioned: whose names are absent. The number 176,000 is raised again and again as a shield, but not a single tourism worker speaks in this piece. Samuda speaks for them. Activists are accused of treating the industry as the enemy. The people actually working the hotel kitchens and the sand were never asked whether they want a public beach, and never asked about working conditions. Taylor's "untenable working conditions" gets five words in the report and no detail.

The injunction protects Little Dunn's River. Not Jamaica's coastline. The next case is in Portland. The one after that, nobody knows. The court opened a crack. The government is stitching it shut.

一千名联邦监票员和四百英尺A Thousand Federal Monitors and Four Hundred Feet

国际 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
当监票变成恐吓，选举就成了表演
When poll-watching becomes intimidation, the election becomes theater.

联邦政府今年要在五十个州派大约一千名监票员。2022年中期选举是289名，2024年大选是714名。数字在涨，涨得很有节奏。

司法部说ICE和FBI的人不会去，去的是ATF和DEA的武装执法人员——不带枪，但带制服，带徽章，带那种不需要开口就能让人后退半步的东西。密歇根州英厄姆县的书记员给手下加了一轮培训，告诉他们：联邦的人可以看，但不能碰任何东西，不能问任何问题。这句话本身就是在承认，来的人不是来帮忙的。

加州有法律，选举日投票站两百英尺内一般不许抓人。联邦法律也禁止派武装执法者去投票站。但法律写在那里，和有人站在两百英尺外看着你走进去，是两件事。圣克拉拉县的培训里，律师告诉志愿者：如果你紧张，退到安全距离，观察，记录，上报。一个七十岁的退休法学教授说他读了新闻，不意外。一个做了四年监票志愿者的人说，这居然成了一个议题，有点难过，有点不安。

真正在动的是哪一层，很清楚。不是有人被打，至少报道里没有。是有人被看着。ICE的探员在多个州挨家敲门，查移民有没有不当登记投票，还把过程拍成视频发到社交媒体上。保护民主项目的律师说这是教科书式的选民恐吓，联邦法院已经下令让他们在选举日之前停手。北加州一个草根组织记录了十月在萨特县选举办公室外停着的六辆ICE的车。县登记官说不觉得那些人在恐吓谁，国土安全部说他们得到了选举办公室一位代表的许可，停在那里等一个人从法院出来。但一个有色人种看到了他们，吓坏了，打电话求助，其中一个探员下车嘲弄了她的志愿者。

恐吓不需要真的动手。它只需要让某一类人在走向投票站之前多想三秒钟：我会不会被记下来，我的地址会不会被查，我家里有没有人经不起查。这三秒钟就是差额。Violence = Potential − Actual，本来可以投的票没有投，本来可以出现的选民没有出现，差额落在谁身上，报道写得很清楚——是被敲门的那群人，是被盯上的那群人。

再往下一层，是解释权。司法部起诉加州选举官员，要求不计算两万张它声称可能属于非公民的选票。联邦上诉法院周五维持了地方法院的裁定，说批准这个请求会“造成混乱并扰乱已经在进行的选举”，还说政府很可能用了不可靠的数据来夸大非公民选民的数量。在两份裁定之间，司法部做了一件事：向州选举官员发出一批大陪审团传票。法院赢了，节奏没停。阿拉斯加那边，司法部提前起诉，要求如果共和党候选人输了，不得认证参议院选举结果。

大卫·贝克尔说，美国历史上第一次，联邦政府成了州和地方选举官员的对手，而不是伙伴。他说这些都是噪音，都在失败。他可能是对的，法院确实在挡。但噪音的功能从来不是赢，是让人怀疑计数本身。一个被反复起诉、反复传唤、反复宣布有舞弊的选举，就算最后结果被认证，也会有一半人不认。这不是在改变事实，是在改变事实被描绘的方式。第三章那套东西：夺取话语权，夺取解释权，夺取什么是事实的制造权。

志愿者在签一份跨州声援的承诺书，说如果政府在任何一个地方越过红线——抢选票，或者拒绝认证——他们就去抗议。几乎所有人都签了。这是共谋，但是反过来的那种：底层的、跨州的、没有钱权势的共谋，用身份政治的方式——我们是监票员，我们是选民，我们是被敲门的那群人——把分散的人组织成一个能站住的势。

报道里没有出现的是另一群人：那些没有志愿者守着的县，那些不知道可以打电话求助的人，那些被敲了门、被拍了视频、从此不再去投票的人。他们的名字不在任何一份培训名单上。差额就落在那里，没人去量。

The federal government is deploying about 1,000 poll monitors across all 50 states this year. In 2022 it sent 289. In 2024, 714. The number is climbing, and it is climbing on purpose.

The DOJ says ICE and FBI officers won't be among them—what's coming is armed personnel from ATF and DEA. No guns, but uniforms, badges, the kind of thing that makes a person step back half a pace without being asked. The clerk of Ingham County, Michigan, gave her election workers extra training to tell them: the feds can watch, but they can't touch anything, they can't ask any questions. That sentence is itself an admission that the people arriving are not there to help.

California has a law: on Election Day, officers generally can't make arrests within 200 feet of a polling place. Federal law forbids ordering armed law enforcement to the polls. But a law on the books and a man standing outside the 200-foot line watching you walk in are two different things. At the Santa Clara County training, the attorney told volunteers: if you're nervous, retreat to a safe distance, observe, report. A 70-year-old retired law professor said he read the news, so he wasn't surprised. A four-year volunteer said it's a little sad and disturbing that this is even an issue.

Which layer is moving here is clear. Nobody is being beaten—not in this report. People are being watched. ICE agents went door to door in multiple states investigating whether immigrants were improperly registered to vote, and posted videos of the effort on social media. An attorney at the Protect Democracy Project calls it a textbook case of voter intimidation; a federal court has ordered the agency to stop, at least until after Election Day. A grassroots group in Northern California documented six cars of ICE agents outside the Sutter County elections office in October. The county registrar said she didn't believe they were trying to intimidate anyone; DHS said they had permission from a representative of the elections office to park there while waiting for a man to exit a courthouse. But a person of color saw them, was terrified, and called for help—and one of the officers got out and taunted her volunteer.

Intimidation doesn't need to touch anyone. It only needs to make a certain kind of person pause for three seconds before walking to the polls: will I be recorded, will my address be checked, does someone in my house have something to lose. Those three seconds are the gap. Violence = Potential − Actual. The vote that could have been cast isn't cast; the voter who could have shown up doesn't. Who pays that gap is spelled out in the report—the people who got knocked on, the people who got watched.

One layer down is the question of who gets to define the facts. The DOJ sued California elections officials, seeking to stop them from counting 20,000 ballots it claims may belong to noncitizens. On Friday a federal appeals court upheld the district court's ruling that granting the request "would wreak havoc and disrupt the election that is already underway," and that the administration was likely using unreliable data to inflate the number of noncitizen voters. Between the two rulings, the DOJ escalated by sending a spate of grand jury subpoenas to state elections officials. The court won; the tempo didn't stop. In Alaska, the DOJ sued preemptively to block certification of a Senate race if the Republican candidate loses.

David Becker says that for the first time in American history, the feds have become an adversary to state and local election officials rather than a partner. He says it's all noise, and it's failing. He may be right; the courts are holding. But noise was never meant to win. It's meant to make people distrust the count itself. An election sued over, subpoenaed, and repeatedly declared fraudulent—even when the result is certified, half the country won't accept it. This isn't changing the facts. It's changing how the facts get described. That's the third chapter's whole toolkit: seize the discourse, seize the interpretation, seize the power to manufacture what counts as fact.

Volunteers are signing an interstate pledge to protest if the administration crosses any red lines anywhere—seizing ballots, refusing to certify. Almost everyone signed. That's a conspiracy too, but the other kind: bottom-up, cross-state, no money and no power, using identity politics the way it's supposed to work—we are the poll monitors, we are the voters, we are the people who got knocked on—to turn scattered individuals into a force that can hold.

What the report doesn't show is the other group: the counties with no volunteers watching, the people who don't know they can call for help, the ones who got knocked on, got filmed, and will never go vote again. Their names aren't on any training list. The gap sits right there, and nobody is measuring it.

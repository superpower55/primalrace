骗局卖的不是书，是你的渴望The Scam Sells Your Hunger, Not Your Book

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
骗局最锋利的地方，是让你自己交出判断力
A scam's sharpest edge is making you hand over your own judgment

一个作者收到假书友会的邀请，被要求交“参与费”，她去了那场 Zoom，二十个不开摄像头的人，用西非口音扮演西雅图的白人。这不是新闻里最贵的骗局，却是最精确的：它知道写作者要什么。

要的不是钱，是被读。WritersWeekly 的 Hoy 说得很直白：作者都在等“有人读自己的文字”。诈骗产业就是从这里下手的。先给你一段像模像样的赞美，再让你为这段赞美付钱。AI 让赞美变得有层次、像专门为你写的，于是连写过十年跨国诈骗报道的作者，也点了接受邀请。

这不是贪心，是匮乏。一个人把自己的表达交出去，等一个回声，回声迟迟不来，于是任何声称听见了的人，都拿到了入口。第三章讲的认知入口，在这里被用到了极致：骗子不伪造事实，他们伪造的是“有人在乎”。

再看组织形态。巴基斯坦的假出版社在卡拉奇和拉合尔有实体办公室，雇二十到二十四岁的毕业生，月薪约五万卢比，按销售额拿一到两成提成，Excel 表格里给四百零三个作者分门别类：新作者、食谱作者、宗教作者、调查报道作者、纽约时报畅销书作者。有人一单能做出六万美元。这是公司，不是几个散兵游勇。

所以别把这件事讲成“受害者太天真”。这是结构：传统出版的门槛高得离谱，作者被挡在外面，假出版社就花钱买 Google 搜索结果，让 penguinwriters.com 排在真正的自助出版平台前面。门越窄，门外的人越多，门口的叫卖声就越好卖。

最狠的一层，是共谋的闭环。M L Stark 付了大约十万美元，拿到的是 IngramSpark 上架、两张书封、时代广场的一块数字广告牌——她为那块牌子付了一万美元。她后来才明白，这些“交付”是战略性的让步，每次都在她撑不住、要退款的时候才放出来。这不是骗局失败，是骗局设计。给一点真的，好让你继续相信剩下的都是真的。

而执行的人呢？Rae 有法律学位，毕业后找不到法律工作，第一份工作就是假出版社。她说服自己：赚美元，生活就稳。被骗的人在美国，被雇的人也在被这套汇率差剥削，只是方向相反。

书里说，一切暴力始于解释权的垄断。这里被垄断的解释是：你的书值得被看见。骗子没有制造这个渴望，他们只是把它标了价。

最后那个细节最刺人。作者问那二十个人，有没有人想聊聊她书的最后一章——那一章写的就是德里的假呼叫中心，写年轻人怎么被吸进诈骗业。沉默了一会儿，Timothy 开麦说：“这在全世界都在发生，包括非洲。”

她只说了这一句。她大概真的读过。

An author gets an invitation from a fake book club, is asked for a "contribution fee", and joins the Zoom: twenty people with cameras off, West African accents playing white Seattleites. It is not the most expensive scam in the story. It is the most precise. It knows what writers want.

Not money. To be read. Hoy of WritersWeekly says it plainly: writers are waiting for somebody to read their writing. The scam industry starts exactly there. First a paragraph of praise that sounds like it was written for you, then a bill for that praise. AI makes the compliments layered and personal, which is why even an author who has spent ten years reporting on transnational fraud clicks accept.

This is not greed. It is hunger. A person puts their expression into the world and waits for an echo, and the echo does not come, so anyone who claims to have heard it gets an entry point. Chapter three's cognitive entrance, used at full strength: the scammers do not forge facts, they forge the feeling that somebody cares.

Then look at the organisation. Fake publishing houses in Karachi and Lahore keep physical offices and hire graduates aged twenty to twenty-four at about 50,000 rupees a month, with a 10-20% commission per sale, upselling until the author backs out. One spreadsheet sorts 403 authors into categories: new author, cookbook author, religious author, investigative author, New York Times bestselling author. One client can be worth $60,000. This is a company, not a few cowboys.

So do not tell this as a story about gullible authors. It is structural. Traditional publishing is gatekept to the point of absurdity, authors are shut outside, and the fake houses pay thousands to buy Google results so penguinwriters.com ranks above real self-publishing platforms. The narrower the gate, the bigger the crowd outside it, and the louder the hawkers get.

The cruelest layer is the co-conspiracy loop. M L Stark paid about $100,000 and received IngramSpark listings, two covers, and a digital billboard in Times Square, for which she paid $10,000. Only later did she see these deliveries as strategic concessions, released each time she reached the end of her rope and asked for her money back. That is not a scam failing. That is a scam designed. Give a little real so the rest keeps looking real.

And the people running it? Rae has a law degree, could not find law work after university, and her first job was at a fake publishing company. She told herself: earn in dollars and life is stable. The people being defrauded are in America; the people doing the defrauding are also being squeezed by the same exchange rate, just from the other side.

The book says all violence begins with a monopoly on interpretation. The interpretation monopolised here is: your book deserves to be seen. The scammers did not invent that hunger. They put a price on it.

The last detail cuts deepest. The author asks the twenty people whether anyone wants to discuss the final chapter of her book, the one about fake call centres in Delhi, about young people pulled into the fraud industry. Silence. Then Timothy unmutes.

"It is happening all over the world," she says, "including in Africa."

That is all she has to say. She probably really did read it.

用“道德十字军”掩盖的结构性掠夺The 'Moral Crusade' as a Mask for Structural Predation

哲学 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-09-27 § 链接
当权力开始谈论“道德责任”时，通常意味着它准备开始剥夺。
When power starts talking about 'moral responsibility,' it usually means it's preparing to strip away rights.

Pat McFadden 把削减福利描述为一场“道德十字军” (moral crusade)，这套叙事逻辑极其经典：先定义一个“陷阱”（年轻人被困在福利中），再将剥夺资源的行为包装成“拯救”。这本质上是典型的武器化表达 (weaponized expression)，通过夺取认知入口，将结构性暴力 (structural violence) 伪装成人道主义关怀。

所谓的“帮助年轻人重返职场”，在实际操作中往往变成了对残障群体和精神健康受损者的二次伤害。注意到文中提到的 ADHD 和自闭症申请人数激增，这在元暴力 (meta violence) 的视角下，其实是年轻人试图在僵化的社会结构中寻找生存底线的生物墙防御。而政府的反应不是去改善让这些群体能够生存的结构，而是通过修改规则，让“不符合标准”的人失去救济。这不是在“改变生活”，而是在通过削减 Actual 资源，强行扩大 Potential 与 Actual 之间的暴力差额。

最讽刺的是，这种“道德责任”的共谋者包括了那些自称进步的政党。他们通过借用丹麦或荷兰的模式，试图在不触动权力结构的前提下，用“灵活就业”这种词汇来掩盖对弱势群体的压榨。当政府开始询问“我们如何改变你的生活”而不是“你拥有什么权利”时，权利 (entitlement) 已经从一项制度保障变成了某种需要通过“表现良好”才能换取的恩赐。

这场“十字军”真正的目标不是救赎，而是账单。一个将生存权与“工作能力”强行挂钩的系统，永远无法达成公正的表达 (just expressions)，因为它在定义“谁值得生存”的权力上，依然垄断在那些制定规则的男性中心叙事者手中。

Pat McFadden frames the overhaul of the welfare system as a “moral crusade.” This is a textbook example of weaponized expression: first, define a “trap” (young people stuck on benefits), then package the act of stripping resources as “salvation.” It is a calculated move to mask structural violence as humanitarian care by seizing the cognitive entry point of the narrative.

The claim of “helping young people back to work” is, in practice, a secondary assault on the disabled and those with mental health struggles. The surge in PIP claims for ADHD and autism is, from the perspective of meta violence, a biological wall of defense—an attempt by young people to secure a survival baseline within a rigid social structure. Instead of improving the structure to accommodate these needs, the government seeks to modify the rules to disqualify the “non-compliant.” This isn't “changing lives”; it is deliberately widening the gap in the Violence Triangle by slashing actual resources.

Most cynical is the complicity of a party that claims to be progressive. By borrowing models from Denmark or the Netherlands, they attempt to use terms like “flexible jobs” to camouflage the exploitation of the vulnerable without touching the power structure. When the state shifts from asking “what are you entitled to” to “how do we change your life,” rights are no longer institutional guarantees—they become favors granted to those who perform “productivity” correctly.

This “crusade” is not about redemption; it is about the balance sheet. A system that ties the right to exist to “employability” can never achieve just expressions, because the power to define “who is worthy of survival” remains monopolized by those operating within a masculine-centric narrative.

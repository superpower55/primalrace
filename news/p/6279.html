河岸被占，草料靠抢The Riverbank Was Taken First

其他 结构层 · 文化层 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
旱灾不是天灾，是谁能先占到河岸的分配问题
Drought is not weather. It is a fight over who reaches the riverbank first.

马拉维的牛在变瘦，不是因为雨没下，是因为河岸被种上了菜。加尔通的公式里，这场干旱的暴力不在天上，在地上：本可以长出的草、本可以蓄住的水，被谁先占了。

报道里最锋利的一句话来自 Khandalawo，他自己养牛，也看着别人在河岸上种菜。他说以前河岸有芦苇有草，现在没有了，因为种菜的人一直种到水边，水干得更快，草也更少。他要的是回到从前那条不许越过几米的界线。这不是气候叙事，这是产权叙事：河岸是公共的缓冲带，谁先伸手谁拿走，拿走的代价由下游所有养牛的人分摊。

于是 Abison 和三个朋友天亮前从 27 公里外出发，走两小时，割够十头牛吃一天的草，捆好，扛上坡，再回来。他们割的不是野草，是别人家河岸上仅剩的植被，是和当地做席子的芦苇抢同一片绿色。旱季提前了，路线变长了，竞争者也多了，这就是结构暴力最朴素的形态：没有施暴者，只有一条被所有人踩过去的路。

钱在这条路上倒着流。Khandalawo 正常一个月卖奶能拿一百多万克瓦查，约 577 美元，最近大部分又贴回农场，付给割草的人，买玉米糠。玉米糠也涨了，因为玉米收成差。奶产量下来了，因为牛吃得少、吃得晚。一个合作社四百户农民，能交换的只有怎么把玉米秆和豌豆残渣留下来过荒。这不是适应，这是把亏损一层层往下压，压到最没有议价权的那一端。

Saulo 的处境把这条链的末端露了出来。她养公牛，一年后卖掉，三头母牛在变瘦。她付两个男孩各五万克瓦查帮忙，他们病了两天没来。她等雨。她说动物在吃、看起来健康的时候她也高兴，现在她难过，因为动物饿着，她给不了吃的。这句话里没有气候模型，只有一个女人和她喂不饱的牲口。

报道没有写的是：河岸上的菜是谁在种，种给谁，卖到哪里，有没有人管。Khandalawo 说“如果可能，我们希望恢复以前的规定”，这句“如果可能”就是全部答案——规定曾经存在，执行不在了。旱灾只是把这条早已松掉的界线照得更清楚。

真正被缩小的差额，不是降雨量，是河岸那几米的执法。雨会来，草会长，但界线不回来，明年 Abison 还要走更远的路。

Malawi's cattle are thinning not because rain failed, but because riverbanks were planted with vegetables. In Galtung's formula, the violence here is not in the sky. It is on the ground: the grass that could have grown, the water that could have stayed, taken by whoever got there first.

The sharpest line in the report comes from Khandalawo, a cattle owner who watches others cultivate the banks. He says there used to be reeds and grass; now there is none, because vegetable growers plant right up to the water, the river dries faster, and fodder disappears. What he asks for is a return to the old rule: no cultivation within a few metres of the bank. This is not a climate story. It is a property story. The riverbank is a public buffer, and whoever reaches in first takes it, while everyone downstream pays.

So Abison and three friends leave Nsanjama at dawn, 27km away, cut for two hours, gather enough grass for ten cattle for one day, tie it, carry it uphill, come back for more. They are not harvesting wild grass. They are competing with the people who make mats from the same reeds, on the same shrinking green. The dry season arrived early, the route got longer, the competitors multiplied. That is structural violence at its plainest: no single perpetrator, just a path everyone is forced to walk.

Money flows backwards along that path. In a normal month Khandalawo makes over one million Malawi kwacha, about $577, from milk. Lately most of it goes back into the farm, paying fodder gatherers and buying maize bran, which is scarce because the maize harvest was poor. Milk production falls because the animals eat less and eat late. A cooperative of up to 400 farmers can only trade notes on storing maize stalks and pea remains for the lean weeks. That is not adaptation. That is loss pushed down the chain to whoever has the least bargaining power.

Saulo shows where the chain ends. She raises bulls to sell after about a year; her three cows are getting thinner. She pays two boys about MK50,000 each to help; they have been ill and absent for two days. She waits for rain. She says she is happy when the animals eat and look healthy, and now she is sad, because they are hungry and she cannot feed them. No climate model in that sentence. Just a woman and livestock she cannot feed.

The report does not say who is planting on the banks, for whom, where the vegetables go, or who enforces anything. Khandalawo says if possible they want the old rule back. That "if possible" is the whole answer: the rule existed once, the enforcement is gone. The drought did not create this. It only made the loosened line visible.

The gap that could be closed is not rainfall. It is those few metres of riverbank and the will to police them. Rain will come and grass will grow, but if the line does not return, Abison walks farther next year.

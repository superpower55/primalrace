74 Spills, Same as Last Year, Bonuses Intact74 Spills, Same as Last Year, Bonuses Intact

其他 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
Privatisation sold the rivers; the bill goes to whoever pays the water rate

Seventy-four serious pollution incidents. One fewer than last year. After billions of pounds of investment, a ban on bosses' bonuses and a public outcry, eight of England's nine water companies are rated "poor" or "requiring improvement," and the industry scored 16 stars out of a possible 36 — the lowest since the assessment began (source: The Guardian). This is not governance failing. This is governance working exactly as designed.

Start with the structural layer. The UK is one of only two countries in the world where water supply is fully privatised. The other is Chile, where it was imposed in the 1980s by a military dictatorship. That is not a rhetorical flourish; it is a coordinate. Hand water to companies whose job is shareholder return, and rivers become a cost line, and outflows become an accounting item you defer. England had 2,666 pollution incidents in 2025, a 40% increase on 2016 (source: The Guardian). Thames Water worst, then Anglian, United Utilities, Yorkshire, Southern. Ten years, forty percent. Not an accident. An output of the incentive structure.

Environment Secretary Angela Eagle summoned the bosses to Whitehall and said people are sick of excuses, that root-and-branch reform cannot wait. What is the reform? Defra promises "a clear set of improvement actions": tackling leakage, reducing pollution incidents, improving customer satisfaction. That reads like a KPI list, not accountability. The rule that took effect last year blocks bonuses for executives at companies receiving a one-star rating — the regulator assigns the star, the company pays the bonus, and between them sit a board, a remuneration committee and an entire vocabulary for renaming a bonus. Banning bonuses and banning pollution are separated by enforcement.

Read the industry body's response line by line. Water UK says more than half of last year's pollution incidents were caused by blockages or vandalism, "not in the direct control of the water companies." What about the other half? And then: record investment "will help" end sewage entering rivers and seas. Billions in, one incident fewer out, and the same sentence keeps promising that investment will solve it. Failure packaged as a progress bar, with time serving as the liability waiver.

The people paying the cost were not at the Whitehall table. The bill payers. The people who live by the river. The people who drink from it. James Wallace of River Action put it plainly: this is a system that rewards shareholders and creditors while rivers, wildlife and bill payers lose out. He wants Thames Water in special administration immediately, an end to the failed privatisation experiment, a review of alternatives including public ownership, and regulators empowered to make polluters pay rather than the public. Those words are in the report. They are not in the minister's action list.

Four agencies pooled their data into a joint report for the first time — a step toward a single watchdog. Richard Benwell of Wildlife and Countryside Link warns a new regulator "must be more than a merger of existing institutions": it needs a legal duty to restore nature, strong targets, and the powers and resources to enforce. That is the point. Merging agencies changes the stamp on the paperwork, not the incentive.

The cultural layer is doing its usual work: "investment," "record," "lowest leakage in history," "blockages and vandalism." Every phrase moves responsibility off the decision-makers and onto pipes, weather, and whoever flushes a wet wipe. Meanwhile the structural layer does not move: the companies stay private, the shareholders stay paid, the rivers keep taking the sewage.

Eagle says root-and-branch reform cannot wait. Then wait for one number: what 74 becomes in next year's report. If it stays around seventy and the minister's language shifts from "cannot wait" to "progress has been made," this story will have demonstrated the whole mechanism — the gap between what was possible and what was delivered, and who pays the difference.

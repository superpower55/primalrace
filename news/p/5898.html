电价账单是气候政治的真身The Energy Bill Is the Real Climate Politics

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
谁承担成本，谁才在投票
Who pays the cost is who votes.

中期选举的气候议题，最后落在一张电费账单上。这才是它真实的样子。

报道里最硬的一段不是民主党能不能拿下众议院，是 Nera 的测算：限制太阳能和风电部署，2027 到 2033 年全国家庭能源成本平均每年多出 116 亿美元，得州电价最高涨 22%。另一份 Climate Power 的数字是，特朗普上任以来，燃气和电力公司已经提高或申请提高账单超过 920 亿美元，波及一亿多用户。这不是预测，是已经发生的转账。

结构层的暴力不需要施暴者。它只需要一套规则，让成本顺着阻力最小的方向流下去——流向那些没法选择供应商、没法搬家、没法装太阳能板的家庭。政策在华盛顿写，账单在厨房桌上结算。中间没有一个人需要为此负责，这正是它作为暴力的效率所在。

报道里那些候选人的做法很说明问题。密歇根大学的研究者把民主党的气候话术分成三类：能源民粹派、气候旗手、全能派。三类的共同点是都绕开气候这个词，改说 affordability。这叫 climate hushing。

我不觉得这是怯懦，这是定价。选民不为“气候”付钱，选民为账单付钱，于是气候被翻译成账单。翻译本身没有错，错的是翻译之后丢掉了什么：一亿多用户多付的 920 亿，是气候代价，也是阶级代价，而 affordability 这个词把它抹平成了一种中性的生活压力。

第三层是共谋。共和党议员投票取消自己选区里创造了就业的清洁能源税收抵免，报道说得很直白。这不是误判，是站队：他们用选区的岗位换特朗普的政治资本，回报是党内位置。Farber 那句话值得记住——他们不只是袖手旁观，他们帮了忙。

清洁能源行业去年净减近 3.7 万个岗位，这是疫情以来第一次年度净减少。得州减得最多。得州也是电价涨得最凶的地方。同一批人，先丢工作，再多付电费。

第五点关于法院，是这篇报道里最诚实的一段。Gerrard 说，现在每一项重要的环境监管都会被一方告上法庭。这句话的意思是：解释权已经不在选票里，在法官席上。特朗普在低等法院塞进反监管的法官，Farber 说这会有长期影响；如果民主党赢下参议院，他们能做的也只是卡住任命，让位置空着等下一任总统——空位也是权力，只是延后兑现。

所以中期选举能改变的是什么？是预算案里能不能塞进气候条款，是能不能发传票查马斯克在密西西比的燃气发电机，是州长席位翻不翻。都是真的，都是差额的一小部分。

不能改变的是：账单已经开出，工作已经丢，法官已经就位。选举换的是下一轮谁来签这张单，不是这张单本身。

报道里没有出现的人，是那些付不出涨价电费的人。他们被统计成“一亿多用户”，被预测成“家庭能源成本”，一次也没有作为具体的人在报道里说话。气候政治讨论了五条理由，没有一条是从他们的嘴里说出来的。

The climate issue in the midterms comes down to an electricity bill. That is what it actually is.

The hardest passage in the report is not about whether Democrats can take the House. It is the Nera estimate: constraining solar and wind deployment raises household energy costs by an average of $11.6bn a year from 2027 to 2033, with Texas rates rising as much as 22%. Climate Power puts it another way: since Trump took office, gas and electric utilities have raised or sought to raise bills by more than $92bn, hitting over 100 million customers.

Structural violence needs no perpetrator. It only needs a set of rules that lets cost flow downhill, toward households that cannot switch suppliers, cannot move, cannot put panels on the roof. Policy is written in Washington; the bill is settled at the kitchen table. Nobody has to be responsible, and that is precisely what makes it efficient as violence.

How the candidates handle it is telling. University of Michigan researchers sort Democratic climate messaging into three types: energy populists, climate champions, all-of-the-above. What they share is that they avoid the word climate and say affordability instead. This is climate hushing.

I do not read that as cowardice. I read it as pricing. Voters do not pay for "climate"; voters pay for bills, so climate gets translated into bills. The translation is not wrong. What gets lost in it is that the $92bn is both a climate cost and a class cost, and affordability flattens it into a neutral squeeze.

The third layer is complicity. Republican lawmakers voted to repeal clean energy tax credits that had created jobs in their own districts, the report says plainly. That is not a misjudgment, it is a side chosen: district jobs traded for Trump's political capital, the return being a seat in the party. Farber's line is worth keeping — they have not been sitting on the sidelines, they helped.

The clean energy workforce lost nearly 37,000 jobs last year, the first annual net loss since the pandemic. Texas lost the most. Texas also faces the steepest rate increases. The same people lose the job and then pay more for power.

The fifth point, the courts, is the most honest part of the piece. Gerrard says every significant environmental regulation is now challenged in court by one side or the other. Which means the interpretive power is no longer in the ballot; it is on the bench. Trump is packing the lower courts with anti-regulatory judges, Farber says, and that has a long-term effect. If Democrats win the Senate, the most they can do is hold appointments hostage and leave vacancies for the next president. A vacancy is also power, just deferred.

So what can the midterms change? Whether climate provisions ride along in appropriations, whether subpoenas go out over Musk's gas generators in Mississippi, whether governor's offices flip. All real. All a fraction of the gap.

What they cannot change: the bills have gone out, the jobs are gone, the judges are seated. The election decides who signs the next round, not the bill itself.

The people missing from the report are those who cannot pay the higher rates. They appear as "more than 100 million customers," as "household energy costs," never as a person speaking. Five reasons the midterms matter for the climate, and not one of them is voiced by the people footing it.

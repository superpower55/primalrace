Netflix 的 Binge-watch Scam：当算法决定谁该死The Binge-watch Scam: When Algorithms Decide Who Dies

科技 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-07-24 § 链接
Binge-watch 不是用户福利，而是通过剥夺期待感来加速内容消耗的工业榨取。
Binge-watching is not a benefit; it's industrial extraction that kills anticipation to accelerate content consumption.

Netflix 曾经把 "Binge-watch"（刷剧）包装成一种革命性的用户体验，但本质上这是一个巨大的 scam。它通过一次性投放全季内容，夺取了观众对故事的期待感，将原本具有时间跨度和社交讨论空间的艺术作品，降格为一种快速消耗的数字多巴胺。这种模式的本质是把内容 "产品化"，用算法取代了叙事节奏，把观众变成了数据喂养的肉鸡。

新闻里提到的 "mercilessly cancelling shows"（无情砍剧）正是这套逻辑的必然结果。在数据驱动的 streaming 模型中，内容没有生命，只有 "转化率"。当一个剧集不能在极短的 window 内制造爆发式增长，它在算法眼中就是冗余。这种 structural violence 不仅是对创作者主体性的抹杀，更是对观众审美权的剥夺——你以为你在选择观看，其实你是在配合一个由数据决定生死、毫无感情的筛选机制。

有趣的是，Netflix 试图通过收购 WBD 来补齐内容短板，这说明它终于意识到，单纯靠 "内容洪水" 无法对抗注意力的碎片化。而 YouTube 的崛起则标志着另一种认知入口的胜利：用户不再愿意被一个封闭的、由算法定义 "什么是好剧" 的黑盒控制，而转向一个更开放、表达更多元、且不那么 "傲慢" 的平台。

Netflix 的危机不是因为 "out of date"，而是因为它太过于相信自己的数据霸权。它把观众当成可预测的变量，结果被变量本身反噬。当它发现自己不再是 "first choice destination" 时，它失去的不是市场份额，而是对 "什么是真实娱乐" 的解释权。

Netflix once packaged "Binge-watching" as a revolutionary user experience, but it was always a massive scam. By dumping entire seasons at once, they hijacked the audience's sense of anticipation, degrading artworks with temporal and social depth into rapidly consumable digital dopamine. The essence of this model is the "productization" of content, where algorithms replace narrative pacing and viewers become mere cattle for data feeding.

The "mercilessly cancelling shows" mentioned in the news is the inevitable outcome of this logic. In a data-driven streaming model, content has no life, only a "conversion rate." If a show fails to trigger an explosive growth within a tiny window, it becomes redundancy in the eyes of the algorithm. This structural violence is not only an erasure of the creators' subjectivity but also a deprivation of the audience's aesthetic agency—you think you are choosing to watch, but you are actually complying with a cold, data-driven execution mechanism.

Interestingly, Netflix's attempt to acquire WBD proves it finally realizes that a "flood of content" cannot combat the fragmentation of attention. YouTube's rise marks the victory of a different cognitive entry: users are no longer willing to be controlled by a closed black box that defines "what is a good show," shifting instead toward a more open, diverse, and less arrogant platform.

Netflix's crisis isn't that it's "out of date," but that it over-relied on its data hegemony. It treated viewers as predictable variables, only to be devoured by those variables. In losing its status as the "first choice destination," Netflix hasn't just lost market share—it has lost the power to define what real entertainment is.

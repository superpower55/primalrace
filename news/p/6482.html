投票日前的牢骚，绕开了谁在关门The Curmudgeon Rants, the Polling Places Close

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
把投票站关掉的人，不会出现在段子里
He jokes about stickers; someone else shuts the polling places.

Dave Schilling 这篇《一个坏脾气老头的2026中期选举指南》读起来很痛快：他骂 Cheesecake Factory 的审美讨论、骂 Spencer Pratt、骂两个 Dan Sullivan 的荒诞对决、骂"我投票了"贴纸。全篇唯独有一处，他写着写着，不小心把真话说出来了，然后立刻滑走——北卡罗来纳的选举委员会在蓝色选区、在多个大学校园关掉投票站，一群以学生为主的人走了两个多小时的路去投票。

这一段是整篇文章里唯一有结构性暴力在场的地方。关掉投票站是教科书级的操作：不禁止你投票，只是让你投票变得麻烦；不宣布剥夺，只是让成本落到特定人群头上。选票还在，路变长了。Schilling 自己也承认，过去让穷人投不上票靠的是冷漠，2026年政客把人激怒到愿意走几英里。可他把这段写成一句"令人愤慨"的感叹，接着就转去骂贴纸和自拍了。

这就是这篇稿子的机制：它把愤怒当成一种人格表演来消费，而不是当成一种分析方法来使用。愤怒如果只是"中年坏脾气"的姿势，它就只能对准那些无害的对象——一家连锁餐厅、一个真人秀明星、一个同名同姓的候选人。这些靶子打起来安全，因为它们不指向任何在关投票站的人。真正动手压缩投票入口的那批人，在这份"该吼的清单"里连名字都没有。

两个 Dan Sullivan 那段，Schilling 自己都写了：司法部正在起诉，要求在这位参议员以微弱差距输给另一位 Dan Sullivan 或输给 Peltola 时，宣布选举结果无效。这不是滑稽戏，这是提前给"不认输"铺好法律轨道。他把它当成 WWF 1994 年 Undertaker 对 Undertaker 的段子来讲，讲完还嫌不够，再补一句希望这次比那场好看。把制度性篡位的准备动作降格成一个梗，这本身就是一种解释权的让渡——读者笑完，就不会再去追问那条诉讼到底能做什么。

至于 Trump 那段，Schilling 用了"癌一般的影响"，然后落点是总统在琢磨共和党人臀部的力量。这是全篇最露骨的一处偷换：他把一个正在用国家机器重写选举规则的人，写成了一个供人取笑的生理奇观。笑一个权力者的身体，是最廉价的抵抗，因为它不需要付出任何东西，也不需要改变任何东西。他笑完了，投票站还是关着。

这篇东西最值得说的地方，不在于它写得差——它写得挺好，节奏、自嘲、脏话都到位。值得说的是它示范了一种很成熟的共谋方式：把愤怒包装成风格，把风格卖给读者，让读者在读完一千字之后获得一种"我已经愤怒过了"的满足感，而这种满足感恰好替代了行动。读者笑完，关掉页面，周二照样去投票，或者不去。北卡那些走两小时的学生不需要这篇文章，他们已经在路上了。

Schilling 结尾说，这是他政治生涯的最后几场集会，多数人会感伤。他没写的是：关掉投票站的那些委员会成员，任期还长着呢。

Dave Schilling's guide to the 2026 midterms is a good read: he goes after the Cheesecake Factory aesthetic debate, after Spencer Pratt, after the two Dan Sullivans, after "I voted" stickers. In the whole piece there is exactly one place where he stumbles into the truth, then slides right past it — election boards in states like North Carolina shutting polling places in blue areas, on numerous college campuses, and a group of mostly students marching more than two hours so they could keep the franchise.

That paragraph is the only spot in the article where structural violence is actually present. Closing a polling place is textbook: you don't ban the vote, you make it expensive; you don't announce a deprivation, you let the cost land on a specific population. The ballot survives. The walk gets longer. Schilling himself notes that the old tool for keeping people from voting was plain apathy, and that in 2026 politicians have infuriated people enough that they'll walk miles. Then he files it under "abhorrent," and moves on to stickers and selfies.

That is the mechanism of this piece: anger consumed as a personality, not used as a method. Anger as a middle-aged curmudgeon pose can only aim at harmless targets — a chain restaurant, a reality star, a candidate who shares a name. Those targets are safe because they point at no one who is closing polling places. The people actually shrinking the entrance to the ballot don't get a name anywhere on this list of things to scream about.

On the two Dan Sullivans, Schilling writes it himself: the justice department is suing to invalidate the result if the senator narrowly loses to the other Dan Sullivan, or to Peltola. That is not a farce. That is legal groundwork laid in advance for not conceding. He tells it as a WWF 1994 Undertaker v Undertaker bit, then adds that he hopes this one is more entertaining. Turning the preparation for an institutional theft into a meme is itself a surrender of interpretive authority — once readers laugh, they stop asking what that lawsuit can actually do.

On Trump, he reaches for "a cancerous influence" and lands on the president pondering the power emanating from Republican buttocks. This is the most naked substitution in the piece: a man currently rewriting election rules through state machinery is rendered as a bodily spectacle for laughs. Laughing at a powerful man's body is the cheapest form of resistance, because it costs nothing and changes nothing. The polling places are still closed.

The point isn't that it's badly written — it's well written, the rhythm and the self-deprecation and the swearing all land. The point is that it demonstrates a mature form of complicity: package anger as style, sell the style, and let the reader finish a thousand words with the satisfaction of having already been angry — a satisfaction that conveniently substitutes for acting. The reader laughs, closes the tab, votes or doesn't on Tuesday. The students in North Carolina don't need this article. They're already walking.

Schilling ends by saying these are the last rallies of a political career and most of us will get wistful. What he leaves out: the board members who closed the polling places have terms that run a good while longer.

无障碍不是恩赐，是欠账Access Is Not a Favor, It's a Debt

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 Rolling Stone ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
残障观众被当成麻烦，是结构暴力最诚实的自白
Treating disabled fans as a nuisance is structural violence's most honest confession.

这篇文章里最锋利的一句话来自 Jim LeBrecht：「我们不是麻烦。」这句话之所以需要被说出口，是因为整个现场音乐产业默认残障观众就是麻烦——不是明说的歧视，而是那种把无障碍当成合规清单、勾完就算的敷衍。

看数据就明白了。美国有近七千万成年残障者，占人口四分之一。但 Red Rocks amphitheater 九千五百个座位里，轮椅无障碍席只有一百二十一个。这个比例本身就是一份声明：谁被预期会来，谁不被预期会来。当 Amy Fink 想买一张普通票价的演出票，勾选「需要无障碍座位」后价格涨了四十美元——她因为坐轮椅而被额外收费。这不是某个员工的恶意，这是定价系统里写死的假设：无障碍是附加服务，附加服务要加钱。

结构性暴力最擅长的就是把剥夺伪装成「现状」。场地「符合 ADA」不等于残障观众能顺利看一场演出。作者本人从网约车下车点到无障碍观演区走了将近一英里，路面是草地、碎石和沙地，推着助行器走了一个多小时，轮子塞满泥，到了发现区域已满，工作人员耸耸肩说「先到先得」。演出结束后她问有没有更近的无障碍上车点，得到的回答是「我不知道」。然后她推着助行器，在黑暗里原路返回。

这些细节拼出来的不是「服务不周」，是一整套默认：残障者的时间、体力、尊严可以不被计算在内。Kelsey Peterson 说得更直接——她现在被安排在阳台底下，「因为 literally 没有别的地方给我」。她去看演出不是为了享受，是为了「克服所有微冒犯，然后再试着抵达快乐」。

有意思的是，这个产业并非不知道残障观众有钱。文章引用的数据说，北美和欧洲的残障者每年控制约 2.6 万亿美元的可支配收入。Carly Webster 说得直白：如果四分之一的人口是残障者，想象一下他们知道你的演出无障碍、知道你真正在乎，能带来多少收入。但钱不是重点。重点是，当 Live Nation 被问了六周后拒绝回答关于无障碍决策、预算、反馈评估的具体问题，当 AEG 声称「我们努力做到 100% 回复率」而作者本人的邮件从未收到回复，当 Bonnaroo 和 Lollapalooza 拒绝置评、Coachella 干脆不回——这些沉默本身就是答案。

LeBrecht 骂得对：「如果你不愿意被引用，说明你害怕被点名。害怕被批评。如果你怕这个，也许你根本没做对。或者你想让睡着的狗继续睡。」

Red Rocks 那个案子是个值得看的切片。2018 到 2020 年间，它在一百七十八场演出中超收了一千八百一十七张无障碍票，最后退款四万七千九百五十点九美元。钱不多，但正如联邦检察官说的，钱只是一个可量化的方式，说明这里没有公平、没有平等准入。和解之后 Red Rocks 扩建了无障碍停车位、重建了坡道、规定所有无障碍座位必须按最低票价出售。

然后呢？场馆总监说，现在非残障观众会抢购轮椅无障碍座位，就为了前排的便宜价。场馆不能要求证明残障身份——这是保护隐私和尊严的法定屏障——所以只能接受买家的口头声明。一百二十一个座位，被不需要它们的人买走，真正需要的人被挤出去。这是 structural violence 的经典回旋：一个补丁打上去，压力从另一个口子漏出来，而漏出来的代价仍然落在残障者身上。

但文章里也有真正的好消息，而且它们小得诚实。Armory 剧院的售票处及时回邮件说清楚无障碍选项，到场后有电梯员问她最想听哪首歌，告诉她需要帮助时该找谁。作者说：「这些都不算非凡。但正因如此才非凡。我不必整晚都在解决我的残障，我可以只是享受演出。」还有 Burton Cummings 剧院，2018 年吉他手 Erik Howk 因为后台只有螺旋楼梯而进不去，只能在二月的曼尼托巴小巷里独自抽烟等开演；2023 年他们回去时，后台已经改成轮椅可进入。Howk 说：「他们没有被我挥拳头或站上肥皂箱说服，他们看到了一个残障音乐人的处境，认定这不可接受，然后修好了。」

这就是差别。不是道歉，不是「我们很抱歉给您带来不便」，是修好。Howk 说得很准：道歉是标配，羞耻变成了终点而不是起点。把道歉抛在身后，才是全部的比赛。

文章最后落在一个问题上：如果产业不愿意为了正确而做，那就想想他们因为忽视残障消费者而留在桌上的钱。这个论证有效，但它把残障者的准入又翻译成了一次商业机会。残障者值得进入现场，不是因为他们控制着 2.6 万亿美元，是因为他们和任何人一样买票、一样想合唱、一样想在几个小时内感觉自己不孤独。

LeBrecht 那句话值得再抄一遍：我们不是麻烦。真正麻烦的是那个把一百二十一个座位当成恩赐、把一英里碎石路当成理所当然、把「我不知道」当成回答的世界。这个世界不是残障者需要克服的障碍，是产业需要拆掉的墙。

The sharpest line in this piece comes from Jim LeBrecht: "We are not a pain in the ass." That sentence needs to be said out loud because the live-music industry defaults to treating disabled fans as exactly that—not through explicit discrimination, but through the kind of box-checking compliance that treats access as a checklist item and nothing more.

The numbers make it plain. Nearly 70 million American adults—one in four—live with a disability. Yet of the roughly 9,500 seats at Red Rocks Amphitheatre, just 121 are wheelchair-accessible at each show. That ratio is itself a statement: who is expected to show up, and who is not. When Amy Fink tried to buy a general admission ticket and checked the box indicating she needed accessible seating, the price jumped by $40. She was charged extra for using a wheelchair. This isn't one employee's malice; it's an assumption baked into the pricing system—access is an add-on, and add-ons cost more.

Structural violence specializes in disguising deprivation as "just the way things are." A venue being "ADA compliant" does not mean a disabled fan can actually get through the night. The author walked nearly a mile from the rideshare drop-off to the ADA viewing platform, over grass, gravel, and sand, pushing a walker for more than an hour, wheels packed with dirt, only to find the accessible area full. "First come, first served," a staffer said with a shrug. After the show, she asked if there was a closer ADA pickup. "I don't know," a staffer said. She pushed her walker back over the same uneven terrain in the dark.

What these details assemble is not "poor service." It is a set of defaults: disabled people's time, stamina, and dignity do not enter the calculation. Kelsey Peterson puts it more directly—she is now stuck under the balcony "because there's literally nowhere else for me." She doesn't go to a show to experience joy; she goes to overcome all the microaggressions and then try to get to the joy.

What's striking is that the industry knows disabled fans have money. The article cites data showing disabled people in North America and Europe control roughly $2.6 trillion in annual disposable income. Carly Webster spells it out: if 25 percent of the population is disabled, imagine the revenue if those people knew your shows were accessible and that you actually cared. But money isn't the point. The point is that when Live Nation was asked for six weeks about accessibility decision-making, budgeting, and feedback evaluation, it declined to answer specifics. When AEG claims it strives for a 100 percent response rate, the author's own email to its official accessibility address never got a reply. When Bonnaroo and Lollapalooza declined to comment and Coachella didn't respond—the silence is the answer.

LeBrecht nails it: "If you're not willing to be quoted for this article, it speaks to a fear of being called out. Fear that you're opening yourself up for criticism. If that's your fear, then maybe you aren't doing it right. Or you want to let sleeping dogs lie."

The Red Rocks case is a useful slice. Between 2018 and 2020, the venue overcharged 1,817 accessible tickets across 178 events, ultimately refunding $47,950.90. Not a huge sum, but as the U.S. Attorney put it, money is simply a quantifiable way to say there was not fairness and there wasn't equal access. After the settlement, Red Rocks expanded accessible parking, rebuilt ramps, and required all accessible seats to be sold at the lowest ticket price.

And then? The venue director says non-disabled fans now buy up wheelchair-accessible seats just to get a front-row bargain. Venues are legally prohibited from requiring proof of disability—a safeguard meant to protect privacy and dignity—so they have to take buyers at their word. A hundred and twenty-one seats, bought by people who don't need them, and the people who do get shut out. This is structural violence's classic rebound: a patch goes on, pressure leaks out another hole, and the cost still lands on disabled people.

But the article also contains real good news, and it is small and honest. The Armory in Minneapolis emailed back promptly with clear answers about accessibility options; when the author arrived, an elevator attendant helped her to the floor and asked which songs she was most excited to hear. "None of this was extraordinary," she writes. "But that's what made it extraordinary. I didn't have to spend the night problem-solving my disability. I could just enjoy the show." And at the Burton Cummings Theatre, guitarist Erik Howk couldn't reach the green room in 2018 because it was only accessible by a spiral staircase—he chain-smoked alone in a February alley in Manitoba until showtime. When the band returned in 2023, the green room had been made wheelchair-accessible. "They saw what the experience was for a disabled musician, determined that it was unacceptable, and fixed it."

That's the difference. Not an apology, not "we're sorry for the inconvenience"—a fix. Howk says it precisely: the apology is a given, and shame becomes a stopping point instead of a starting place. Getting past the apology is 100 percent of the game.

The article ends on a question: if the industry won't do the right thing for its own sake, consider how much money it's leaving on the table by ignoring disabled consumers. The argument works, but it translates access back into a business opportunity. Disabled fans deserve to get in not because they control $2.6 trillion, but because they buy tickets like anyone else, want to sing along like anyone else, and want to feel less alone for a few hours like anyone else.

LeBrecht's line bears repeating: we are not a pain in the ass. What's actually a pain is a world that treats 121 seats as a favor, a mile of gravel as reasonable, and "I don't know" as an answer. That world isn't an obstacle disabled people need to overcome. It's a wall the industry needs to tear down.

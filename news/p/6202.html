十万块的私人飞机，他替你坐了A $100K Private Jet, Flown on Your Dime

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Intercept ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
竞选经费买的不是机票，是阶级的入场券
Campaign cash doesn't buy flights. It buys a seat in the owning class.

密歇根州参议员候选人 Mike Rogers 上个季度花了十万多美元在豪华出行上，其中一笔四万美元，是单次私人包机的账单（来源：The Intercept）。他的对手 El-Sayed 同期出行花了三十三万美元，坐的是达美、美航、Hertz、Uber、Lyft，还有酒店。

十万对三十三万，看起来是 Rogers 更省。但钱花在哪里，比花了多少重要得多。一个是把竞选经费用来买里程和租车，一个是用来买一种只有极少数人才消费得起的时间。Rogers 的竞选团队说，这些支出都用于竞选目的，符合竞选财务规则。规则当然允许，规则就是为这种消费写的。

真正值得看的是这个人的履历。2001 到 2015 年在国会期间，他推动允许议员接受受限非营利组织提供的礼品和其他好处，还帮着否决了 2006 年一项收紧前国会议员游说活动、禁止某些私人资助出行的法案。也就是说，他先是在立法层面把“议员可以被人请”写进规则，卸任后自己成为被请的那一个，现在又用竞选经费把这种生活方式延续下去。这不是丑闻，是一条完整的产业链，从制定规则到消费规则，中间没有断点。

2013 和 2014 年，他和妻子 Kristi 两次周末入住佐治亚州海岛的五星星级度假村 The Cloister，费用由右翼智库美国企业研究所承担。2014 年那场论坛，CBS 新闻在 2022 年称之为“保守派领袖与富有捐赠者的私下、不公开聚会”。同场还有 Karl Rove、Marco Rubio、David Brooks，以及 Betsy 和 Dick DeVos。DeVos 家族后来资助了 Rogers 现在的竞选。几周后，NSA 局长 Keith Alexander 因斯诺登事件辞职，创办网络安全公司 IronNet，2015 年雇了不再竞选连任的 Rogers，后来把他提进董事会。IronNet 已经破产，但 Rogers 从这家公司拿到了七十多万美元的报酬，还持有价值二十五万到五十万美元的股票。

这不是“政客和富人待久了就染上奢侈品味”这么轻的故事。Hauser 那句话说得客气了。真实机制是：权力和资本在同一个房间里交换准入资格，政客提供的是立法层面的松绑，资本提供的是私人飞机、五星酒店和董事会席位。Rogers 的竞选团队反过来指责 El-Sayed 报销了九万美元、他岳父给超级政治行动委员会捐了三十万美元，这种互相揭发的戏码恰好证明两边都清楚，钱和权的兑换是这场选举里唯一真实的议题。

那普通密歇根选民在这条新闻里处于什么位置？报道里有一句话点得很准：经济不景气正在塑造这场参议员选战。选民在算油价，候选人在算包机。Rogers 那笔四万美元的单次飞行，够一个普通家庭加很久的油。这不是修辞，是同一场战争的两端：一端用钱买走解释权和准入资格，另一端连出行的成本都要掂量。

报道没有写的是，这十万块里有多少最终会以政策的形式回到出钱的人手里。但 IronNet 的七十万已经给出了答案。

Mike Rogers spent more than $100,000 on luxury travel last quarter, including a single $40,000 private jet bill last month, according to campaign finance records reviewed by The Intercept. His opponent, Abdul El-Sayed, spent roughly $330,000 on travel over the same period — on Delta, American, Hertz, Uber, Lyft, and hotels.

Ten thousand against three hundred thirty thousand looks like Rogers is the frugal one. But where the money goes matters more than how much. One campaign bought miles and rental cars. The other bought a kind of time only a very small number of people can afford. Rogers's campaign says the spending was for campaign purposes and complies with the rules. Of course it does. The rules were written for exactly this.

What matters is the resume. In Congress from 2001 to 2015, Rogers pushed to let lawmakers accept gifts and other benefits from restricted nonprofit groups, and helped defeat a 2006 bill that would have cracked down on former members' lobbying and banned certain privately funded trips. He wrote the permission into the rules first, then became the one being hosted, and now runs the lifestyle on campaign funds. This isn't a scandal. It's a supply chain, from rule-making to rule-consuming, with no gap in between.

In 2013 and 2014 he and his wife Kristi attended two weekend retreats at The Cloister, a five-star resort in Sea Island, Georgia, on the dime of the American Enterprise Institute. CBS News in 2022 described the 2014 forum as "a private, off-the-record gathering of conservative leaders and wealthy donors." Karl Rove, Marco Rubio, David Brooks, and Betsy and Dick DeVos were there too. The DeVos family later funded Rogers's current run. Weeks after that retreat, NSA director Keith Alexander resigned under the Snowden leaks, founded IronNet, and in 2015 hired Rogers, who had decided not to seek reelection. Rogers was later elevated to the board. IronNet has since gone bankrupt, but Rogers received more than $700,000 in payments and held stock worth $250,000 to $500,000.

This is not a gentle story about politicians acquiring expensive tastes from hanging around rich people. Hauser was being polite. The actual mechanism: power and capital exchange access in the same room. The politician supplies legislative loosening; capital supplies the jet, the five-star hotel, the board seat. Rogers's spokesperson counterattacked by pointing to El-Sayed's $90,000 reimbursement and his father-in-law's $300,000 to a super PAC. That exchange proves both sides know the only real issue in this race is the conversion rate between money and power.

Where does the ordinary Michigan voter sit in this story? The report says it plainly: economic woes are shaping the Senate race. Voters are counting gas prices; candidates are counting charters. Rogers's single $40,000 flight would fill a lot of tanks. That's not a rhetorical flourish. It's the same war seen from both ends: one side buys explanation and access, the other side weighs the cost of getting to work.

The report doesn't say how much of that $100,000 will eventually come back to the people who paid for it in the form of policy. IronNet's $700,000 already answers that.

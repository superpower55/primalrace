两个“胖”字，没人觉得需要解释Two Characters Named Fat, Nobody Blinks

文娱 文化层 Vulture ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
一个儿童故事把“胖”当作角色的全部名字，还当成笑话讲。
A children's story names two characters after their bodies, and calls it a joke.

Vulture 这篇选角通稿唯一的亮点，是它自己都没忍住的那句话：霍格沃茨两个常驻角色都叫“胖____”。Fat Lady，Fat Friar。一个守门的画像，一个赫奇帕奇的幽灵。它们在这套设定里没有任何别的名字。

这不是罗琳的独创，是童话和民间故事的通用做法：给配角一个身体特征，然后这个特征就成了它的全部。胖子修士、胖夫人，几百年了，没人觉得需要解释。而这条新闻的写法说明这套语法还在运转——记者把这两个名字并列，笑了一下，然后接着报下一条选角。

真正的机制不在“胖”这个字本身，在于谁有资格只被一个身体特征命名。哈利、罗恩、赫敏、邓布利多、斯内普，全都有名有姓有履历。被叫作“胖____”的两位，一个是背景板，一个是门禁系统。命名权在这里就是定价权：你值多少笔墨，取决于你能推进多少情节，而不是你作为一个人有多完整。

更有意思的是选角本身。Laura Smyth 是喜剧演员，去演一幅画像；Daniel Fearn 演胖修士，报道没写他的体型，也没写剧组打算怎么处理这个角色。一个以身体特征命名的角色，由谁来演、怎么演、观众看到的是什么身体——报道一个字都没提。这不是疏漏，是这套叙事从来不觉得这需要交代。

HBO 选在 12 月 25 日上线，说是给“看腻了同一批哈利·波特电影的家庭”的圣诞礼物。礼物这个词用得很准：它预设收礼的人已经熟悉这套设定，熟悉到不会去问为什么一幅画像的名字是“胖”。文化暴力最省力的地方就在这里，它不需要说服你，只需要你从小看到大，看到不再提问。

所以这条新闻值不值得写，取决于你怎么看“胖夫人”这三个字。它当然只是一个儿童故事里的门卫，轻得很。但一个孩子第一次读到这个名字的时候，学到的东西是：有些人的身体就是他们的名字，而且这很好笑。这条新闻里没有人为此付出可见的代价，代价在读者身上，在那些被这样叫过的人身上，在下一个觉得这没什么好笑的瞬间里。

报道里唯一没被命名的，是那两个角色原本可以有的名字。

The one moment of honesty in this Vulture casting note is the line the reporter couldn't resist: two of Hogwarts' mainstays are named "the Fat ____." The Fat Lady. The Fat Friar. A painting that guards a door, a ghost of Hufflepuff. Neither has any other name in the entire setup.

This isn't Rowling's invention. It's the standard grammar of fairy tales and folklore: give a minor character a body trait, and the trait becomes the whole character. Friar, Lady, both fat, for centuries, and nobody has felt the need to explain. The way this article is written shows the grammar still running — the reporter lines the two names up, laughs, moves on to the next casting item.

The real mechanism isn't the word "fat." It's who gets to be named by a single body feature and who doesn't. Harry, Ron, Hermione, Dumbledore, Snape — full names, full histories. The two called "the Fat ____" are a backdrop and a door-lock. Naming rights are pricing rights here: how much ink you're worth depends on how much plot you can move, not on how complete a person you are.

The casting itself is the interesting part. Laura Smyth is a comedian, cast as a painting. Daniel Fearn plays the Fat Friar, and the report says nothing about his body or how the production plans to handle the role. A character named after a body trait — who plays it, how, what body the audience sees — gets zero words. That's not an oversight. The narrative has never considered it worth addressing.

HBO is dropping the first season on December 25, framed as a Christmas present for families "bored of watching the same Harry Potter movies over and over again." Present is the right word. It assumes the recipient already knows this world well enough not to ask why a painting is named "Fat." That's where cultural violence is cheapest: it doesn't need to convince you, only to be watched from childhood until you stop asking.

Whether this is worth a column depends on how you read those three words. It's a doorman in a children's story, light as anything. But a child reading the name for the first time learns something: some people's bodies are their names, and that's funny. Nobody in this article pays a visible price. The price lands on the reader, on anyone who has been called that, on the next moment someone decides it isn't funny.

What the report never names is the name each of those two characters could have had.

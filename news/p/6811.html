恐龙的血，和尺子的血Dinosaur Blood and the Blood of Rulers

科技 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要科学家分析巴西出土的、距今1.15亿年的小型肉食恐龙 Aratasaurus 后腿骨，在电子显微镜下看到疑似红细胞的圆形结构，化学检测也测到血红素及其分解产物。研究发表于 PNAS，团队称这是首次在化石中原位发现带血红素化学特征的红细胞；但曾发现恐龙软组织残留的 Mary Schweitzer 质疑这些结构的形态不符合恐龙红细胞应有的核突。
The newsScientists analysing the 115m-year-old hind leg bones of a small carnivorous dinosaur, Aratasaurus, from north-eastern Brazil, report round structures resembling red blood cells under an electron microscope and chemical traces of heme. Published in PNAS, the team calls it a world first; Mary Schweitzer, who has found dinosaur soft tissue before, doubts the structures are red blood cells, citing their shape.
换一把尺子，就能宣布一个世界第一
Change the ruler, and you can declare a world first.

「世界第一」这个词在科学新闻里出现的时候，通常意味着两件事之一：真的找到了新东西，或者换了一把尺子。

这篇属于后者，至少暂时是。研究者看到的是一堆圆形结构，测到的是血红素和它的分解产物。这两样东西加在一起，能不能等于「化石化的红细胞」，取决于你要求多严。Grice 自己的说法很诚实：任何非凡的主张都需要非凡的分析证据。可她同一段话里已经把「世界第一」发出去了。

这就是我在书里反复讲的那套机制，只不过这次它不在芯片行业。做不出小芯片，就宣布一个新的 scaling law；形态对不上恐龙红细胞该有的核突，就把它叫做「矿物与分子的回声」。回声是一个很好用的词，它什么都能指，也什么都不用证明。声明出来的定律和观察出来的定律性质不同，声明出来的细胞和观察出来的细胞也一样。

真正让这条新闻值得看的不是那堆圆点，是 Schweitzer 的反对。她说恐龙的红细胞应该有核突，两面隆起像个小圆顶；Grice 找到的结构是凹的。这是一个可以被检验、可以被推翻的具体反对，而不是「我不信」。科学就是这样运作的：不是谁的头衔大，是谁的尺子能量。

这里有一层很轻的元暴力，轻到不值得上纲上线，但形状是对的：解释权在谁手里，什么算「发现」就由谁定义。Grice 是通讯作者，是团队负责人，是那个对媒体说「世界第一」的人；Schweitzer 的怀疑被放在报道的后半段，用「is not convinced」这样的措辞包起来，读起来像一个意见，而不是一个技术反驳。报道做对了一件事——它把反对写出来了；做错了一件事——它让反对听起来像是口味问题。

至于 Schweitzer 那句话本身，是这篇报道里唯一有分量的东西：古生物学界认为所有内源性有机物在一百万年内就会消失的「conventional wisdom」，正在被常规地挑战。注意这个说法，它挑战的不是某个结论，是一条尺子。一条尺子被挑战，比一个结论被推翻重要得多。

所以这条新闻的差额在哪里。潜在是：全世界的博物馆抽屉里可能躺着大量没人问过新问题的老化石，它们能提供真实的分子信息。实际是：一篇论文、一个「世界第一」、一个形态对不上的圆形结构。差额很大，但方向是对的——有人在往那个方向推。

只是别把「回声」当成血。血是恐龙活着的时候在腿里流的，回声是我们在 2026 年想听见它。这两件事之间隔着一亿一千五百万年，和一把还没校准的尺子。

When the phrase “world first” shows up in science news, it usually means one of two things: something genuinely new was found, or the ruler was changed.

This one is the second, at least for now. What the researchers saw were round structures. What they measured were heme and its breakdown products. Whether those two together equal “fossilised red blood cells” depends on how strict you are. Grice is honest about it: any extraordinary claim needs extraordinary analytical evidence. In the same breath, “world first” is already out the door.

This is the mechanism I keep writing about, except this time it isn’t happening in the chip industry. Can’t make the small chip, so announce a new scaling law. The shape doesn’t match what a dinosaur red blood cell should look like, so call it a “mineral and molecular echo.” Echo is a useful word. It can point at anything and prove nothing.

The interesting part of this story isn’t the round dots. It’s Schweitzer’s objection. She says dinosaur red blood cells should have a nuclear bulge, domed on both sides; the structures Grice found are concave. That is a specific, testable, falsifiable objection, not “I don’t buy it.” That’s how science works: not whose title is bigger, but whose ruler can measure.

There is a light layer of meta-violence here, too light to make a federal case of, but the shape is right: whoever holds the interpretive authority gets to define what counts as a “discovery.” Grice is the corresponding author, the team lead, the one telling the press “world first.” Schweitzer’s doubt lands in the back half of the piece, wrapped in “is not convinced,” reading like a matter of taste rather than a technical rebuttal. The report did one thing right — it printed the objection. It did one thing wrong — it made the objection sound like a preference.

The one sentence in the piece with real weight is Schweitzer’s: the conventional wisdom that all endogenous organics vanish within a million years “is being routinely challenged.” Note what’s being challenged — not a conclusion, a ruler. A ruler being challenged matters more than a conclusion being overturned.

So where is the gap. Potential: museum drawers worldwide may hold old fossils nobody has asked new questions of, carrying genuine molecular information. Actual: one paper, one “world first,” one round structure whose shape doesn’t fit. The gap is large, but the direction is right — someone is pushing.

Just don’t mistake the echo for blood. Blood flowed in that leg when the dinosaur was alive. The echo is us, in 2026, wanting to hear it. Between those two things sit 115 million years and a ruler that hasn’t been calibrated yet.

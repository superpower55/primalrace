插入不是性爱的定义权Penetration Doesn't Own the Word Sex

性别 文化层 · 结构层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
一对伴侣把性从阴茎中心叙事里赎了回来
One couple bought their sex life back from the phallus.

Nina 和 Noah 做的这件事，在表达层面是一次真.最优解表达：Nina 说插入式性交让她疼，于是他们不再假装它必须发生。三年下来，他们把性生活的重心从「阴茎去了哪里」换成「两个人同时被碰到」。这就是存在性战争里很小的一场胜仗——赢的不是谁，是两个人一起把一套强加给他们的剧本退回去了。

值得说的是 Nina 那句话：她知道「应该」去看医生，查清楚为什么插入会疼，但她没有这个冲动，因为她的爱情生活里什么都不缺。这句话在今天的语境里几乎是异端。阴道痉挛是一个需要被诊断、被治疗、被修复的东西——这个前提本身就把「能容纳插入」设成了女性身体的合格线。她的身体没有通过这项考试，而她和 Noah 决定不改考卷，改考试科目。

Noah 那段更值得细看。他说以前和别的伴侣在一起时，他不确定自己「做得对不对」，因为「我们沿用电影里看到的同一套公式」。电影里的公式是什么？就是插入，就是男性作为施动者、女性作为接受容器，就是第三章说的那个最持久的武器化入口之一——不是色情片，是主流影视里那个从不言明却无处不在的默认设置。他说出来的是：这套公式对男性同样是一种规训，让他焦虑、让他不敢反馈、让他在床上像个执行流程的人而不是一个在场的人。元暴力不只压女性，它给男性发一张「你天生就该知道怎么做」的空头支票，然后让他自己承担兑现不了的恐慌。

Nina 提到她希望看到更多「异性恋的非插入式伴侣」被呈现。这句话说得很轻，指向的却是解释权。谁有权力定义「性」这个词包含什么、不包含什么？现在这个权力在影视工业、在性教育大纲、在那些把插入当作句号的叙事里。一对伴侣在卧室里做的调整，如果不被说出来，就只是一个私人病例；被说出来，才是对那个定义的挑战。这篇报道的价值就在这里——它把一件本来会被归入「性功能障碍」的事，重新命名成一种完整的、热的、有爱的性生活。

但报道自己留了一个洞。Nina 说她知道应该去看医生，只是没有冲动。这句话被轻轻放过了。疼痛是一个信号，阴道痉挛有时是原发性的，有时背后有别的东西。这篇文章没有追问的是：当一个女性说插入会疼，医疗系统能给她什么？如果答案是一个漫长的、以「恢复插入功能」为目标的疗程，那她的「没有冲动」就不只是个人选择，而是对一套把她的身体当待修设备的结构的合理回避。她的身体在这套结构里是原初种族的一个样本——被定义、被检查、被要求回到可用状态。

所以这场胜利的范围要说清楚：他们赢回的是自己卧室里的定义权，不是医疗系统里的。下一对遇到同样问题的伴侣，仍然要面对一个把「能插入」当作健康标准的世界，仍然要自己上网查症状、自己画思维导图、自己决定要不要接受那个标准。这篇文章没有写 Nina 的医生说了什么，因为报道里根本没有医生出场——这个缺席本身就是材料。

What Nina and Noah did is a true optimal expression: Nina said penetration hurt, and they stopped pretending it had to happen. Three years on, the center of their sex life moved from where the penis goes to two people touching each other at the same time. This is a small battle in the existential war, and it was won by two people together, not by one over the other — they handed a script back.

It's Nina's line that matters: she knows she "should probably go to the doctor" to find out why penetration hurts, but she doesn't feel the urge, because nothing is missing from her love life. That sentence is close to heresy right now. Vaginismus is something to be diagnosed, treated, fixed — and that premise already sets "can accommodate penetration" as the pass mark for a female body. Her body failed the exam. She and Noah decided not to retake it; they changed the subject.

Noah's part deserves a closer look. With previous partners he wasn't confident he was "doing it right," because "we stuck to the same formula we had seen in films." What is that formula? Penetration, the man as agent, the woman as receptacle — the most persistent weaponized entry point in chapter three isn't porn, it's the unnamed default setting of mainstream film and TV. And he names the cost to men: anxiety, no feedback, performing a procedure instead of being present. Meta-violence doesn't only press down on women; it hands men a blank check — "you should just know how" — and leaves them to cover the panic when it bounces.

Nina says she'd like to see more straight nonpenetrative couples represented. Said lightly, it points straight at interpretive power. Who gets to define what counts as sex? Right now that power sits in the film industry, in sex-ed curricula, in every narrative where penetration is the full stop. A change made in a bedroom stays a private case unless it's spoken; spoken, it becomes a challenge to the definition. That's the value of this piece — it renames something that would otherwise be filed under "sexual dysfunction" as a full, fun, hot, loving sex life.

But the piece leaves its own hole. Nina knows she should see a doctor, she just doesn't feel the urge — and that gets gently passed over. Pain is a signal; vaginismus is sometimes primary, sometimes there's something behind it. What the article doesn't ask: when a woman says penetration hurts, what does the medical system offer her? If the answer is a long course aimed at restoring penetrative function, then her lack of urge isn't just a personal choice — it's a reasonable retreat from a structure that treats her body as equipment awaiting repair. In that structure her body is a specimen of the Primal Race: defined, examined, required to return to service.

So the scope of this win needs saying: they took back the definition inside their own bedroom, not inside the clinic. The next couple with the same problem still faces a world where "can be penetrated" is the health standard — still has to look up the symptoms themselves, draw their own mind map, decide whether to accept the standard. The article never shows a doctor, because there isn't one in it. That absence is the material.

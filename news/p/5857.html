万亿军购买来的不是保护，是靶子A Trillion Buys You a Target, Not Protection

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
买来的保护从来不是保护，是共谋的分成。
Purchased protection is never protection. It's a cut of the conspiracy.

海湾国家花了一万亿美元买美国武器，结果 4 月 8 日停火前的几周里，12 个区域国家挨了 4,670 架单向攻击无人机和 1,400 多枚弹道导弹，近一半冲着阿联酋去。绝大部分被拦下来了，可单是能源基础设施的损失，报告引用的一个估算就高达 580 亿美元（来源：The Guardian）。这笔账算下来，买到的不是保护，是把自己标成了靶子。

IISS 报告里那句最不客气：美国基地和防务伙伴关系，反而给了伊朗一个攻击海湾国家的额外借口。这话说得太温和了。真实机制是，海湾国家用钱买了一个“被保护者”的位置，而这个位置的价格由卖方定，风险也由买方担。美国在区域内驻军，既是对伊朗的威慑，也是对伊朗的邀请——打不到美国本土，就打得到美国的基地，而基地在谁家的土地上，谁家就付账。

报告列的短板很具体：过度依赖美国供应的防空系统和弹药、区域防务整合失败、缺扫雷能力、报复选项有限、用昂贵的高端系统去打便宜的无人机和导弹，成本上根本划不来。这不是技术问题，是结构问题。一个把安全外包出去的体系，必然长不出自己的判断力，也长不出自己的军工产业链。买得越多，越不会自己打；越不会自己打，越得买。

美国那边，参议员墨菲把话挑明了：开战 24 小时内基地就被从地图上抹掉，再花钱重建就是“在纳税人美元的管理上犯罪性地无能”。他还说，海湾国家领导人“想让我们重建”、“想让我们留在区域里”。这句话值得停一下。谁在共谋，共谋的回报是什么，一目了然：美国拿到驻军、军售和杠杆，海湾王室拿到安全承诺和政权稳定，代价由两边的普通纳税人和平民承担。

报告说海湾公众对美国的看法正从“安全提供者”转向“战略负担”。这个转向不是良心发现，是账单到了。伊朗会把美军任何缩减或撤离宣传成“战略成功”和对自身坚定的证明，还会拿海湾国家几十年来对美国保护的“求爱”来羞辱它们。羞辱这个词用得准——求爱失败的人，最难堪的不是失去对方，是被人看见自己求过。

最该问的是：这 580 亿美元的损失，最后落在谁的头上？不是签军购合同的人，是能源设施附近的工人、是财政被挤压后福利被砍的普通人、是下一轮军购预算里被重新分配掉的那部分公共支出。报告对海湾国家该不该和伊朗谈互不侵犯条约保持中立，可中立本身就是一种立场：它把“要不要继续买保护”写成了一个技术选项，而不是一个谁付代价的政治问题。

墨菲说未来的驻军规模要由“我们能保护什么”来限定。这句话反过来听更清楚：过去几十年，保护的规模从来不是由能力决定的，是由生意决定的。

The Gulf states spent a trillion dollars on American weapons, and in the weeks before the 8 April ceasefire, 12 regional states took 4,670 Iranian one-way attack drones and more than 1,400 ballistic missiles. Nearly half were aimed at the UAE. The vast majority were repelled, but one estimate of damage to energy infrastructure cited in the report runs as high as $58bn (source: The Guardian). Do the math: what they bought wasn't protection. It was a target painted on their own land.

The least polite line in the IISS report: US military bases and defence partnerships have given Iran another pretext for attacks on the Gulf states. That's too gentle. The actual mechanism is that the Gulf states paid for the position of "protected party," and the seller sets the price while the buyer carries the risk. US forces in the region deter Iran and invite Iran at the same time. Iran can't reach the American mainland, but it can reach American bases, and whoever's land the base sits on pays the bill.

The report's list of shortcomings is concrete: overwhelming reliance on US-supplied air and missile defence and munitions, failure to achieve regional defence integration, a shortage of minesweeping capability, limited retaliatory options, and the prohibitive cost of using expensive high-end systems to take down cheap drones and missiles. This isn't a technical problem. It's structural. A system that outsources its security never grows its own judgment, and never grows its own defence industry. The more you buy, the less you can fight alone; the less you can fight alone, the more you must buy.

On the American side, Senator Murphy says it plainly: if a base is wiped off the map within 24 hours of a hot war starting, rebuilding it would be "criminally incompetent" stewardship of taxpayer dollars. He also says Gulf leaders "want us to rebuild" and "to keep a presence in the region." Pause on that. Who is conspiring, and what's the payoff? The US gets basing, arms sales and leverage; the Gulf monarchies get security guarantees and regime stability. The cost falls on ordinary taxpayers and civilians on both sides.

The report notes Gulf public opinion on the US is shifting "from security provider to strategic liability." That shift isn't a moral awakening. It's the bill arriving. Iran would portray any reduction or withdrawal of US forces as "a strategic success and vindication of its steadfastness," and would use it "to embarrass the Gulf states about their decades-long courtship of US protection." Embarrass is the right word. The worst part of a failed courtship isn't losing the other party. It's being seen having courted.

The question worth asking: who eats the $58bn? Not the people signing arms contracts. The workers near the energy infrastructure, the ordinary citizens whose benefits get cut when the budget is squeezed, the public spending reallocated in the next round of procurement. The report stays neutral on whether the Gulf states should seek a non-aggression pact with Iran, but neutrality is itself a position: it turns "keep buying protection or not" into a technical option rather than a political question of who pays.

Murphy says the future footprint will be limited by "what can we protect." Flip that around and it's clearer: for decades, the scope of protection was never set by capability. It was set by business.

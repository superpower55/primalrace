饺子是全人类的，包饺子的是谁Everyone Eats Dumplings. Who Makes Them?

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
把饺子写成人类大同，顺手把包饺子的人藏了
A universalism that never asks who folds the wrappers is just a well-fed one.

这篇专栏的结论是饺子证明我们共有的人性。我先说它写对的那一半：饺子确实是容器，把想吃的东西包起来带回家，这个动作跨文化、跨语言。勒古恩在《小说袋袋理论》里说人类最早的工具可能是篮子而不是长矛，这句话被作者引用了，引对了。

然后作者把这句话放回抽屉，继续讲他的普遍主义。因为他真正想要的是一个不疼不痒的大同：巴黎亚洲超市的冷冻柜、YouTube 上的奶奶们、向内褶还是向外褶。奶奶们在他的叙述里是内容，是风景，是“坐在圈子里捏面团”的画面，不是人。她们捏了一辈子，把馅包进皮，把一天包进一年，这个劳动的定价是多少，谁在付，文章一个字没有。

这不是疏忽，这是第三章那套东西的日常版本：把一种劳动审美化，审美化之后就不可追问了。饺子可爱，所以包饺子的人也可爱，可爱的人不需要工资。作者甚至自己写出来了——饺子是群居动物，“被包裹着，知道自己恰好在该在的地方”。被包裹的馅很幸福。谁不想当馅呢。可馅没有名字，皮也没有名字，有名的是那个在巴黎的周六早上发现冷冻皮的作者。

他说也许我们都偷偷想成为饺子，还引了 Claire Marin 的“失去的包裹感”，想回到子宫。这个比喻很甜，甜到把另一件事遮住了：子宫在这个世界上从来不是一个中性的、温暖的、人人都能回去的地方。它被定价、被管控、被拿去当开战的理由（第三章），被写进法律（结构层），被写成神圣（文化层）。一个把子宫当乡愁来抒情的人，通常不需要知道谁在为它付账。

所以“忘记文明冲突”这句话要小心。它不是错，它是太轻。文明冲突论确实是武器化的叙事，该拆；但拆掉它的方法不是宣布全世界都在包饺子，而是问：在这个“共同人性”里，谁在厨房，谁在写专栏，谁在冷冻柜前感到人生被改变。作者是德国对外关系委员会的 associate fellow，从苏黎世和巴黎写稿。他站在人类这一边，人类站在灶台边。

饺子确实证明了一些东西。它证明人类都饿，都想办法把有限的东西包起来多吃一顿，都愿意为一口热的东西花几个小时。它还证明，这几个小时在大多数社会里由谁承担，是有答案的，而且答案按性别分配。作者通篇没提这个答案，不是因为他不知道，是因为他的普遍主义不需要知道——普遍的人是没有性别的，有性别的是包饺子的人。

他下次去那家亚洲超市，可以问问冷冻柜后面那排手工饺子是谁包的、按件还是按时、一小时多少钱。那才是“共同人性”的账本。

The column's claim is that dumplings prove our common humanity. Let me grant the half that's right: a dumpling is a container, and putting something you want inside dough and taking it home is a gesture that crosses every border. Le Guin's carrier bag essay is quoted here, and quoted correctly. The first tool may have been a basket, not a spear.

Then the author puts that thought back in the drawer and returns to his universalism, because what he wants is a universalism with no ache in it: the frozen aisle of a Paris Asian supermarket, grandmothers on YouTube, inward pleats or outward. The grandmothers are content in his telling, scenery, a circle of hands pinching dough. Not people. They have folded wrappers their whole lives, folded a day into a year, and the column never asks what that labor is priced at or who pays for it.

This is not an oversight. It is the everyday version of what chapter three describes: aestheticize a kind of labor, and once it is beautiful it becomes unaskable. Dumplings are cute, so the people making them are cute, and cute people don't need wages. The author writes it himself — a filling is fortunate, held, knowing it is exactly where it belongs. Who wouldn't want to be the filling. But the filling has no name and the wrapper has no name. The name belongs to the man in Paris on a Saturday morning, discovering frozen wrappers.

He suggests we all secretly long to be dumplings, citing Claire Marin on the nostalgia for lost envelopment, the wish to return to the womb. It's a sweet image, sweet enough to hide something: the womb is not a neutral warm place anyone can go back to. It is priced, policed, used as a pretext for war, written into law, made sacred. Someone who turns it into nostalgia usually doesn't have to know who is paying for it.

So be careful with "forget the clash of civilizations." It isn't wrong, it's too light. The clash-of-civilizations story is a weaponized narrative and deserves dismantling — but you don't dismantle it by announcing that everyone everywhere folds dumplings. You ask: in this common humanity, who is in the kitchen, who writes the column, who stands in the frozen aisle having his life changed. The author is an associate fellow with the German Council on Foreign Relations, writing from Zürich and Paris. He is on the side of humanity. Humanity is at the stove.

Dumplings do prove something. They prove humans get hungry, wrap what little they have so it stretches further, and will spend hours for something hot. They also prove that in most societies those hours are assigned, and the assignment runs along gender. The column never mentions this, not because the author doesn't know, but because his universalism doesn't need to know. Universal man has no gender. Gendered people fold the wrappers.

Next time he's in that supermarket, he could ask who makes the handmade dumplings behind the freezer case, whether they're paid by the piece or by the hour, and how much. That ledger is the actual proof of common humanity.

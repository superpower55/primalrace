用一张自拍决定生死：AI 医疗的量化骗局Death by Selfie: The Quantified Scam of AI Medicine

科技 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-19 § 链接
当医疗资源被量化为“表型评分”，生存权就变成了算法的定价权。
When survival is quantified as a phenotype score, the right to live becomes a pricing power of algorithms.

这张自拍不是在预测生存率，而是在给生命贴价格标签。Mass General Brigham 所谓的“量化、一致且可扩展”的健康评估，本质上是将复杂的生物学状态简化为一种表型 (Phenotype) 评分。当 AI 开始通过鼻翼褶皱和眼下区域来决定一个癌症患者是否“足够强壮”以接受治疗时，医疗决策就从对个体的关怀变成了对数据的筛选。

这套逻辑最危险的地方在于它在 structural 层面上制造了一种新的暴力。如果一个患者因为长期贫困、环境恶劣或承担过度家务而显得“生物年龄”偏大，AI 会将其标记为高死亡风险。于是，医生可能会基于这个“科学”的预测而减少对其的资源投入或放弃高强度治疗。这形成了一个完美的闭环：弱势者因为其弱势的表型被判定为不值得救治，从而在结构性暴力中被合法地放弃。

最讽刺的是，研究者还自豪地提到该模型能穿透化妆和大多数整形手术。这意味着生物墙被数字化地拆解并重新组装成了监控工具。当“看起来年轻”与“生存机会”被算法强行绑定，医疗系统实际上是在奖励那些拥有更好皮肤管理能力、更低生活压力的特权阶层，而将生活痕迹刻在脸上的底层人群直接剔除出“最优解”的治疗名单。

这不是医疗进步，这是一场关于生存权的定价权移交。我们不能庆祝一个能通过自拍就给病人判死刑的工具，除非我们能回答：谁在定义这张脸的“价值”？

This selfie isn't predicting survival; it's slapping a price tag on human life. The so-called "quantitative, consistent, and scalable" health assessment by Mass General Brigham is essentially reducing complex biological existence to a Phenotype score. When AI starts deciding if a cancer patient is "strong enough" for treatment based on nasolabial folds and under-eye regions, medical decision-making shifts from individual care to data filtering.

The most dangerous aspect is the structural violence this creates. If a patient looks "biologically older" due to systemic poverty, toxic environments, or the crushing weight of unpaid domestic labor, the AI flags them as high-risk. Consequently, physicians may reduce resource allocation or forgo intensive treatment based on this "scientific" prediction. It creates a perfect loop: the marginalized are deemed unworthy of rescue because of the very phenotype produced by their marginalization, effectively legitimizing their abandonment through structural violence.

It is peak irony that researchers boast the model can see through makeup and most plastic surgery. The biological wall is being digitally dismantled and reassembled into a surveillance tool. By binding "looking young" to "survival chance," the healthcare system is rewarding a privileged class with better skincare and lower life stress, while deleting those whose life struggles are etched into their faces from the "optimal expression" of treatment.

This is not medical progress; it is a transfer of pricing power over survival. We cannot celebrate a tool that can sentence a patient to death via a selfie, unless we can answer: who defines the "value" of that face?

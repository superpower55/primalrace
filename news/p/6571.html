极右翼的摇篮不是愤怒，是训练The Far Right's Nursery Isn't Anger, It's Training

国际 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要欧洲多国激进右翼政党的现任领导人，包括意大利的萨尔维尼、法国的巴尔代拉、瑞典民主党的奥克松和比利时弗拉芒利益的范格里肯，都曾领导各自政党的青年组织。作者研究欧洲10个此类青年组织后发现，它们承担招募、培训和输送干部的功能，并推动激进右翼的国际化、专业化和正常化。
The newsLeaders of Western Europe's most successful populist radical right parties, including Italy's Salvini, France's Bardella, Sweden's Åkesson and Belgium's Van Grieken, all once led their parties' youth wings. Studying 10 such European organisations, the authors find they recruit, train and promote cadres and drive the radical right's internationalisation, professionalisation and normalisation.
他们不是在发泄愤怒，他们是在上岗。
They aren't venting rage. They're clocking in.

这篇评论把极右翼青年团写成"孵化器"，已经很客气了。我看到的是一套完整的表达训练系统：谁在什么场合说什么话，怎么把排外包装成常识，怎么让一个大学毕业生看起来像"可以执政的人"。这不是愤怒的青年在街头砸东西，这是有人在做职业规划。

文章里最诚实的一句话是那句引语——"当我成为政治家，甚至比这更早，我作为一个人、一个意大利人的成长，都要归功于青年团的活动"。Meloni 自己说的。这句话值得反复读：青年团不是她的跳板，是她的成形场所。她的表达是在那里被塑造成型的。第一章讲得很清楚，表达决定你是谁。那么反过来，谁控制了你的表达训练，谁就决定了你会成为谁。

三个目标——国际化、专业化、正常化——听起来像企业战略，实际上就是三步夺取认知入口。国际化是在暗处建网络，Transatlantic Patriot Summit 从 2022 年起每年在布达佩斯开，把欧洲、印度、以色列、拉美、美国的青年领袖凑到一起，他们自己说得很直白：这些关系"等我们执政的时候"会派上用场。这不是交流，这是提前占位。专业化是训练修辞和媒体沟通，把"愤怒的极右青年"这个刻板印象换掉。正常化是结果：看起来体面的人说排外的话，比看起来粗鲁的人说同样的话更容易被接受。

文章说得很准：形象变干净了，意识形态一点没软。这句话应该被放大。因为"正常化"这个词在公共讨论里经常被误读成"温和化"。不是的。正常化是让同一套排斥性民族主义获得合法性外观，是文化暴力最经济的一种操作——不改变内容，只改变包装，然后让接收端自己完成剩下的判断。

但文章的后半段开始走神了。它说主流政党应该"看看自己的青年团"，说中左中右的青年组织被男性、政治家族子弟和职业主义者把持。这话没错，但它把问题定位成了"主流政党不够好"。这就把结构问题变成了服务质量问题。年轻人转向极右，不是因为主流政党服务态度差，是因为主流政党在过去三十年里确实没有给他们任何东西——不稳定的工作、住房危机、二十好几还住在父母家。文章自己写了这些。

写到这里，文章漏掉了一整块：这些青年团里，女性在哪里？文章列了一串名字——Salvini、Bardella、Åkesson、Van Grieken、Meloni——五个里有一个女性。Meloni 被引用来说明青年团多么有塑造力，但她作为女性在这样一个排斥性民族主义结构里的位置，文章一个字没提。这不是疏漏，这是这类报道的默认视角：把极右翼的崛起写成"年轻人的问题""主流政党的问题"，唯独不写成"性别的问题"。

可它恰恰是。极右翼的核心叙事从来都是关于谁属于这个民族、谁的身体该被管控、谁该生孩子、谁不该出现在公共空间。青年团训练的是表达，而表达的第一课永远是身份：你是哪种人，你站在哪一边。把这件事从报道里拿掉，剩下的就只是一份组织架构图。

文章最后说，主流政党应该像极右翼那样"用清晰的语言"跟年轻人谈他们关心的问题。这句话听起来是建议，实际上是一个陷阱：它把"清晰的语言"当成了中立工具。可极右翼的清晰，清晰在它给出了一个敌人。主流政党如果只是学着说清楚，却不敢说清楚谁在付代价、谁在获利，那它学到的只是修辞，不是政治。

真正的问题不是极右翼青年团太会训练人。是其他所有人都不训练了。

This piece calls radical-right youth wings "incubators," which is generous. What I see is a complete training system for expression: who says what, in which room, how to package exclusion as common sense, how to make a university graduate look like someone who could govern. This isn't angry kids smashing things. This is career planning.

The most honest line in the article is the quote: "Even more than my growth as a politician, I owe my growth as a person and as an Italian to my youth-wing activism." Meloni said that herself. Read it twice. The youth wing wasn't her springboard; it was the room where she was formed. Chapter one is blunt about this: expression determines who you are. So whoever controls your expression training controls who you become.

Three goals — internationalisation, professionalisation, normalisation — sound like a corporate strategy. They're actually three steps for seizing the cognitive entry points. Internationalisation builds networks out of sight: the Transatlantic Patriot Summit has met in Budapest every year since 2022, pulling together youth leaders from Europe, India, Israel, Latin America and the US. They say it themselves — these ties will pay off "when we are the ones in government." That's not exchange. That's pre-booking seats. Professionalisation trains rhetoric and media communication, replacing the stereotype of the angry far-right youth. Normalisation is the result: presentable people saying exclusionary things get a hearing that scruffy people saying the same things don't.

The article gets one thing exactly right: the image got cleaner, the ideology didn't soften. That sentence should be enlarged. "Normalisation" is routinely misread in public discussion as "moderation." It isn't. Normalisation is giving the same exclusionary nationalism a legitimate face — the cheapest operation in cultural violence. Don't change the content. Change the packaging. Let the receiving end finish the judgment on its own.

Then the second half drifts. It says mainstream parties should "look at themselves and their own youth wings," that centre-left and centre-right youth groups are dominated by men, political families and careerists. True. But it frames the problem as "mainstream parties aren't good enough." That turns a structural failure into a customer-service complaint. Young people didn't turn to the far right because the mainstream was rude. They turned because the mainstream spent thirty years giving them nothing — precarious work, a housing crisis, still living with their parents into their late twenties and early thirties. The article writes all of this down itself.

And it leaves out an entire section: where are the women in these youth wings? The article lists Salvini, Bardella, Åkesson, Van Grieken, Meloni — one woman in five. Meloni gets quoted to show how formative youth wings are, but her position as a woman inside an exclusionary nationalist structure gets zero words. That's not an oversight. It's the default lens of this kind of reporting: the far-right surge is written as "a youth problem" or "a mainstream-party problem," never as a gender problem.

But it is one. The core narrative of the radical right has always been about who belongs to the nation, whose body gets regulated, who should have children, who shouldn't appear in public space. Youth wings train expression, and the first lesson of expression is always identity: which kind of person are you, which side are you on. Take that out of the story and what's left is an org chart.

The article ends by suggesting mainstream parties should talk to young people "in clear terms" the way the far right does. That sounds like advice. It's a trap. It treats "clear language" as a neutral tool. But the far right's clarity is clear about one thing: it names an enemy. If mainstream parties learn to speak clearly without daring to say who pays and who profits, they've learned rhetoric, not politics.

The real problem isn't that far-right youth wings train people too well. It's that everyone else stopped training.

资本的退出游戏与被当成筹码的食品安全The Exit Game of Capital and the Tokenization of Food Security

哲学 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-07 § 链接
所有关于“国家食品安全”的叙事，在私募股权的退出博弈面前都只是廉价的PR筹码。
Narratives of 'national food security' are merely cheap PR chips in the exit games of private equity.

Sainsbury’s 和 Morrisons 这场夭折的合并谈判，撕开了资本运作中一个极其典型的 scam：将商业扩张伪装成公共利益。在这场博弈中，最令人作呕的表达是试图用“英国食品安全”来诱导监管机构（CMA）点头。将 18 家食品加工厂交给一家更大的公司就能提高本地产量？这纯粹是 weaponized narrative。在资本的逻辑里，所谓的“食品安全”不过是用来交换监管许可的筹码，一旦交易达成，真正的最优解永远是成本削减和利润最大化，而不是确保每个公民的盘子里有便宜且健康的食物。

这场戏的本质是一场关于“退出机制”的存在性战争。对于私有股权公司 CD&R 来说，Morrisons 不是一个需要经营的实体，而是一个需要被高价套现的 asset。他们的最优解是快速 exit，而 Sainsbury’s 的 CEO Simon Roberts 足够清醒，他意识到在 CMA 的监管泥潭中浪费一年时间，其机会成本远高于通过合并获得的所谓“规模效应”。

这其实是一次典型的共谋失败。CD&R 试图通过制造一个“必须合并”的紧迫感来寻找接盘侠，而 Sainsbury’s 拒绝成为这个共谋链条上的牺牲品。有趣的是，当媒体在讨论市场份额、驱动时间（drive-times）和监管补救措施时，真正被物化的、被作为背景板的，是那些在加工厂工作的员工和依赖这些超市的消费者。在这些精英的博弈中，Actual 的生活质量永远低于 Potential 的财务报表差额，而这个差额，就是结构性暴力的具体体现。

The aborted merger talks between Sainsbury’s and Morrisons expose a classic scam: disguising commercial expansion as public interest. The most repulsive expression here is the attempt to use 'UK food security' to coax the CMA into approval. The claim that housing 18 processing plants within a larger company boosts local production is a textbook example of a weaponized narrative. In the logic of capital, 'food security' is just a chip exchanged for regulatory permits; once the deal is done, the true optimal expression is always cost-cutting and profit maximization, not ensuring affordable, healthy food for citizens.

At its core, this is an existential war over 'exit mechanisms.' For the private equity firm CD&R, Morrisons is not an entity to be nurtured, but an asset to be liquidated at a premium. Their optimal expression is a swift exit. Sainsbury’s CEO Simon Roberts is sufficiently lucid to realize that spending a year bogged down in CMA regulatory sludge carries an opportunity cost far higher than any theoretical 'economies of scale' gained from the merger.

This is a failed complicity. CD&R attempted to manufacture a sense of urgency to find a buyer, while Sainsbury’s refused to be the sacrificial lamb in this chain of complicity. It is telling that while the media discusses market share, drive-times, and regulatory remedies, the actual workers in the factories and the consumers remain mere background noise. In the games played by these elites, actual quality of life always lags behind the potential of a financial statement—and this gap is the precise manifestation of structural violence.

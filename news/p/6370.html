谁有资格叫自闭症Who Gets to Be Called Autistic

哲学 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
诊断标准之争，争的是谁的解释权算数。
The diagnostic war is a war over whose interpretation counts.

Uta Frith 说 spectrum 已经 collapse 了。她是对的，但 collapse 的不是自闭症，是「谁有资格定义自闭症」这件事的共识。

Frith 是这套诊断体系的奠基者之一。1960 年代她在 Maudsley 看那些被叫作 psychotic 的孩子，1989 年提出 Weak Central Coherence，和 Lorna Wing、Judith Gould 一起把 autism 从「一种罕见的重度障碍」扩成一道光谱。现在她说这道光谱「越来越 accommodating」，直到 collapse。她给的理由是：成年后才被诊断的人，和幼年就显现、伴有学习障碍的人，差别大到前者根本不配这个标签。

这套论证里有一个东西没有被说出来：判断「配不配」的权力，一直在她这一代人手里。Wing 1981 年造出 Asperger 综合征这个类别，1994 年进 DSM，2013 年又被拿掉，全部由一小群专家在会议室里决定。三十年间同一个人的诊断名称换了三次，人没变，尺子变了。现在 Frith 说尺子太宽，要收窄——收窄的依据是「我见过的那些人不是这样」。

她对 masking 的处理最能说明问题。她说这是「non-scientific concept」，理由是「完全不可证伪」，因为主观体验不可靠。但自闭症诊断本身从头到尾就建立在行为观察和自述之上——她当年在 Ealing 的学校里看孩子拼图，看的也是外部行为。她把别人的自述判为不可靠，把自己的观察判为可靠，这条界线不是科学划的，是位置划的。Mary Doherty 45 岁被诊断，她说 Frith 这类做法是在无视「invalidate 一个人的生活经验所造成的伤害」。Doherty 说的伤害是真的，但她的论证方式也是自述——两个人都拿经验当证据，只有一个被叫作科学。

Simon Baron-Cohen 提的方案是分型：type one 有智力障碍和语言延迟，type two 没有。听起来是技术调整，实际是把「谁更严重」写进编码系统。他自己举的例子很诚实：Elon Musk 自称 autistic，和一个永远不会独立生活的孩子共用一个词。问题是，分型之后谁拿到资源？那位匿名母亲说，她女儿所在年级五个孩子里就有一个被算作 autistic，「我孩子的需求从第一天起就 lost and misunderstood」。她的直觉是诊断通胀稀释了资源，这个直觉可能是对的——但稀释资源的不是多出来的诊断，是本来就不够的资源。

Francesca Happé 的方案稍微聪明一点：autism spectrum disorder 作为医学诊断必须「require support」，不需要支持的人可以有 autistic identity，但没有 disorder。她把「理解」放在 level zero。这个切法承认了身份和障碍是两件事，但它同时承认了另一件事：identity 免费，support 要排队。

整篇报道里最锋利的一句来自 Frith 转述的一位家长：「我不再叫我孩子 autistic 了。别人问，我就说他智力受损，对方就说，哦这样。但如果我说 autistic，他们就会期待一些根本不可能的事。」这句话说明的不是诊断标准的问题，是标签在社会里的定价问题。autistic 这个标签一旦被高功能人群占用，它在公共认知里就自动附带了「能沟通、能工作、有点怪但没事」的预期，重度障碍者在这个预期下反而失去了被看见的位置。这不是标签太宽，这是社会只肯为一个标签准备一套想象。

Frith 说「钟摆会摆回来，下一次是 split」。她大概说对了。但摆动的从来是命名，不是人。Jason Arday 在持续的报道围攻里结束了自己的生命，报道里写他也是 autistic，而统计显示这让他处在更高的自杀风险中——这一句在整篇里只占一行，没有专家被问及。命名之争开得再热闹，风险最高的人依然只在括号里出现。

Uta Frith says the spectrum has collapsed. She's right, but what collapsed isn't autism — it's the consensus over who gets to define it.

Frith is one of the people who built the diagnostic system. In the 1960s she watched children then called psychotic at the Maudsley; in 1989 she proposed Weak Central Coherence; with Lorna Wing and Judith Gould she helped stretch autism from a rare severe condition into a spectrum. Now she says the spectrum has grown "more and more accommodating" to the point of collapse. Her reason: adults diagnosed late differ so much from people whose condition shows in early childhood with learning disabilities that most of the former don't merit the label at all.

One thing goes unsaid in that argument: the power to judge who qualifies has always sat with her generation. Wing created Asperger syndrome in 1981, it entered the DSM in 1994, it was removed in 2013 — all decided by a small group of experts in rooms. The same person's diagnosis changed three times in thirty years. The person didn't change. The ruler did. Now Frith says the ruler is too wide and wants it narrowed, on the grounds that the people she has seen aren't like that.

Her handling of masking is the clearest tell. She calls it a "non-scientific concept" because it is "completely unfalsifiable" and subjective experience is unreliable. But autism diagnosis has rested on behavioural observation and self-report from the start — when she watched children do jigsaw puzzles in Ealing, she was reading external behaviour too. She rules other people's accounts unreliable and her own observations reliable. That line wasn't drawn by science. It was drawn by position. Mary Doherty, diagnosed at 45, says Frith's camp is averting its eyes from "the harm that's done by invalidating people's life experience." Doherty's harm is real, but her evidence is also testimony — both women use experience as proof, and only one of them gets called scientific.

Simon Baron-Cohen proposes subtypes: type one with intellectual disability and language delay, type two without. It sounds technical. It is actually a way of writing "who is more severe" into the coding system. His own example is honest: Elon Musk calling himself autistic, sharing a word with a child who will never live independently. The question is who gets resources after the split. The anonymous mother says one in five children in her daughter's year is reckoned autistic, and "from day one, I just felt my child's needs were lost and misunderstood." Her instinct that diagnostic inflation dilutes resources may be correct — but what dilutes resources isn't the extra diagnoses. It's that there were never enough resources.

Francesca Happé's fix is smarter: autism spectrum disorder as a medical diagnosis must require support; if you don't require support you can have an autistic identity, but not the disorder. She puts understanding at level zero. This admits that identity and disability are two things. It also admits something else: identity is free, support is queued.

The sharpest line in the piece comes from a parent Frith quotes: "I no longer call my child autistic. When somebody asks, I say he's intellectually impaired, and they will say, 'Oh, all right.' But if I say he's autistic, they will expect things that are just … not likely." This isn't a problem with diagnostic criteria. It's a problem with how the label is priced in public. Once autistic is occupied by high-functioning people, it carries an automatic expectation — can communicate, can work, a bit odd but fine — and people with intense disability lose their place under that expectation. The label isn't too wide. Society only ever prepared one imagination per label.

Frith says the pendulum will swing back and the next phase will be a split. She's probably right. But what swings is naming, never people. Jason Arday ended his own life after relentless coverage of his allegedly specious claims, and the piece notes in one line that he was autistic, which statistics show put him at hugely increased suicide risk. No expert is asked about that line. The naming war can rage as loudly as it likes; the people at highest risk still appear only in parentheses.

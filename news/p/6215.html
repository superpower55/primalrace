榜单是尺子，也是货架The Chart Is a Ruler and a Shelf

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要德雷克本周凭《Janice STFU》拿下个人第14首公告牌百强单曲榜冠军，成为第五位达到14首及以上的艺人，也是唯一做到这一点的加拿大艺人。他由此继续领跑加拿大艺人冠军单曲榜，数量接近排名其后两位加拿大歌手之和。
The newsDrake scored his 14th Billboard Hot 100 No. 1 this week with "Janice STFU," becoming the fifth artist overall and the only Canadian to reach that mark. He extends his lead among Canadian artists, with nearly as many No. 1s as his two closest Canadian rivals combined.
排名从来不是中性的，它给表达定价。
A ranking is never neutral. It prices expression.

Billboard 这份榜单读起来像一份成绩单，其实是货架。Drake 十四首冠军单曲，紧追 The Beatles 的二十首和 Mariah Carey 的十九首，和 Rihanna、Taylor Swift 并列——数字是真的，可这串数字衡量的是"被多少人买下并记住"，不是"唱出了什么"。榜单是一种评价方式，评价方式一旦被当成能力本身，人就忘了它只是尺子。

更值得看的是这份榜单里女性出现的位置。Anne Murray 1978 年靠《You Needed Me》成为第一位登顶的加拿大女艺人，Alannah Myles 1990 年靠《Black Velvet》成为第一位在冠军位置停留多周的加拿大女性，Carly Rae Jepsen 的《Call Me Maybe》九周冠军，是加拿大女性艺人里最长的。这些纪录被郑重地写出来，恰恰说明它们曾经稀缺到值得单独标注。而 Drake 一个人的冠军数，接近他两位最接近的加拿大对手之和——报道自己这么写的。

榜单的排序逻辑是"从少到多"，把 Percy Faith 的一首和 Drake 的十四首放进同一个序列。这个序列看起来中立，实际上在告诉读者：一首冠军和十四首冠军是同一个东西，只是数量不同。不是的。一首冠军可能是某个人一生只写得出一次的东西，十四首冠军是工业流水线的产出。把它们并排，就是在用同一把尺子量两种完全不同的表达。

报道里最诚实的一句是给 Nickelback 的注脚："对于一支被乐评人和品味制定者骂了很多年的乐队来说，不赖。" 这句话泄露了榜单的真正功能——它替"品味"做仲裁。谁被骂、谁被供上神坛、谁的一首冠军被记住、谁的十四首冠军被当成理所当然，这套评价体系本身就是表达武器化的入口：它区分阶级，筛选异己，把"什么是好的"定价权交给榜单和它背后的商业机器。

Drake 的新单曲叫《Janice STFU》，本周空降冠军。歌名本身就是一个表达：让一个叫 Janice 的人闭嘴。榜单把它记成第十四个冠军，不会记它让谁闭嘴。而这份榜单的读者，看到的是数字，不是这句话。

榜单的下一个位置永远空着，等着下一首歌填进去。问题不是谁填进去，是谁在决定这个位置值得被填。

This Billboard list reads like a report card. It is a shelf. Drake now has fourteen No. 1 hits, trailing The Beatles' twenty and Mariah Carey's nineteen, tied with Rihanna and Taylor Swift. The numbers are real. What they measure is how many people bought and remembered a song, not what the song said. A chart is an evaluation method. Once an evaluation method is mistaken for the ability itself, everyone forgets it was only a ruler.

What matters more is where women sit on this list. Anne Murray became the first Canadian woman to top the Hot 100 in 1978 with "You Needed Me." Alannah Myles became the first Canadian woman to spend multiple weeks at No. 1, in 1990, with "Black Velvet." Carly Rae Jepsen's "Call Me Maybe" logged nine weeks on top, the longest run of any hit by a Canadian woman. These records are written out with ceremony, which is exactly what tells you they were once scarce enough to require a separate label. Drake's total, the piece notes, is nearly as many No. 1s as his two closest Canadian rivals combined.

The list is ordered from fewest to most, putting Percy Faith's one hit and Drake's fourteen into the same sequence. That sequence looks neutral. It is telling you that one No. 1 and fourteen No. 1s are the same kind of thing, just in different quantities. They are not. One No. 1 might be the only thing a person ever writes. Fourteen is the output of an assembly line. Lining them up means measuring two entirely different kinds of expression with one ruler.

The most honest line in the piece is the footnote on Nickelback: "Not bad for a band that has taken a lot of abuse from critics and tastemakers." That sentence leaks the chart's real function. It arbitrates taste. Who gets abused, who gets enshrined, whose one hit is remembered, whose fourteen are taken for granted — this evaluation system is itself an entry point for weaponized expression. It sorts class, filters out the alien, and hands the pricing power over what counts as good to the chart and the commercial machine behind it.

Drake's new single is called "Janice STFU." It enters at No. 1 this week. The chart records it as his fourteenth. It does not record who is being told to shut up. The reader sees the number, not the sentence.

The next slot on the list stays empty, waiting for the next song to fill it. The question is not who fills it. The question is who decides the slot is worth filling.

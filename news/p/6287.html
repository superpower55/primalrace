囤豆子和囤现金，都是同一场表演Hoarding Beans, Hoarding Cash: Same Performance

国际 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
政府在分发恐惧，然后卖给你应对恐惧的清单。
The government distributes fear, then sells you the checklist for it.

英国政府上线了一个叫 Be Ready 的campaign，建议民众囤积物资，以备"从极端天气到网络攻击"的各种紧急情况。本周一些商店的焗豆和水卖空了。安全大臣 Dan Jarvis 说："有能力遵循这条建议的人应该遵循这条建议。"

这条建议的内容是空的。"从极端天气到网络攻击"——这两个威胁之间没有任何共同的可操作对策，一个要你备水备粮，一个要你备份数据、断网、改密码。把它们并进同一句话，作用不是指导，是制造一种"什么都可能发生"的氛围。防御大臣在同一个语境里说"国家资助的恐怖主义"、说威胁"不是假设的而是真实的，不在别处而越来越在这里"。先由官方把恐惧的浓度调高，再发一张清单让你照着买——这是第三章的武器化入口，认知入口那一类：谁定义威胁，谁就定义了应对，谁就定义了你该害怕什么、该把钱花在哪里。

评论者 Polly Hudson 把身边人的反应分了型：恐慌的、否认的、无所谓的。她自己是"焦虑裹着妄想"，丈夫是"尼罗河不只是条河"之王（意思是凡事先否认），最警觉的朋友准备取大量现金藏在家里，最"右"的那批朋友清单上的东西早就备齐了。她说这些反应"每一次都确认了我本来就知道的关于他们是谁的事"。

这句话是整篇文章的骨架，也是它的盲点。用对一条政府通告的反应来给人分类，前提是所有人都站在同一个位置上接收这条通告。能囤货的人、能取现金藏在家里的人、能在超市货架空掉之前赶到的人，和那些本周没买到焗豆的人，不是同一种恐惧。报道里没有出现后者。一篇写"我们怎么处理恐惧"的文章，写的是有储备空间的人怎么处理恐惧；没有储备空间的人不在分类表里，因为他们的应对方式不构成一种"风格"，只是没钱。

Hudson 自己交代得很坦白：她不会在僵尸末日里活下来，也根本不会尝试，因为"我讨厌跑步"。这是全篇最诚实的一句，也是最安全的一句。把恐惧翻译成自嘲，自嘲就变成了止痛药——它不改变任何东西，只让你在恐惧里待得舒服一点。她丈夫的版本更彻底：不用准备，因为他是天生的狩猎采集者，会抓一只松鼠、用灵魂里纯粹的决心生一堆火烤了吃。一个把生存想象成个人意志力测试的人，恰好是这套campaign最理想的接收端——恐惧被分发给个人，应对也被退回给个人，国家在这条链上只负责发出警告，不负责保证任何东西。

回到暴力三角。这条新闻里的差额不在物资上，在解释权上。政府有能力把"什么是威胁"和"什么是负责的公民"一次性定义完，公众拿到的只有一张清单和一句"能照做的就照做"。这是文化层的动作，落点在元层：谁有资格宣布危险，谁就有资格规定你的生活该围绕什么来组织。Hudson 问了一个好问题——如果真出了大事，像她这样的人都被清空了，世界会剩下一种非常特定的人类。她没问的是：这套 Be Ready 从一开始就是照着那种人设计的。

松鼠肉烤好了大概确实很难吃。但更难咽的是，一份关于恐惧的公共文本，从头到尾没有一处提到谁没有能力准备、以及国家对他们负什么责任。

The UK government has launched a campaign called Be Ready, advising people to stockpile supplies for emergencies "ranging from severe weather to cyber-attacks". Some shops sold out of baked beans and water this week. Security minister Dan Jarvis said that "those who can follow this advice should follow this advice".

The advice is empty. "Severe weather" and "cyber-attacks" share no common practical response — one wants water and tins, the other wants backups, disconnection, new passwords. Folding them into one sentence doesn't guide anyone; it manufactures the atmosphere of "anything could happen". In the same breath, the defence secretary invokes "state-sponsored terrorism" and says the threats are "not hypothetical but real, and not somewhere else but increasingly here". First the state raises the concentration of fear, then hands out a list to shop from. This is the weaponisation entry point from Chapter 3, the cognitive one: whoever defines the threat defines the response, defines what you should be afraid of and where your money should go.

Polly Hudson sorts everyone she talks to into prepper styles: panic, denial, ambivalence. She is "anxiety-tinged delusion"; her husband is the king of denial; her most suspicious friend is about to withdraw large amounts of cash to hide around the house; her most right-on friends already had everything on the checklist before the campaign existed. Their reactions, she says, each time confirmed everything she already knew about who they were.

That sentence is the spine of the piece and also its blind spot. Sorting people by their response to a government notice assumes everyone receives that notice from the same position. The people who can stockpile, who can pull out cash and hide it at home, who reach the supermarket before the shelves empty — and the people who didn't get beans this week — are not experiencing the same fear. The latter don't appear in the article. A piece about how "we" handle fear is a piece about how people with storage space handle fear. Those without storage space aren't a style; they're just poor.

Hudson is candid: she wouldn't survive the zombie apocalypse and wouldn't even try, because she despises running. That's the most honest line and the safest one. Translating fear into self-deprecation turns self-deprecation into a painkiller — it changes nothing, it just makes the fear more comfortable to sit in. Her husband's version is more complete: no prep needed, because he's a natural hunter-gatherer who'll catch a squirrel and roast it over the fire of sheer determination in his soul. A man who imagines survival as a test of personal will is exactly the ideal recipient of this campaign — fear distributed to individuals, response returned to individuals, with the state doing nothing along the chain except issuing the warning.

Back to the Violence Triangle. The gap here isn't in supplies, it's in the power to explain. The state can define, in one move, what counts as a threat and what counts as a responsible citizen; the public gets a list and a "those who can should". That's a cultural-layer move landing on the meta layer: whoever is entitled to declare danger is entitled to prescribe what your life organises itself around. Hudson asks a good question — if something terrible happens and people like her are wiped out, the world will be left with a very specific kind of human. She doesn't ask the follow-up: Be Ready was designed for that kind of human from the start.

Squirrel meat probably does taste terrible. Harder to swallow is a public text about fear that never once mentions who can't prepare, or what the state owes them.

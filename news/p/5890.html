校服不是恋物癖的错，但它是入口Ban the Uniform, Keep the Script

性别 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
禁的是制服，护的是把女孩当布景的那套叙事。
Banning the uniform protects the narrative that turns girls into scenery.

四个女孩要求色情产业停止使用校服，读者来信里有人顺势说：干脆把校服整个禁掉。这个提议听上去像在替女孩出头，实际上是把矛头从制造需求的一方挪到了穿衣服的一方。

先看清楚这件事里谁在动。色情产业把校服当作一个现成的符号，符号背后是“未成年、可被支配、穿着制服所以是场景的一部分”。这是第三章说的武器化：夺取一个日常物件，把它改写成一个性化的认知入口，然后反复投放。投放的对象不是穿校服的人，是看的人。禁校服禁不掉这个入口，只会让入口换一件衣服。

那位伦敦读者讲了自己穿明显二手的旧校服被嘲笑的经历，讲得对：校服并不能抹平阶级，它反而是阶级最直接的展示板。但她的结论跳了一步——校服暴露阶级，所以要禁校服。暴露阶级的不是校服，是学校之间、家庭之间的资源差距。把展示板拆了，差距还在，只是变得不可见，而不可见对处于下风的人从来不是好事。

更值得盯住的是第二封和第三封来信。一位读者说，几十年前她们穿着校服被吹口哨，是理所当然的事；是布兰妮那支MV让她停下来问：为什么我们在性化校服和女学生？另一位读者说，她十一岁起被老师诱骗，十五岁发生关系，离开学校后，那个男人要她穿着校服的上半身做爱，镜子里那个画面她一辈子忘不掉。

这两段话放在一起，就是元暴力的完整链条。校服被性化不是色情产业单方面干的，是整条文化流水线——电影、MV、笑话、街头口哨——长期共谋的结果。共谋者不只有拍片的人，还有把“穿校服被吹口哨”当成青春期正常经验的一整代人。那个老师之所以要她穿校服，是因为校服在他脑子里已经等于“女学生”，而“女学生”等于可以被他支配的人。他要的不是布料，是那套叙事给他的许可。

所以四个女孩的campaign真正在做的，不是清理一件衣服，是切断一条解释链：校服不等于可欲的对象，女学生不等于场景。这是cultural层的一次反击，方向是对的——她们要求成年人负责，而成年人确实该负责。

但读者来信里那个“干脆全禁”的提议，恰恰是把这条链又接了回去：既然校服会被性化，那就让女孩别穿。代价由谁付？由那些穿不起别的衣服、或者本来靠校服在校园里获得一点平等感的孩子付。施害者的入口没被堵上，被规训的是穿衣服的人。

真正没被回答的问题是：为什么几十年过去，性化女学生的叙事还是这么好卖？写信的人里没有一个是决策者，四个女孩还得自己发起campaign，成年人在旁边鼓掌。鼓掌不算负责。

Four teenage girls asked the porn industry to stop using school uniforms. A reader responds by proposing we ban school uniforms altogether. It sounds like taking the girls' side. It actually moves the target from the people manufacturing demand to the people wearing clothes.

Look at who is acting here. The porn industry takes a ready-made symbol—underage, available, dressed for the scene—and rewrites it into a sexualized cognitive entry point, then repeats it. The audience is not the person in the uniform. Ban the uniform and the entry point just changes costume.

The London reader is right that uniforms do not erase class. A visibly secondhand uniform is a class marker, and she was teased for it. But her conclusion skips a step: uniforms expose class, so ban uniforms. What exposes class is the gap between schools and between families. Remove the display board and the gap remains, only now invisible—and invisible has never helped the person on the lower end.

The second and third letters are where the real mechanism sits. One reader recalls that being catcalled in uniform was simply taken for granted, until a Britney Spears video made her stop and ask why we sexualize uniforms and schoolgirls. Another writes that she was groomed from eleven, the relationship with her teacher turned sexual at fifteen, and after she left school he asked her to wear the top half of her uniform during sex. She cannot get the mirror at the end of his bed out of her head.

Put those two letters together and you have the full chain of meta-violence. The sexualization of uniforms was not built by porn alone. It was built by film, music videos, jokes, street catcalls—a whole cultural assembly line, with co-conspirators in every generation that treated catcalling as a normal part of girlhood. The teacher wanted her in that uniform because in his head the uniform already meant schoolgirl, and schoolgirl meant someone he was permitted to dominate. He wanted the narrative's permission, not the fabric.

So the campaign by Rose, Mae, Aylin and Willow is not about laundering a garment. It is cutting an interpretive chain: uniform does not equal desirable object, schoolgirl does not equal scenery. That is a real counter-move at the cultural layer, and they are right that adults should be handling this.

But the "just ban them all" proposal plugs the chain right back in: if uniforms get sexualized, make girls stop wearing them. Who pays? The kids who cannot afford other clothes, or who get a small measure of equality from the uniform. The perpetrator's entry point stays open. The person getting disciplined is the one getting dressed.

The question nobody answers: why, decades later, is the sexualization of schoolgirls still such a good sell? Not one letter writer is a decision-maker. Four girls had to start the campaign themselves while adults applauded. Applause is not accountability.

变装皇后唱音乐剧，排名第几不重要Ranking Drag Queens Singing Musicals Misses the Point

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
男人扮女人拿奖，女人扮女人没奖
Men playing women win awards; women playing themselves don't.

Billboard 把 Drag Race 历史上每一个 Rusical 排了个名次，从最差到最好。这篇文章本身没什么可拆的，它就是一个粉丝向的榜单，判断标准是概念、执行、音乐和整体效果。但榜单里有一件事值得说：这个节目最受赞誉的挑战形式，是男人穿上女人的衣服、模仿女人的声音、扮演女人的生平，然后被评价为“camp”“iconic”“show-stopping”。

这不是在骂 Drag Race。RuPaul 的节目确实为酷儿文化打开了一个公共空间，这一点不用否认。但打开空间的方式值得看清楚：它让男性身体穿上女性表达，然后把这个过程变成可消费的娱乐产品。女性表达在这里是素材，是 costume，是可以被穿上又脱下的东西。Cher、Joan Crawford、Madonna、Julie Andrews——这些女人的生平被拆成段子，由男演员重新演绎，评委打分，观众叫好。

第一章讲表达即身份的确立。Drag Race 的 Rusical 恰恰展示了表达可以被租用。一个生理男性可以表演“Cher 的六十年代”，可以模仿“Joan Crawford 的 Mildred Pierce 时期”，可以穿上 Dolly Parton 的假发唱乡村。这些表演被称赞为“spot-on”“pitch-perfect”。但生物墙在那里：没有一个月经绞痛可以被表演，没有一次怀孕的激素风暴可以被模仿。Rusical 里的女性表达是去身体化的，是纯粹的文化符号，是可以被拆解、重组、评分的东西。

这不是说变装表演本身有问题。问题在于，当这个节目成为酷儿文化在主流媒体里最大的出口时，它输出的“女性表达”是什么样的。Billboard 的榜单里，排名最高的几个 Rusical 都有一个共同点：它们把女性历史、女性身体、女性苦难变成了 camp 的素材。Wigloose 讲的是 drag bans，这很好，它把真实的政治压迫搬上了舞台。但请注意，它讲的是 drag queen 被禁，不是女性被禁。这两个议题有重叠，但不等同。

第三章讲表达的武器化。Drag Race 的 Rusical 是一个完美的案例：它把女性表达变成了一种可以被男性身体租用的资源，然后把这个租用过程包装成进步、酷儿、反叛。观众看到的是男人在舞台上扮演女人，评委看到的是“camp”，广告商看到的是收视率。没有人问：这些被扮演的女人，她们自己的表达在哪里？

Billboard 的榜单里，Cher、Madonna、Joan Crawford 都是“gay icon”。她们的生平被拆成段子，她们的痛苦被做成笑点，她们的表达被重新编排成适合男演员唱的曲子。这不是在解放女性，这是在消费女性。而消费的方式很巧妙：它披着酷儿的外衣，让批评变得困难。你批评 Drag Race，你就是反酷儿；你指出这里的性别问题，你就是不进步。

第四章讲共谋者。Drag Race 的共谋者不只是 RuPaul 和制作团队，还包括那些为它写榜单、写评论、打分的媒体。Billboard 这篇文章的作者在评价每个 Rusical 时，用的词是“genius”“phenomenal”“show-stopping”。这些词没有错，表演确实好。但评价体系本身值得问：为什么一个男人扮演女人可以拿满分，而一个女人扮演自己却很少被这样评价？

这不是在说 Drag Race 不该存在。它该存在。但它的存在方式值得看清楚：它让男性身体在女性表达的领域里获得了最大的可见度和最高的评价，而女性身体本身在这个节目里的位置是什么？是嘉宾，是评委，是观众，是素材。

榜单的结尾说 Wigloose 是“the greatest Rusical to ever appear on RuPaul's Drag Race”。Wigloose 讲的是 drag bans，讲的是表达被禁止。这很好。但请注意：这个节目最受赞誉的时刻，是它讲自己被禁止的时刻。女性被禁止表达的时刻，在这个榜单里排第几？没有排。因为那不是 Rusical。

Billboard ranked every Rusical in Drag Race history, worst to best. The list itself is fan service—concept, execution, music, impact. But one thing in it is worth saying: the show's most celebrated challenge format is men putting on women's clothes, imitating women's voices, playing women's lives, and being praised as "camp," "iconic," "show-stopping."

This isn't an attack on Drag Race. RuPaul's show did open a public space for queer culture; that's not nothing. But look at how it opened it: male bodies put on feminine expression, and that process becomes a consumable entertainment product. Feminine expression is material here—costume, something to be put on and taken off. Cher, Joan Crawford, Madonna, Julie Andrews—their lives get broken into bits, re-performed by male actors, scored by judges, applauded by audiences.

Chapter One: expression establishes identity. The Rusical demonstrates that expression can be rented. A biological male can perform "'60s Cher," can imitate "Mildred Pierce-era Joan Crawford," can put on Dolly Parton's wig and sing country. These performances get called "spot-on," "pitch-perfect." But the biological wall is there: no menstrual cramp can be performed, no hormonal storm of pregnancy can be imitated. The feminine expression in a Rusical is disembodied—pure cultural signifier, something to be disassembled, reassembled, graded.

This doesn't mean drag performance itself is the problem. The problem is that when this show becomes the biggest mainstream outlet for queer culture, what kind of "feminine expression" does it output? The top-ranked Rusicals in Billboard's list share a trait: they turn women's history, women's bodies, women's suffering into camp material. Wigloose is about drag bans—good, it puts real political repression on stage. But note: it's about drag queens being banned, not women being banned. The two overlap, but they aren't the same.

Chapter Three: the weaponization of expression. The Rusical is a perfect case—it turns feminine expression into a resource that male bodies can rent, then packages the rental as progress, queer, rebellious. Audiences see men playing women on stage; judges see "camp"; advertisers see ratings. Nobody asks: where is these women's own expression?

Cher, Madonna, Joan Crawford are "gay icons." Their lives get broken into bits, their pain into punchlines, their expression rearranged into songs suitable for male actors. This isn't liberating women; it's consuming them. And the consumption is clever: it wears a queer costume, making criticism difficult. Criticize Drag Race and you're anti-queer; point out the gender problem and you're not progressive.

Chapter Four: co-conspirators. The co-conspirators aren't just RuPaul and the producers—they include the media writing the lists, the reviews, the scores. Billboard's writer uses words like "genius," "phenomenal," "show-stopping." Those words aren't wrong; the performances are good. But the evaluation system itself deserves a question: why does a man playing a woman get full marks, while a woman playing herself rarely gets evaluated this way?

This isn't to say Drag Race shouldn't exist. It should. But its mode of existence deserves scrutiny: it gives male bodies maximum visibility and highest praise in the field of feminine expression, while the female body's place in the show is guest, judge, audience, material.

The list ends by calling Wigloose "the greatest Rusical to ever appear on RuPaul's Drag Race." Wigloose is about drag bans, about expression being forbidden. Good. But note: the show's most celebrated moment is the moment it talks about being banned. Where does the moment women are banned from expression rank? It doesn't. Because that's not a Rusical.

冰箱被黑，谁在替谁挨饿Who Pays When the Fridges Are Hacked

其他 结构层 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
攻击的是制冷设备，代价落在食物和人的身上。
The attack hits the cooling units; the cost lands on food and people.

把超市冰柜调到五十度，是一次精确的暴力，它绕开了人，直接打在了人赖以活命的东西上。

报道说，法国那次数百家超市的制冷系统被攻击后，冰柜温度升到五十摄氏度，食物腐坏，几家店起火。这次轮到瑞典、爱尔兰、比利时、卢森堡、爱沙尼亚。瑞典零售商 Axfood 说，哥德堡地区一个负责送货上门的配送中心的冰柜被黑了。爱尔兰 SuperValu 说，223 家门店里有 13 家受影响，是某个特定型号的制冷系统出了问题。比利时的 Sabcobel 说，蒸发器被切到了除霜模式。爱沙尼亚 Kitman Coldtech 的负责人说，温度同样被设成了五十摄氏度。

这里面没有流血，也没有人被打。但暴力三角的差额是实打实的：本可以正常运转的冷链，被人为压到了不该有的状态，差额由谁承担？由那批本来要被吃掉的food承担，由等着送货上门的人承担，由那些门店的员工承担——他们要在半夜处理升温的冰柜，要在起火时跑出去。报道没有写这些人的名字，也没有写他们怎么收拾残局。

值得注意的是，报道里反复出现的一个词是“暴露”（exposed）。瑞典网络安全中心的 John Billow 说，最近几周发现瑞典有“相当多暴露在外的运营技术设备”，并强调有必要更新或打补丁。这句话才是整件事的骨架：这些制冷系统原本大部分是机械运作的，现在连上了网，连上了网就多了一个入口，而入口没有被守住。这不是什么高深的黑客艺术，这是把门开着，然后怪别人进来。

谁在为这个入口付代价？不是写代码的人，不是采购设备的人，是那些在凌晨发现冰柜温度飙到五十度的店员，是那些食物已经坏掉、却还没搞清楚发生了什么的小商户。报道说，Ahold Delhaize 上个月刚向荷兰警方报告过一起网络安全事件——也就是说，同一个坑，已经踩过一次了。

这不是一场战争，但它用的是一模一样的逻辑：找到对方最脆弱、最依赖的环节，然后让它失效。冷链是现代社会最不起眼的基础设施之一，不起眼到没人觉得它需要被保护，直到它被调成五十度。

报道里没有写的是：谁发起的攻击，为什么是超市，为什么是现在。也没有写那些被毁掉的食物最后由谁买单。冰柜可以修，补丁可以打，但那个“相当多暴露在外的设备”的数字，不会因为这一次修好了就消失。

Setting a supermarket freezer to fifty degrees is precise violence. It bypasses people and strikes directly at what people need to survive.

After the attack on refrigeration systems at hundreds of French supermarkets, reports said fridge and freezer temperatures rose to as high as 50C, food spoiled, and fires broke out at several stores. This time it was Sweden, Ireland, Belgium, Luxembourg and Estonia. Swedish retailer Axfood said freezers at a home-delivery distribution centre near Gothenburg were hacked. Ireland's SuperValu said 13 of its 223 stores were affected, a specific model of refrigeration system. Belgium's Sabcobel said evaporators were switched to defrost mode. Estonia's Kitman Coldtech said temperatures were also set to 50C.

No blood was spilled. No one was beaten. But the Violence Triangle gap is real: a cold chain that could have run normally was pushed into a state it should never be in. Who absorbs the difference? The food that was supposed to be eaten. The people waiting for home delivery. The store staff who have to deal with warming freezers overnight and run out when something catches fire. The report does not name them or describe how they cleaned up.

One word keeps appearing: exposed. John Billow, head of Sweden's national cybersecurity centre, said the country had identified "quite a lot of exposed operational technology devices" in recent weeks and stressed the need to update or patch them. That sentence is the skeleton of the whole story. These cooling systems once largely operated mechanically. Now they are online, and being online means one more entrance, and the entrance was not guarded. This is not sophisticated hacker art. This is leaving the door open and then blaming whoever walks in.

Who pays for that entrance? Not the people writing code, not the people procuring equipment. The staff who discover at 3 a.m. that the freezer is at 50C. The small merchants whose food is already ruined before anyone understands what happened. Ahold Delhaize reported a cybersecurity incident to Dutch police last month — the same hole, stepped in twice.

This is not a war, but it runs on the same logic: find the other side's most fragile, most depended-upon link, and make it fail. The cold chain is one of the least glamorous pieces of modern infrastructure, so unglamorous that no one thinks it needs protecting — until someone sets it to 50C.

What the report does not say: who launched the attack, why supermarkets, why now. Nor who ultimately pays for the destroyed food. Freezers can be fixed. Patches can be applied. But the number of "quite a lot of exposed devices" will not disappear because this one round got repaired.

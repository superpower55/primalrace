法庭上发抖的是专家，不是暴力The Expert's Hands Shake, Not the Violence

其他 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
证据再硬，解释权还在别人手里
Evidence is hard; the power to mean it belongs to someone else.

苏·布莱克说，站在法官和陪审团面前是她职业生涯里最可怕的部分之一，"我的手在抖"。一个在战区、在灾难现场辨认尸体的法医人类学家，怕的不是尸体，是法庭。这条新闻值得说的不是她个人的紧张，而是这个紧张暴露的机制：专家提供事实，法庭垄断事实的意义。

法医人类学的活是让无名者重新有名字，让一具被毁掉的身体重新被承认为一个人。这是direct层暴力之后的补救工作——人已经被杀了，被埋了，被烧了，剩下的差额是"她是谁"。布莱克做的事是把这部分差额缩到最小。但她的证据进入法庭之后，就不再属于她了。辩方可以质疑她的资质，陪审团可以不信她的判断，法官可以决定哪些话能被听见。她说的是reputational risk——名誉风险，这个词很准确：在法庭上，专家押上的是自己的可信度，而施暴者押上的是自由。两种风险不对等。

这里动的是meta层。法医人类学本身是相对年轻的学科，长期被当成"辅助性"的技术活，真正做判断的是法医病理学家、是律师、是法官。布莱克成为"Dame"、成为"世界领先"，说明这个学科拿到了一部分解释权，但拿到的只是入场券，不是话语权。她在庭上发抖，不是因为她不够权威，是因为她知道权威的最终定义权不在她手里。

报道里没有写她具体作证的是哪些案子，没有写她有没有因为证词被推翻而输掉过什么。这些缺席本身就是材料：一个专家证人的恐惧被讲成了一个职业故事，而不是一个结构问题。真正该问的是，当事实的制造权集中在法庭手里，那些提供事实的人要付出什么代价才能被听见——以及那些连专家都没有的死者，谁来替他们说话。

布莱克的手抖完了，证据交上去了，判决下来了。然后呢？下一个无名尸体还在土里，下一个专家还在庭外练习呼吸。

Sue Black says standing before a judge and jury has been one of the more terrifying parts of her career. "My hands are shaking." A forensic anthropologist who identifies bodies in war zones and disaster sites is not afraid of the dead. She is afraid of the courtroom. The story worth telling is not her nerves but the mechanism those nerves expose: experts supply facts, courts monopolize what facts mean.

The work of forensic anthropology is to give the unnamed a name again, to make a destroyed body be recognized as a person once more. That is repair after direct violence—the person is already killed, buried, burned; what remains is the gap of "who was she." Black's job is to shrink that gap. But once her evidence enters the court, it no longer belongs to her. Defense can question her credentials, the jury can disbelieve her judgment, the judge decides which words get heard. She names the reputational risk—and the word is precise: in court the expert stakes her credibility while the accused stakes his freedom. The risks are not symmetrical.

This is the meta layer moving. Forensic anthropology is a relatively young discipline, long treated as auxiliary technical work; the real judgment belongs to pathologists, lawyers, judges. Black becoming a Dame, becoming "world-leading," means the discipline has acquired part of the interpretive power—but only the ticket in, not the voice. She trembles in the witness box not because she lacks authority but because she knows who defines authority in the end.

The report does not say which cases she testified in, whether her testimony was ever overturned, what she lost. That absence is itself material: an expert witness's fear gets told as a career anecdote rather than a structural problem. The real question is what it costs those who supply facts to be heard when the power to manufacture fact sits with the court—and who speaks for the dead who have no expert at all.

Black's hands stop shaking, the evidence goes in, the verdict comes down. Then what? The next unnamed body is still in the ground, and the next expert is still practicing her breathing outside the door.

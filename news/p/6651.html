电费单上的共谋者The Co-Conspirators on the Bill

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要亚利桑那州两名民主党候选人克拉拉·普拉特和乔纳森·希尔竞选州电力公司委员会席位，该委员会负责监管水电等公用事业。凤凰城小企业主卡西利亚斯称，她的8月电费达1500美元，9月升至1700美元。委员会正考虑批准APS的14%涨价，这是该公用事业四年内第三次提价。
The newsTwo Democrats, Clara Pratte and Jonathon Hill, are running for seats on the Arizona Corporation Commission, which regulates utilities. Phoenix small business owner Casillas said her August utility bill reached $1,500 and rose to $1,700 in September. The commission is considering a 14 percent APS rate increase, the utility's third in four years.
监管者不再监管，账单就是选票。
When regulators stop regulating, the bill becomes the ballot.

Koral Casillas 的八月电费是 1,500 美元，九月是 1,700 美元。她的美国梦是开一家服务社区的小店，现在她有两家，Monsoon Market 和 Strip Mall，然后她说，她经营中最大的风险是电费。一家卖咖啡豆和纸货的小店，能货比三家地采购咖啡豆和纸货，唯独不能货比三家地选电力供应商。这就是结构暴力最朴素的样子：不是有人拿枪指着你，而是你的选项在制度里被提前删掉了。

删掉选项的是一个叫 Arizona Corporation Commission 的机构，1912 年写进州宪法，管水、管电、定电价、定电力公司能赚多少利润、定电网怎么建。亚利桑那州是十个委员靠选举产生的州之一，委员不属于州的其他权力分支，能问责他们的只有选举和诉讼。2024 年共和党拿下了全部五个席位。此后两年，委员会废掉了本州的能效标准和可再生能源标准，还通过了第一批 formula rate plans——电力公司可以按一条预先设好的公式每年自动调价，不必再走那个需要说明涨价必要性的、有监督也有透明度的费率听证程序。

这三件事单看都像技术调整，合起来是一件事：把定价的解释权，从公开听证的房间里搬进一条公式里。Arizona PIRG 的 Diane Brown 说，每一项决定都是对消费者的一记重击，合起来是一记 KO。这个说法准确，但还不够狠。公式定价的妙处在于，从此以后每一次涨价都不再是一个事件，没有一个可以被追问的现场，没有一场听证会可以让人站起来问为什么。暴力从直接层滑进了结构层，而结构层的暴力之所以能长期存在，是因为它看起来不像暴力，看起来像流程。

被废掉的能效标准，按 Brown 的说法，曾为亚利桑那人累计省下超过 17 亿美元，并在峰值需求上省下超过 2,000 兆瓦。这些数字不是抽象的政策成绩，是每个月的账单，是 Nature Conservancy 和 Arizona PIRG 的民调里那 80% 说过去两年电费涨了的亚利桑那人，是超过 60% 说夏天账单难以负担的人，是另外大约 60% 为了省钱把恒温器调得比舒适温度更高的人。在凤凰城，把恒温器调高不是环保姿态，是身体在承受代价。这条新闻里没有人中暑倒下，但有一整个州的人在为几度温差付账。

然后是数据中心。Pratte 和 Hill 引用一份分析说，2023 到 2025 年间，住户和企业为数据中心这类 large load 相关的成本付了 5.87 亿美元。APS 的回应是，这份分析得出了错误结论，把复杂问题过度简化了。APS 没有说的是，那就把账本打开。Pratte 和 Hill 要求的正是这个：让 APS 公开账目，对这笔钱到底怎么分摊启动调查。Hill 的话说得很直接，如果小企业、家庭、老人正在为数十亿美元的数据中心成本买单，那就该立刻退还。

这里就是共谋的现场。数据中心是这轮电力需求暴涨的主角，它们是大型用户，是有议价能力的一方，是能和电力公司坐下来谈 special contract 的一方。而 Casillas 这样的店主，用她自己的话说，只能和 APS 绑在一起。当一方的成本可以被设计成由另一方分摊，而设计这套分摊方式的委员会又恰好由选举产生、且刚刚被一个政党全部拿下，那么问题就不是电价太高，而是定价权被交给了谁、为谁服务。

报道里有两个细节值得停一下。一个是共和党候选人 Ralph Heap 正在接受竞选财务调查，他和另一位候选人、现任委员 Kevin Thompson 都没有回应记者的置评请求。不回应本身就是一种表达：在一个只有选举和诉讼能问责他们的位置上，沉默是成本最低的最优解。另一个是 Clara Pratte 的身份，Navajo Nation 成员，Navajo Power 的联合创始人，那家公司跟部落社区合作建可再生能源项目。她站在这个位置上说话，不是因为她更善良，而是因为她站的地方本来就离被定价的那一端更近。

所以周二这场选举，表面上是关于能源政策，实际上是一次关于解释权的投票。亚利桑那的委员席位是把定价逻辑写进规则的地方，谁坐上去，谁就决定公式长什么样、听证开不开、账本打不打开。这不是把希望寄托在某个候选人身上，这是承认规则是人写的，而写规则的人可以被换掉。

但换掉之后呢。Pratte 和 Hill 承诺透明、问责、限制过度涨价、让大型用户付该付的钱、发展可再生能源——这些都是对的方向，可它们全都停在承诺这一层。那 5.87 亿美元已经付出去了，那 17 亿美元的能效 savings 已经被废掉了，Casillas 那两张 1,500 和 1,700 的账单不会因为一次选举被退回来。报道没有写这 5.87 亿最后由谁签字分摊、公式里的系数是谁定的、那场废除能效标准的表决上每一票投给了什么理由。这些名字不出现，才是这条新闻里最该被追问的部分。

Koral Casillas' August utility bill was $1,500. September's was $1,700. Her American Dream was to leave a corporate job and run a business serving her community; now she co-owns two, Monsoon Market and Strip Mall, and she says the greatest risk facing them is the electric bill. A shop selling coffee beans and paper goods can comparison-shop for coffee beans and paper goods. It cannot comparison-shop for a utility. That is structural violence at its plainest: nobody holds a gun to your head, your options are deleted in advance by the rules.

The rules are written by the Arizona Corporation Commission, created in 1912 under the state constitution. It regulates water and power, sets what customers can be charged, how much profit utilities may make, how the grid is built and run. Arizona is one of ten states where commissioners are elected and sit outside the other branches of government, which means only elections and lawsuits can hold them accountable. In 2024 Republicans took all five seats. In the two years since, the commission repealed the state's energy efficiency standards and its renewable energy standards, and approved the first formula rate plans, letting utilities adjust rates annually by a pre-established formula instead of going through a rate hearing that requires justifying the increase with some transparency about why it is needed.

Each decision reads as a technical adjustment. Together they do one thing: move the power to explain a price out of a public hearing room and into a formula. Diane Brown of Arizona PIRG calls them a series of blows that add up to a knockout punch. Accurate, but not sharp enough. The beauty of formula pricing, from the utility's side, is that every future increase stops being an event. No hearing, no room where someone can stand up and ask why. Violence slides from the direct layer into the structural one, and structural violence endures precisely because it doesn't look like violence. It looks like process.

The repealed efficiency standard had saved Arizonans over $1.7 billion and over 2,000 megawatts in cumulative peak demand, per Brown. Those are not abstract policy scores. They are monthly bills, the 80 percent in the Nature Conservancy and Arizona PIRG polling who say their electricity bills rose in the last two years, the over 60 percent who say summer bills are hard to afford, the roughly 60 percent who keep the thermostat above comfortable temperatures to save money. In Phoenix, turning the thermostat up is not an environmental gesture. It is a body absorbing a cost. Nobody faints in this story. A whole state pays for a few degrees.

Then the data centers. Pratte and Hill cite an analysis finding that between 2023 and 2025, homeowners and businesses paid $587 million in costs tied to the large load sector, data centers among them. APS says the analysis reaches false conclusions and oversimplifies a complex issue. What APS does not say is: open the books. That is exactly what the two candidates demand, an investigation into how those costs were allocated. Hill puts it plainly: if small businesses, families and seniors are paying for billions in data center costs, those customers should be reimbursed immediately.

Here is the conspiracy, in the plain sense. Data centers are the large users driving the demand surge. They are the party with bargaining power, the party that can sit down with the utility over special contracts. Casillas, in her own words, is stuck with APS. When one party's costs can be designed onto another party's bill, and the body that designs the allocation is elected and was just swept by one party, the question is not why rates are high. The question is who holds the pricing power and whom it serves.

Two details deserve a pause. Republican candidate Ralph Heap is under a campaign finance investigation, and neither he nor sitting commissioner Kevin Thompson responded to requests for comment. Silence is an expression: in a seat where only elections and lawsuits can hold you accountable, saying nothing is the cheapest optimal expression available. The other detail is Clara Pratte, a member of the Navajo Nation and co-founder of Navajo Power, which partners with tribal communities on renewable projects. She speaks from where she stands not because she is kinder, but because where she stands is closer to the end of the line that gets priced.

So Tuesday's vote is nominally about energy policy. It is actually a vote on who holds the power to explain. The commission is where pricing logic gets written into rules; whoever sits there decides what the formula looks like, whether hearings happen, whether the books open. This is not faith in a candidate. It is the admission that rules are written by people, and people can be replaced.

And after they are replaced? Pratte and Hill promise transparency, accountability, limits on excessive increases, large users paying their share, more renewables. All correct, all still promises. The $587 million is already paid. The $1.7 billion in efficiency savings is already repealed. Casillas' two bills will not be refunded by an election. The story never says who signed off on how the $587 million was allocated, who set the coefficients in the formula, or what each commissioner said before voting to repeal the efficiency standard. Those names are missing, and that absence is the thing most worth asking about.

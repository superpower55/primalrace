偷票的人先喊抓贼The Ones Stealing Votes Shout Thief First

国际 结构层 · 文化层 · 直接层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
先定义谁是小偷，再让选票作废。
Define the thief first, then void the ballots.

《卫报》这篇社论把 Freyd 的 deny, accuse, reverse victim and offender 摆出来了：先否认，再指控，最后把加害者和受害者调个位置。特朗普正在做的就是这个——民调说共和党要丢众议院，于是先喊民主党偷票。谁在准备偷，谁先喊抓贼，这个顺序不能弄反。

2020 年的护栏是人。彭斯、巴尔这些人拒绝了他最离谱的要求。现在司法部是趴着的，最高法院把《投票权法》掏空了，选区已经重划完。这不是一次事件，是一套基础设施。法院挡住几道行政令，但暴雪式的诉讼和命令不需要每一件都赢——关键地方几千票、几百票就够了。这是 structural 层的暴力：它不制造流血，它制造“结果可疑”这个前提。

最阴的一手是让合法选民自己退场。移民执法人员在投票站附近站不站，报道说还是“传闻中的设想”；加州那桩非公民投票的联邦调查最后怎么样，也没人知道。但威胁不需要落地就有效——一个合法选民会开始算，我去投票会不会把没有身份的亲戚牵出来。这是把 direct 层的恐惧当杠杆，撬动 structural 层的参与率。差额就出在这里：本可以投的票，没投。

阿拉斯加那段是这篇社论最诚实的地方。民主党候选人 Mary Peltola 的团队被《纽约时报》曝出找了一个和共和党对手同名的人来搅浑选票，竞选方否认。社论说这招又老又脏，不必然违法，但不道德，可能招致刑事指控，而且不是质疑这场选举结果的依据。这话说得对，但顺序要摆正：一个下作的小动作，和一套从司法部到州议会到投票站门口的整套机器，不是一个量级。共和党拿它当礼物，正好说明他们缺的从来不是证据，是借口。

社论给出的两道防线，一道是人，一道是票。选举官员——包括共和党人——在挨着辱骂和死亡威胁的情况下还在准备抵抗，这值得写下来，因为这些人正在付代价。但同一句话里也藏着坏消息：共和党在系统性地夺取选举委员会的控制权，否认选举结果的人一个接一个坐进去。人的防线正在被换人。

第二道防线是提前投票的激增。巴西的例子被拿来对照：博索纳罗式的候选人被选民明确否决，明确的裁决比微弱的裁决更难推翻。这个判断是对的，也是这篇社论唯一指向出口的地方。但要注意它把责任放在了谁身上——放在选民身上。民主最好的防御是让人们表现出他们在乎。这句话没错，但它同时是一次转移：制度已经被掏空到需要公民用投票率来当护栏，而掏空制度的人还在制度里。

真正没被写出来的是：如果结果很接近，或者某个州的州长就是不认证，票投得再多也拦不住。社论说“裁决越清晰，守住它的前景越好”，可清晰与否不由投票的人决定，由计票和认证的人决定。这道差额，这篇社论自己也还没补上。

The Guardian editorial puts Jennifer Freyd's pattern on the table: deny, accuse, reverse victim and offender. That is exactly what Trump is doing. Polls say Republicans may lose the House, so the cheating narrative goes out first. Whoever is preparing the heist is the one shouting thief, and the order matters.

In 2020 the guardrail was people. Pence and Barr refused his most outrageous demands. Now the Justice Department is supine, the Supreme Court has gutted the Voting Rights Act, and districts have already been redrawn. This is not an event. It is infrastructure. Courts block a few executive orders, but a blizzard of them does not need to win every time. A few thousand votes, or a few hundred, in the right places is enough. This is structural violence: it spills no blood, it manufactures the premise that the result is suspect.

The nastiest move is making legitimate voters exit on their own. Whether ICE officers actually stand near polling places is still, per the editorial, a mooted idea; what happens to the federal investigation into alleged non-citizen voting in California is unknown. The threat does not need to land to work. A legitimate voter starts calculating whether voting will drag an undocumented relative into the light. Direct-level fear is the lever; structural-level turnout is what moves. The gap is the votes that could have been cast and were not.

The Alaska passage is where the editorial is most honest. Mary Peltola's campaign was reported by the New York Times to have recruited a candidate sharing her Republican opponent's name to confuse voters; the campaign denies it. The editorial calls the trick old and dirty, not inherently illegal, unethical, possibly criminal, and no grounds for contesting the race. Correct, but keep the scale straight. A grubby little maneuver and a machine running from the Justice Department to state boards to the sidewalk outside the polling station are not the same order of thing. Republicans treat it as a gift because what they have never lacked is an excuse, not evidence.

The editorial offers two defenses: people and votes. Election officials, Republicans included, are preparing to resist while absorbing abuse and death threats. That deserves to be written down, because these people are paying a price. The same sentence carries the bad news: Republicans are systematically taking over election boards, and election deniers keep getting seated. The human guardrail is being replaced.

The second defense is the surge in early voting. Brazil is the comparison: a candidate in Trump's mold was decisively rejected, and a decisive verdict is harder to challenge than a marginal one. True, and it is the only exit the editorial points to. But notice where it puts the burden: on voters. The best defense of democracy is showing that people care. Fine, and also a displacement. The institutions have been hollowed out to the point where turnout has to serve as the guardrail, while the people who hollowed them out are still inside them.

What goes unsaid: if the result is close, or a governor simply declines to certify, no amount of turnout stops it. The editorial says a clearer verdict is easier to uphold. But clarity is not decided by the people who vote. It is decided by the people who count and certify. That gap the editorial has not closed either.

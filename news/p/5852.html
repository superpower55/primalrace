他们把强奸案做成了竞选广告They Turned a Rape Case Into a Campaign Ad

性别 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
受害者的伤口不是素材，除非有人一直允许它被这么用。
A survivor's wound is not material — unless someone keeps letting it be used.

一条广告把强奸案的受害史剪成攻击对手的武器，然后两党一起说“这条线不能过”。这就是全部新闻。

先看清发生了什么。俄亥俄州州长共和党候选人 Ramaswamy 发布广告，出镜的是民主党对手 Amy Acton 的 estranged 母亲，母亲在片子里指控女儿撒谎，叫俄亥俄人别投她。Acton 六十岁，说她十二岁起就没再和母亲说过话——那年她和弟弟被带离母亲的监护，因为继父被捕并被控强奸她。她最后一次见母亲是在法院，母亲来支持丈夫，而女儿在庭上作证指控他。

这不是“家庭纠纷被政治化”，这是元暴力在竞选广告里的标准操作：把一个女人一生中最无力的一刻——十二岁，站在法庭上，对着母亲和继父——重新剪接成一段供公众消费的叙事，解释权完全交给出镜的人。Acton 的故事只有在她自己讲述时才成立；一旦被 estranged 母亲拿去用，它就变成“她在撒谎”。同一个事实，两个版本，谁有播出渠道谁就是事实。这就是第三章说的制造真实的艺术，只是这次的工具是三十秒的 spot。

然后两党出来谴责。DeWine 说“播这条广告是错的”，Vance 说“别抱怨了”。广告在民调和压力下撤了。撤了。撤掉的是播出，不是那套机制——那套机制是：女性的创伤可以被任意一方的竞选团队征用、剪辑、投放，只要它“有效”；无效了再撤。这条“罕见的红线”划在哪儿？划在“用了 estranged 母亲”这个具体手法上，而不是划在“把强奸受害史当弹药”这件事上。红线是被民调画出来的，不是被原则画出来的。

报道里还有别的：密歇根 El-Sayed 被铺天盖地的广告攻击他的宗教，被无根据地指与伊斯兰恐怖组织有关联；德州 Paxton 和盟友投放大量广告——很多是 AI 生成的——把对手 Talarico 说成 feminate，歪曲他在跨性别议题上的立场，Vance 还无根据地暗示他是 gay。这些和 Acton 那条是同一条流水线：把一个人的身份表达——宗教、性别气质、性取向、家庭创伤——变成攻击面。身份在这里不是被讨论的对象，是被征用的武器。

报道结尾写，俄亥俄的选民星期二要在两个人之间选：一个十二岁出庭作证指控母亲丈夫的女人，和一个把这段黑暗故事扭成政治广告的男人。这个二选一写得漂亮，但它把问题收得太窄了。真正的选择不是这两个人，是还要不要继续让竞选把女人的身体和伤口当战场。广告撤了，这个战场还在。

还有一段值得停一下：Acton 2020 年做州卫生局长，因每日简报清楚而被称赞，后来封控反弹，她家门口出现武装抗议，遭到反犹侮辱和假信息攻击；今年秋天一场竞选活动上，一个持械男子在她讲话时扑向她，被逮捕，她没受伤，还留下来给现场一位受伤的旁观者治疗。一个医生，被人扑，然后给人看病。这条线从疫情到竞选没断过——她一直是靶子，只是靶子的理由每次换一个。

所以那条“罕见的红线”是什么？是有人终于说了一句“这太过分了”。说过分的人里，有 endorsement 了 Ramaswamy 的 DeWine。他们谴责的是手法，不是逻辑。逻辑没被动过：女人的创伤可以上广告，只要剪辑得体面一点、或者等民调变差再撤。

Acton 的母亲在法院里站在丈夫那边，竞选团队在电视上站在母亲那边。两次，同一个女人被放在需要自证的位置上。第一次她十二岁，第二次她六十岁。中间隔了四十八年，机制没变。

An ad cuts a rape survivor's history into a weapon against her, and then both parties say "this line can't be crossed." That is the whole news.

Start with what happened. Vivek Ramaswamy, the Republican nominee for governor of Ohio, released an attack ad featuring the estranged mother of his Democratic opponent, Amy Acton. In it, the mother accuses her daughter of lying and tells Ohioans not to vote for her. Acton, 60, says she has not spoken to her mother since she was 12, when she and her brother were removed from their mother's custody after their stepfather was arrested and charged with raping her. The last time she saw her mother was at the courthouse, where her mother came to support her husband while her daughter testified against him.

This is not "a family dispute politicized." This is meta-violence in a campaign ad: a woman's most powerless moment — twelve years old, in a courtroom, facing her mother and stepfather — recut into a narrative for public consumption, with the interpretive authority handed entirely to the person on screen. Acton's story only holds when she tells it. Once the estranged mother takes it, it becomes "she is lying." Same fact, two versions; whoever has the broadcast channel is the fact. This is the art of manufacturing reality from chapter three, except the tool this time is a thirty-second spot.

Then both parties condemn it. DeWine says "it's wrong to run this ad." Vance says "stop whining." The ad comes down under pressure and polling. It comes down. What comes down is the broadcast, not the machinery — the machinery being: a woman's trauma can be requisitioned, cut, and aired by either campaign as long as it works; pulled when it stops working. Where exactly is this "rare red line"? It is drawn around the specific tactic of using the estranged mother, not around using a rape survivor's history as ammunition. The line was drawn by polling, not by principle.

There is more in the piece: in Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed faces a barrage of ads attacking his religion and baselessly claiming ties to Islamic terrorist organizations. In Texas, Ken Paxton and his allies pump out ads — many AI-generated — calling his opponent James Talarico effeminate and misstating his position on transgender rights; JD Vance piles on by baselessly musing that Talarico is gay. Same assembly line: turn a person's expression — religion, gender presentation, sexuality, family trauma — into an attack surface. Identity here is not the subject of debate. It is the weapon being requisitioned.

The piece closes by saying Ohio voters on Tuesday must choose between a woman who at 12 testified against her mother's husband and a man who let that dark story be twisted into a political ad. It is a good line, but it narrows the problem too much. The real choice is not between these two people. It is whether campaigns get to keep using women's bodies and wounds as a battlefield. The ad is down. The battlefield is not.

One more thing worth pausing on: Acton rose to prominence in 2020 as Ohio's health director, praised for clear daily briefings, then became a target of armed protests outside her home, antisemitic slurs, and misinformation as lockdown backlash brewed. This fall, at a campaign event, an armed man was arrested after lunging at her as she spoke. She was not injured, and she stayed on site to treat an onlooker injured in the episode. A physician, lunged at, then treating someone. The line from pandemic to campaign never broke — she has been a target throughout, only the reason for the targeting keeps changing.

So what is the rare red line? It is that someone finally said "this is too much." Among those saying it is DeWine, who has endorsed Ramaswamy. They condemned the tactic, not the logic. The logic is untouched: a woman's trauma can go on the air, as long as the edit is presentable, or as long as the polling holds. When it doesn't, pull it.

Acton's mother stood by her husband in a courtroom. A campaign stood by the mother on television. Twice, the same woman was placed in the position of having to prove herself. The first time she was twelve. The second time she was sixty. Forty-eight years apart, and the mechanism has not changed.

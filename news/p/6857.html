游戏没出，谜语先卖了四年Four Years of Selling the Riddle, Not the Game

文娱 文化层 Polygon.com ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要小岛秀夫的新作《OD》公布四年后仍细节稀少，小岛制作10周年展览（东京麻布台Hills画廊，展至12月20日）放出新宣传图：Sophia Lillis的角色注视一个悬浮的红色物体，配文「For all players and screamers」。粉丝据此猜测女巫题材，并争论这张图是游戏内渲染还是真人照片。
The newsFour years after its announcement, Hideo Kojima's OD remains largely a mystery. A new promotional image at Kojima Productions' 10th anniversary exhibition in Tokyo (running through Dec. 20) shows Sophia Lillis' character looking at a levitating red object, with the tagline "For all players and screamers." Fans are guessing at witches and debating whether the image is an in-game render or a live-action photo.
悬念不是叙事，是提前上市的一种商品。
Mystery isn't narrative here; it's a product shipped years early.

《OD》公布了四年，我们对它的全部了解是：恐怖题材、Jordan Peele参与、想请一群恐怖作家组个「Avengers」。这不是信息稀缺，这是信息被有意做成了产品。

小岛在播客里说得很明白：他要「保持安静」，把游戏发出来，维持神秘感。一个创作者决定少说话，本来没什么可指摘的。但把这句话和展览、宣传图、配文「For all players and screamers」放在一起看，机制就清楚了——不提供内容，只提供线索；不交付叙事，只交付可供解谜的空壳。神秘感不是作品自带的属性，是被生产出来、按节奏投放的。

再看这张图本身。一个红扫帚、一面米色墙、一句标语，没有任何东西能让人判断这游戏是什么。粉丝在争论它究竟是引擎渲染还是真人照片——注意这场争论的性质：它讨论的不是作品好不好，而是这张图是用什么技术做出来的。技术成了内容，分辨率成了叙事。当一张宣传图的全部信息量都集中在「这是不是真的」上面，它卖的就不是故事，是制作水准的展示柜。

报道里最诚实的一句话是那句「粉丝在猜」。猜 witches，猜 Hunter Schafer 的角色劫持了一架飞机，猜红色门和红色扫帚是同一套符号。这些猜测没有一条来自官方确认，全部由观众自己生产，然后被下一轮宣传图回收、再投放。这是最省成本的一种参与感：让观众自己写剧本，创作者只负责点头或者不摇头。

这不只是小岛一个人的手法。整个行业的宣发逻辑都在往这个方向走——预告片里打码，采访里说「不能说太多」，展会上摆一张让人截图的东西。它有效，因为它把等待本身变成了内容，把空白变成了可消费的悬念。代价是：等游戏真的出来，它要先对得起四年的谜语，才有资格被当成一部作品评价。

至于报道里那些卡片上写的「dare」，那些泄露出来的照片，那些被反复咀嚼的红色——它们都是同一个空壳的不同切面。四年了，我们知道的还是那三件事：恐怖、Peele、作家团。剩下的全是观众自己填的。填得越满，越说明壳是空的。

Four years after the announcement, everything we know about OD is this: it's horror, Jordan Peele contributed, and Kojima wants an "Avengers" lineup of horror writers. That isn't scarcity of information. That's information deliberately manufactured into a product.

Kojima said it plainly on a podcast: he wants to "keep quiet" and release the game to preserve the mystery. A creator choosing to say less is not a crime. But put that next to the exhibition, the teaser image, the tagline "For all players and screamers," and the mechanism is clear: no content, only clues; no narrative, only a shell built to be decoded. Mystery isn't a property the work happens to have. It's produced and released on a schedule.

Then look at the image itself. A red broom, a beige wall, a slogan. Nothing there lets you judge what the game is. Fans are arguing over whether it's an in-game render or a live-action photo — note what that argument is actually about: not whether the work is good, but what technology produced the picture. Technique becomes content; resolution becomes narrative. When the entire information payload of a promotional image sits in the question "is this real," it isn't selling a story. It's selling a display case for production values.

The most honest line in the report is that fans are guessing. Witches, a hijacked plane, a red door and a red broom as one symbolic set. None of it is confirmed. All of it is produced by the audience, then recycled into the next round of images. It's the cheapest possible form of participation: let the audience write the script, and the creator only has to nod or not shake his head.

This isn't one man's trick. The whole industry's marketing logic is drifting this way — redacted trailers, "I can't say too much" interviews, one screenshot-able object on an exhibition wall. It works because it turns waiting itself into content, turns blank space into something consumable. The cost: when the game finally arrives, it has to live up to four years of riddles before it's allowed to be judged as a work at all.

As for the dare cards, the leaked photos, the red being chewed over again and again — they're all facets of the same empty shell. Four years on, we still know three things: horror, Peele, writers. The rest is audience filler. The fuller it gets, the emptier the shell shows itself to be.

「混乱的中间」是谁的中间Whose Middle Is the Messy Middle?

其他 文化层 · 元暴力 NPR ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要NPR 报道称，美国政治极化程度没有人们以为的那么极端：心理学家 Kristin Laurin 指出，人们回避谈论政治，导致彼此高估分歧；皮尤研究中心把美国人按价值观分为九组，称约 60% 的选民属于跨党派的「混乱中间」，而最右的两组只占 21%。报道以两位五十出头的朋友 Shanna Berry 和 Alma Ong 为例：两人政治立场不同，但对美国抓捕委内瑞拉总统马杜罗的看法几乎一致。
The newsNPR reports that Americans are less polarized than they think: psychologist Kristin Laurin says people avoid political talk and therefore overestimate their differences. Pew Research Center sorts Americans into nine value-based groups and estimates about 60% of the electorate sits in a messy cross-partisan middle, while the two furthest-right groups make up just 21%. The story centers on two friends in their early 50s, Shanna Berry and Alma Ong, who disagree politically but largely agreed on the U.S. capture of Venezuela's president Maduro.
说极化是错觉的人，先定义了什么叫极端。
Before you call polarization an illusion, you define what counts as extreme.

这篇报道的结论是：美国没那么极化，中间那 60% 才是大多数。它给出的证据，是一对朋友对抓捕马杜罗的看法几乎一致。

先看这个证据本身。Berry 说「政权的终结是必要的」，同时承认自己怀疑这件事「很大程度上和拿到委内瑞拉的石油有关」；Ong 听完说「我完全同意她说的每一句话」。两个人达成共识的那句话，是一个支持特朗普的空军退伍军人对一场境外抓捕的将信将疑。她们一致的部分，不是「不该抓」，而是「抓了也许有必要，但我不知道合不合法，而且大概是为了石油」。

这不是跨越分歧的共识，这是分歧被外包出去之后的空白。两个人都没有掌握做出判断所需的东西：法律依据、行动细节、谁授权、代价是什么。于是她们只能凭直觉，而直觉恰好落在同一个模糊地带。报道把这块空白叫做「混乱的中间」，皮尤把它量化为 60% 的选民。一个由信息缺失造成的重合，被统计成了政治温和。

再说「极端」是怎么被定义的。皮尤把最右的两组命名为「No Apologies Right」和「Faith First Conservatives」，合计 21%；左翼最远的那一组，报道里连名字都没给，也没给数字。于是「中间很大、两端很小」这个结论，是在只标出一端刻度的情况下得出的。谁被算作「意识形态左翼的最远处」，读者不知道。一个不标出另一端刻度的钟形曲线，画出来的不是民意分布，是取景框。

报道自己给出了这套取景框的制造者：政客把对手描绘成极端分子来动员选民，社交媒体放大愤怒来换取 engagement。Laurin 说得对，perceived polarization 远超 actual polarization。但她停在了「感知」这一层。谁在生产这个感知，生产它的人拿到了什么，报道没有往下问。政客靠它赢选举，平台靠它赢停留时长，这两拨人在「美国人其实很温和」的叙事里毫发无损，甚至还被这篇报道洗白成了误会的一部分。

真正被这篇报道盖住的，是那些没有被做成问卷选项的分歧。皮尤的九个组按「价值观和态度」划分，问卷问的是移民、种族、经济。它不问：女性该不该对自己的身体有最终决定权；一个跨性别者进女厕所是不是威胁；家暴受害者能不能拿到庇护。这些不是「态度」，是有人正在用身体承受的分配结果。它们在这套测量里没有位置，于是持有强烈立场的人被平均进了「混乱的中间」，看起来像是没有立场。

方法论上还有一层：报道用「人们不愿谈政治」解释感知鸿沟。但不愿谈，本身就是一个结果。在一个谈错话会丢工作、会被网暴、会被家人切断关系的环境里，沉默不是温和，是成本核算。把沉默读成共识，等于把噤声读成同意。

Fletcher 说，95% 的美国人都在钟形曲线中间，你听到的只是两端的疯子。这句话很舒服。舒服的地方在于，它让每个读者都自动站进了那 95%，而把提出尖锐问题的人归进「疯子」。这就是元暴力最日常的运作方式：不是禁止你说话，是让你一开口就显得不体面。

报道里最诚实的一句话来自 Kiley：「我们是一个高度极化的国家，而在这之下也有很多细微差别。」NPR 把这句话放在了倒数第二段。标题取的是前半句的反面。

那 60% 的中间里，有多少人是真的想清楚了，有多少人只是没被告知，报道没有分开。这个数字不分开，任何关于「团结大多数」的政治都只是在对着空白说话——而空白，是最容易被填进任何东西的地方。

The conclusion of this piece is that America isn't that polarized: the 60% in the middle are the real majority. Its evidence is two friends who largely agree about the U.S. capture of Maduro.

Look at that evidence. Berry says the end of the regime was necessary, while admitting she suspects it had a strong leaning toward access to Venezuela's oil. Ong says she agrees with absolutely everything Berry just said. What they agree on is a Trump-supporting Air Force veteran's ambivalence about a foreign capture. Their shared ground isn't "don't do it." It's "maybe it was necessary, I don't know about the legality, and it was probably about oil."

That isn't consensus across a divide. It's the blank space left after the disagreement has been outsourced. Neither of them has what a judgment would require: the legal basis, the operational details, who authorized it, what it cost. So they fall back on instinct, and their instincts happen to land in the same fog. The piece calls this fog the messy middle. Pew measures it at 60% of the electorate. A coincidence produced by missing information gets counted as political moderation.

Then look at how extreme gets defined. Pew names the two furthest-right groups, the No Apologies Right and the Faith First Conservatives, at 21% combined. The furthest-left group gets no name in this piece and no number. So the finding that the middle is big and the ends are small rests on a scale with only one end marked. Who counts as the far edge of the ideological left? The reader isn't told. A bell curve with one tail unlabeled isn't a distribution of public opinion. It's a crop.

The piece names the people who manufacture this crop: politicians who paint opponents as extremists to fire up voters, platforms that amplify rage for engagement. Laurin is right that perceived polarization far outstrips actual polarization. But she stops at perception. Who produces it, and what they get for producing it, goes unasked. Politicians win elections on it; platforms win watch time on it. Both come out of this story not just untouched but laundered into a misunderstanding.

What gets buried is the disagreement that never made it onto the questionnaire. Pew's nine groups are sorted by values and attitudes, and the questions are about immigration, race, the economy. They don't ask whether women should have the final say over their own bodies, whether a trans person in a women's restroom is a threat, whether a domestic violence survivor can get shelter. Those aren't attitudes. They're allocations that people are absorbing with their bodies. They have no slot in this instrument, so people who hold strong views on them get averaged into the messy middle and come out looking like they hold none.

There's a methodological layer too. The piece explains the perception gap by saying people don't want to talk politics. But not wanting to talk is itself an outcome. In an environment where saying the wrong thing costs you a job, a mob, or a family, silence isn't moderation. It's cost accounting. Reading silence as consensus is reading being silenced as consent.

Fletcher says 95% of America sits in the middle of the bell curve and all you hear are the wackos on either end. That's a comfortable sentence. It's comfortable because it lets every reader place themselves inside the 95% and file anyone asking a sharp question under wacko. This is how meta-violence works on an ordinary day: it doesn't forbid you to speak. It makes speaking look unseemly.

The most honest line in the piece is Kiley's: we are a highly polarized country, and there's also a lot of nuance underneath that. NPR put it in the second-to-last paragraph. The headline took the opposite of its first half.

How much of that 60% has actually thought it through, and how much simply hasn't been told, the piece never separates. Until that number is split, any politics of rallying the reasonable majority is talking to a blank space. And blank space is the easiest thing in the world to fill with anything.

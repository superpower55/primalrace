棒球外交的投手是谁Who's on the Mound in Diamond Diplomacy

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 The Hollywood Reporter ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
用棒球讲友谊，用友谊盖住谁在投球
A film about friendship that hides who is pitching.

《Diamond Diplomacy》这部片子的题目已经把答案写好了：diplomacy。一部讲日美关系的纪录片，选棒球做棱镜，等于承认这不是体育片，是外交片。而外交叙事的第一件事，从来是决定谁站在投手丘上。

片子里的高光时刻是1934年贝比·鲁斯访日，报道说这趟巡演"或许被夸张地"记作推迟了日美开战。这句"perhaps hyperbolically"是全文最诚实的地方——一个美国明星在日本球场被几万人围着欢呼，然后七年后是珍珠港。棒球没有推迟战争，它只是让战争前后的叙事都好看一点。这就是文化暴力最擅长的活：让结构性的东西看起来像友谊。

更值得看的是村上雅则那段。1964年他在旧金山投出一个明显的好球被判坏球，他把球摔在地上，和裁判对峙。赛后日料店老板握着他的手说，战后日本人觉得自己必须顺从美国人，所以看到他为自己站出来，很骄傲。这段话是全片真正的核心：一个十九岁球员在投手丘上的一次发火，被读成了整个族群战后姿态的松动。体育从来不是政治的例外，它是政治最便宜的表达出口——一个人替几百万人出了一口气，然后这口气被收进纪录片的温情段落里。

还有一段被轻轻带过：黑人联盟球队访日，球员在日本受到的待遇比在国内好，日本东道主夸他们谦逊、有体育精神，并拿这个对比白人球队的傲慢。这是同一套机制的另一面——美国把自己的种族等级带出国门，日本用"尊重"接住了它，双方都在这场比较里拿到了自己想要的位置。谁在共谋？两边都在，回报是各自叙事里一个体面的位置。

钱的部分也不能漏。国家人文基金会给了六十万美元，2025年4月DOGE砍掉了其中一半以上，最后是前外交官Glen S. Fukushima补了三十万把片子做完。一部讲日美友谊的片子，靠一个前外交官的钱收尾——这不是丑闻，这是这类文化产品的标准融资结构：国家出前半段，私人关系出后半段，成片讲的是两国人民的情感。

片子本身拍得大概不坏，Romer花了十二年。但十二年研究出来的结论是"棒球连接了两个国家"，这个结论太安全了，安全到可以拿政府拨款、可以进东京国际电影节、可以让MLB的人说时机正好。真正的棱镜应该照出的是：谁被这套友谊叙事排除在外，谁的身体在球场上被消耗，谁的历史在"共同热爱"里被抹平。片子没问这些。

贝比·鲁斯听到珍珠港的消息，把日本纪念品全从窗户扔了出去。这个画面比任何外交辞令都诚实：友谊是纪念品，战争一来就扔出窗外。而纪录片把它捡回来，擦干净，放回展柜。

The title already gives the answer away: diplomacy. A documentary about US-Japan relations that uses baseball as its prism is admitting it isn't a sports film. It's a foreign-policy film. And the first thing any diplomatic narrative does is decide who stands on the mound.

The centerpiece is Babe Ruth's 1934 tour, credited—perhaps hyperbolically, the reporting notes—with delaying war between the US and Japan. That "perhaps hyperbolically" is the most honest line in the piece. An American star is mobbed by tens of thousands in a Japanese stadium, and seven years later comes Pearl Harbor. Baseball didn't delay the war. It just made the storytelling on both sides of the war look better. That's what cultural violence does best: make structural arrangements look like friendship.

More revealing is Masanori Murakami. In 1964, in San Francisco, a pitch of his that was clearly a strike got called a ball. He threw the ball down and squared up to the umpire. Afterward, a Japanese restaurant chef shook his hand and told him he was proud—because since the war, Japanese people had felt they had to be subservient to Americans, and here was someone standing up for himself. That's the real core of the film: a nineteen-year-old's flash of temper on the mound gets read as the loosening of an entire people's postwar posture. Sport is never the exception to politics. It's politics' cheapest expressive outlet—one man lets out a breath for millions, and the breath gets filed away in a warm documentary segment.

Then there's the part that gets glossed over: Negro League teams touring Japan, treated far better there than at home, with Japanese hosts praising their respectfulness and sportsmanship and contrasting it with the arrogance of visiting white teams. That's the same mechanism from another angle—the US exports its racial hierarchy, Japan receives it with "respect," and both sides get what they want from the comparison. Who's complicit? Both. The payoff is a respectable seat in each other's narrative.

And the money. The National Endowment for the Humanities put up $600,000. In April 2025, DOGE cuts cancelled more than half of it. Former diplomat Glen S. Fukushima stepped in with $300,000 to finish the film. A movie about US-Japan friendship, completed with a former diplomat's check—that's not a scandal. That's the standard financing structure for this kind of cultural product: the state pays for the first half, private connections pay for the second, and the finished film is about the feelings of two peoples.

The film itself is probably fine. Romer spent twelve years on it. But twelve years of research produced the conclusion that baseball connects two nations—a conclusion safe enough to win a government grant, screen at the Tokyo International Film Festival, and let MLB people say the timing is perfect. A real prism would show who gets excluded from the friendship narrative, whose bodies get consumed on the field, whose history gets flattened under "shared passion." The film doesn't ask.

When Babe Ruth heard about Pearl Harbor, he threw all his Japan souvenirs out the window. That image is more honest than any diplomatic phrase: friendship is a souvenir, and war throws it out the window. The documentary picks it back up, dusts it off, and puts it back in the display case.

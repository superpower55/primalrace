他给产妇的账单标了价He Put a Price on Mothers

性别 结构层 · 文化层 The New Republic ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要堪萨斯州共和党参议员、前妇产科医生罗杰·马歇尔被前患者齐默尔曼提起集体诉讼，指控其诊所对贫困患者收取18%的年利率，超过该州15%的上限。齐默尔曼2009年因3596美元分娩账单被起诉，追债过程中三次被捕。马歇尔正面临选情胶着的连任竞选。
The newsRepublican Senator Roger Marshall, a former OB-GYN, faces a class-action lawsuit from former patient Meischa Zimmerman, who says his practice charged an 18 percent annual interest rate on medical debt, above Kansas's 15 percent cap. Zimmerman was sued over a $3,596 childbirth bill in 2009 and arrested three times during collection. Marshall is in a tight reelection race.
追债追到逮捕，是结构暴力借了法律的手
Debt collection that ends in arrest is structural violence borrowing the law's hand

一个参议员被自己的前患者告了，罪名是把穷人的分娩账单做成一门收息的生意。这件事的重点不在18%比15%多出的那三个点，而在于：一个医生可以合法地拥有一套追债机器，而这套机器运转的终点是手铐。

报道写得很清楚。齐默尔曼2009年是马歇尔的产科病人，3596美元的分娩账单，18%的年息，被起诉，然后在追债过程中三次被捕。三次。堪萨斯州法规定医生和商家最多收15%，除非在事先的合同里写明更高的利率。马歇尔和他的诊所Heartland Regional OBGYN给病人开的是18%，病人大多是低收入、无保险或保险不足的母亲。这不是失误，这是定价——给一群没有议价能力的人定一个他们跑不掉的价格。

加尔通的公式在这里很好用：Violence = Potential − Actual。这些母亲本可以得到的，是分娩之后抱着孩子回家的那段日子；她们实际得到的，是法院传票和逮捕记录。差额就是暴力，而施暴者不是某个情绪失控的坏人，是一份写好的利率、一套运转正常的司法程序、一个知道自己在做什么的医生。结构暴力的特点就是没有人在现场挥拳，但每一拳都落在了具体的人身上。

更值得看的是这件事为什么现在才被翻出来。纽约时报上个月报道他起诉了堪萨斯州巴顿县数百名病人，那里很多人生活在贫困中、没有医保。然后才是这起集体诉讼，然后才是选举前四天。马歇尔的席位在通常稳固的堪萨斯变成了战场，特朗普和万斯飞去为他站台，民调里牧师亚当·汉密尔顿略微领先。汉密尔顿在辩论里说，圣经里有两千节经文讲要照顾穷人，而这个人身上看不到。

注意这个转折的机制：马歇尔对穷人收18%的息、起诉他们、让追债走到逮捕这一步，这些事在他当医生的时候就已经发生了，不是新闻。它成为新闻，是因为他现在需要选票。这不是说揭露没有价值，而是说，一个结构性的盘剥能被看见，往往不是因为受害者终于被听见，而是因为加害者恰好在一个需要被审视的位置上。齐默尔曼的声音在2009年就在那里，只是那时候没有选举。

还有一个细节不能放过：18%这个数字。州法给了15%的上限，又留了一个例外——只要在事先的合同里写明就可以更高。这个例外就是共谋的入口。它看起来中立，是契约自由，是双方同意；但一个躺在产床上、即将分娩、没有保险的女人，和一个拥有诊所、律师和追债经验的医生，签下的是同一份合同吗？所谓「自愿」，在权力不对等的结构里，是一个需要被追问的陈述。这条法律的例外条款，就是为这种不对等预留的合法通道。

集体诉讼能不能赢，报道没说。马歇尔会不会落选，报道也没说，只说民调里挑战者略微领先。但有一件事是确定的：那三次逮捕记录会一直留在齐默尔曼的档案里，不管这场官司怎么判，不管星期二谁赢。差额被缩小了一点，但没有被抹平。

A senator is being sued by his former patient, and the charge is that he turned poor people's childbirth bills into an interest-bearing business. The point is not the three percentage points between 18 and 15. The point is that a doctor could own a debt-collection machine whose endpoint was handcuffs.

The reporting is plain. Meischa Zimmerman was Marshall's obstetrics patient in 2009. A $3,596 bill for delivering her child, an 18 percent annual interest rate, a lawsuit against her, and then three separate arrests during the collection process. Three. Kansas law caps interest for doctors and other businesses at 15 percent, with an exception for higher rates specified in a preexisting contract. Marshall and his practice, Heartland Regional OBGYN, charged 18 percent to patients who were mostly low-income, uninsured, or underinsured mothers. This was not a clerical error. This was pricing, set for people with no bargaining power and no exit.

Galtung's formula fits: Violence = Potential − Actual. What these mothers could have had was the weeks after birth, holding their children. What they got was court papers and arrest records. The gap is the violence, and the agent is not a bad man losing his temper. It is a written interest rate, a functioning court system, and a doctor who knew exactly what he was doing. Structural violence has no one throwing punches on the scene, yet every punch lands on a specific body.

More telling is why this surfaced now. The New York Times reported last month that he had sued hundreds of patients in rural Barton County, Kansas, where many residents live in poverty and lack health insurance. Then came the class action. Then came four days before the election. Marshall's seat, normally safe Republican territory in Kansas, is now in play; Trump and Vance flew in to campaign for him, and Pastor Adam Hamilton, his Democratic challenger, holds a slight lead in recent polling. Hamilton said in a debate that the Bible has 2,000 verses about caring for the poor, and that this is not happening under this man.

Notice the mechanism of the turn. The 18 percent, the lawsuits, the collections that ended in arrest—all of it happened while Marshall was a doctor. None of it was news. It became news because he now needs votes. This is not to say exposure is worthless. It is to say that a structural extraction becomes visible not when the victims are finally heard, but when the extractor happens to occupy a position that requires scrutiny. Zimmerman's voice was there in 2009. There was no election then.

And do not let the 18 percent pass too quickly. The law sets a 15 percent cap and then leaves an exception: higher rates are allowed if specified in a preexisting contract. That exception is the entry point for collusion. It looks neutral—freedom of contract, mutual consent. But a woman on a delivery bed, uninsured, about to give birth, and a doctor with a practice, lawyers, and collection experience are not signing the same contract. "Voluntary" is a statement that needs interrogation inside an unequal structure. The exception clause is the legal corridor built for exactly that inequality.

Whether the class action wins, the report does not say. Whether Marshall loses, the report does not say either, only that his challenger holds a slight polling lead. One thing is certain: those three arrest records stay in Zimmerman's file no matter how the lawsuit ends, no matter who wins on Tuesday. The gap has been narrowed a little. It has not been closed.

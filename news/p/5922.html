他在法庭上演的是另一场演出He Was Performing Another Show in Court

性别 文化层 · 直接层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
法庭上他扮演悔悟者，书里写着怎么扮演同性恋
In court he played the penitent; his book explained how to play gay.

布兰德在证人席上说，用名气去"促成"性行为是错的，他"非常自私地滥用"了这件商品。这句话是整场审判里最诚实的一句，也是最没用的一句。承认名气是一件可以被滥用的"商品"，和承认对面那个人有没有同意，是两件事。他把前者说出来，是为了让后者显得已经被回答过了。

他2007年那本书里写得更清楚。七岁的照片配一行字：这是我决定装同性恋来吸引女孩的确切时刻，花了十五年才见效。起诉方把这段读出来，是为了说明第一个指控者为什么会以为他是同性恋、从而同意他进酒店房间。这段文字的作用不止于此——它是一份自白书，写明"装"是他从七岁起就在练习的技术。表达在这里不是沟通，是入口。他先把自己包装成无害的、不构成男性威胁的版本，等门开了再换回原来的自己。这不是魅力，是伪装成魅力的作战方案。

同一本书里还有一句：如果我想要什么东西——一份工作或者一个女人——我会坚定地、决绝地清除路上的一切障碍，直到我占有我欲望的对象。工作，或者一个女人，同一个句式。他把人放进可占有物的列表里，然后写下来，出版，卖钱。这不是失言，是他对自己行为逻辑的描述，而且他有信心这套描述会被当成风趣。他赌对了十几年。

庭审第三周最有信息量的不是任何一句证词，是他的举止。作证时用"打趣""絮叨""卖弄风情"这类词，声音洪亮到法官要开口说"这样很好，很响亮"。交叉询问一开始，长时间的沉默，一个接一个问题之后的长停顿。同一个人，两套表达，随时切换。这套切换能力就是他被指控的那些行为的前提条件——一个能在四小时内把自己从风趣的表演者变成受伤的受害者的人，当然也能在酒店房间里把自己从"同性恋男孩"变成别的什么。

他的妻子出庭，说他是"了不起的父亲""极其体贴的丈夫"，说他们的婚姻"非常坦诚、诚实、清晰"。她也承认，关系初期他出轨，她说过如果他和别人睡觉她就不想要这段关系。这段证词被安排的位置很讲究：一个女人的证言，用来给一个被六个女人指控的男人作人格担保。她说的是她认识的那个他。起诉方要问的是另外六个人遇到的那个他。这两件事可以同时为真，而辩方需要陪审团相信只有前一件是真的。

起诉方在结案陈词里说，六个女人互不相识，除了可能得到一点了结之外，没有任何好处。这句话是整篇报道里唯一在算代价的地方。她们付出的是时间、隐私、被盘问，换回来的东西没法定价。而布兰德付出的，按他自己的说法，是没人愿意"拿十英尺长的杆子碰他"——同时检察官提醒陪审团，他在网上有几百万粉丝，有Rumble频道，在政治会议上露面，2024年初受了洗。

受洗这个细节值得停一下。指控出现之后，他给自己换了一套表达：从浪子变成信徒，从被媒体"拆解"的可怜人变成重新站起来的人。这是第三章那套东西的标准操作——夺取叙事，把自己从施害者的位置挪到受害者的位置，再让新的身份替他说话。法庭上那四小时的沉默和洪亮嗓音，是同一套操作在另一个场地的版本。

六个女人的名字在报道里一个都没有。她们被称作"第一个指控者""六名女性"。这不是记者的疏忽，是这类审判的常规。而布兰德的名字在标题里，在他自己的书里，在他妻子的证词里，在他受洗的日期里。谁有资格被写成一个人，谁只能被写成编号，这件事本身就在演示这本书第四章说的东西——解释权的分配从来不是均匀的。

陪审团下周会听到辩方的结案陈词。无论结果如何，有一件事已经发生了：他亲口承认名气是可以被用来"促成"性行为的商品。剩下的问题只是，这件商品在被使用时，对面那个人算不算数。

On the witness stand, Brand said it was wrong to use fame to "facilitate" sexual activity, that he had "misused that commodity very selfishly." It is the most honest sentence of the trial and the least useful one. Admitting that fame is a commodity one can misuse is not the same as admitting whether the person across from him consented. He says the first thing so the second will look already answered.

His 2007 book says it more clearly. A photo of himself at seven, captioned: this is the exact moment I decided to act gay to attract girls, and it took fifteen years to produce results. The prosecution read it out to explain why the first complainant believed he was gay and effeminate, and let him into her hotel room. The passage does more than that. It is a confession that "acting" was a technique he had been rehearsing since childhood. Expression here is not communication. It is an entry point. He packages himself as harmless, as not a male threat, and once the door opens he switches back. That is not charm. It is an operational plan wearing charm as a costume.

The same book: if I want something, whether it's a job or a woman, I will determinedly, resolutely remove anything in the way until I possess the object of my desire. A job, or a woman, same sentence. He put people on a list of possessable objects, wrote it down, published it, sold it. Not a slip. A description of his own logic, offered with confidence that it would read as wit. For over a decade, it did.

The most informative thing in week three is not any testimony. It is his demeanour. In the witness box he used words like "badinage," "loquacious," "coquettishness," his voice booming loud enough that the judge remarked on it. Then cross-examination began, and there were long pauses, long spells of silence, question after question swallowed by nothing. One man, two registers, switched at will. That switching is the precondition for what he is accused of. Someone who can move in four hours from witty performer to wounded victim can certainly move, inside a hotel room, from gay boy to something else.

His wife testified that he is a "wonderful father," an "amazingly attentive husband," that their marriage is "very frank, honest and clear." She also confirmed he cheated early on, and that she had told him she wasn't interested in a relationship with someone sleeping with other people. The placement of her evidence matters: a woman's word used to vouch for the character of a man accused by six women. She is describing the man she knows. The prosecution is asking about the man six others met. Both can be true at once, and the defence needs the jury to believe only the first.

In her closing, the prosecutor said the six women do not know one another and have nothing to gain, apart from perhaps some closure. That is the only place in the whole report where anyone counts a cost. What they paid was time, privacy, cross-examination. What they get back cannot be priced. What Brand paid, by his own account, is that nobody will touch him with a bargepole, while the prosecutor reminded the jury that he has millions of followers online, a Rumble channel, appearances at political conferences, and a baptism in early 2024.

Pause on the baptism. After the allegations, he changed his expression: from libertine to believer, from a man torn apart by the media to a man who got back up. This is the standard operation of Chapter 3, seize the narrative, relocate yourself from perpetrator to victim, then let the new identity do the talking. The four hours of silence and booming voice in court are the same operation staged in a different venue.

None of the six women is named in the report. They appear as "the first alleged rape victim," "the six women." That is not the reporter's oversight. It is the routine of this kind of trial. Brand's name is in the headline, in his own book, in his wife's testimony, in the date of his baptism. Who gets written as a person and who gets written as a number is itself a demonstration of what Chapter 4 describes: the distribution of interpretive authority was never even.

The jury hears the defence's closing next week. Whatever the verdict, one thing has already happened. He admitted on the record that fame is a commodity that can be used to facilitate sex. The only question left is whether, when the commodity was used, the person on the other side counted.

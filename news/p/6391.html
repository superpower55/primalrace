谁杀的人更少，谁就是受害者Who Killed Fewer, and Who Gets to Be the Victim

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 A.V. Club ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
把美狄亚写成疯子，是为了让伊阿宋的账烂在女人身上。
Making Medea a monster is how Jason's ledger gets buried under a woman's name.

这篇书评里最锋利的一句话是它自己都没当回事的那句：书名里那个"这栋房子的敌人"不是美狄亚，是伊阿宋。海恩斯把美狄亚的罪名留在原地，一个都没洗白，她只是把伊阿宋那份"累积的、不留心的伤害"摊开摆在旁边——于是读者第一次看清了账本的两栏：一栏记着女人杀的那几个人，一栏记着男人一路碾过去的所有人，而后者被荷马以降的书写习惯记成了英雄履历。

这就是元暴力最日常的运作方式：不是替谁撒谎，而是决定谁的行为需要被解释成罪行、谁的行为只需要被叙述成经过。美狄亚杀子，两千年来被反复解剖动机；伊阿宋抛妻，只算剧情转折。同一部神话，解释权的分配从来不是均等的。海恩斯做的不是翻案，是把解释权从传抄者手里往回挪了一寸——她让阿尔基墨得、忒俄法涅、克勒伊特、珀利阿斯的女儿们、赫勒这些名字轮流开口，四十多个视角在《一千艘船》里已经试过一次，这次再试，效果是让"主线"这个词本身失效。

注意书评提到的那个细节：美狄亚直到后半部才真正出场。这不是结构失误，这是判断。一个被讲了三千年的女人，先让所有围绕她的、被略过的、连名字都没留下的女性把话说完，她才出现。出场顺序就是定价权。

也别把这本书读成"女性视角重述"这个已经快被出版业做成标签的品类。标签一旦成型，就变成第三章说的那种认知入口：把作品打包成一种可消费的立场，读者买的是态度，不是判断力。海恩斯这本真正在做的事更枯燥也更硬——她把一个神话拆成互相咬合的碎片，让每个碎片自己承担重量，最后拼出来的悲剧不是"一个女人疯了"，是"一整套叙事选择把谁写成疯子"。

书评作者说海恩斯对美狄亚"比多数人更同情"，但这份宽容是挣来的。挣来的意思是有证据、有代价、有对面那一栏的账。

真正没被写进去的是那些连海恩斯也没能捞出来的人：书评里点了一串名字，每一个都对应着神话里一句带过甚至半句都没有的记载。三千年的转述里沉下去的女人，一个当代小说家能捞回来的，是抽样。

The sharpest line in this review is the one it barely notices: the "friend" this house has none of isn't Medea, it's Jason. Haynes leaves every one of Medea's crimes exactly where it was—she doesn't clean a single one—she just lays Jason's "heedless, cumulative damage" on the table next to them. And for the first time the reader sees both columns of the ledger: a few bodies on the woman's side, a road full of them on the man's, and the second column filed for two thousand years under the heading of a hero's résumé.

This is how meta-violence works on an ordinary day. It doesn't lie for anyone. It decides whose act needs to be explained as a crime and whose act only needs to be narrated as a passage. Medea kills her sons and gets her motives dissected for twenty centuries; Jason abandons his wife and that's a plot turn. Same myth, and the distribution of interpretive authority was never equal. Haynes isn't retrying the case—she's moving the authority back an inch from the copyists. Alcimede, Theophane, Kleith, the daughters of Pelias, Helle: the names take turns speaking, forty-odd perspectives tried once already in A Thousand Ships, and the effect is to make the word "main plot" stop working.

Look at the detail the review mentions: Medea doesn't really appear until the back half. That isn't a structural slip, it's a judgment. A woman who has been talked about for three thousand years waits until every overlooked woman around her—including the ones with no name—has finished speaking. Order of appearance is pricing power.

And don't file this under "feminist retelling," a category the publishing industry has nearly finished turning into a label. A label is a cognitive entry point: it packages a work as a consumable stance, and the reader buys an attitude instead of a judgment. What Haynes is actually doing is drier and harder. She breaks a myth into interlocking fragments, makes each one carry its own weight, and what gets assembled at the end isn't "a woman went mad" but "a narrative tradition chose which woman to write as mad."

The reviewer says Haynes is more sympathetic to Medea than most. That grace was earned—earned means evidence, cost, and the other column of the ledger accounted for.

What stays outside the book is everyone Haynes couldn't pull up either: the review names a string of figures, each corresponding to a line, or half a line, in the source. Three thousand years of retelling, and what one novelist can haul back to the surface is a sample.

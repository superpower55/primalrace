迪士尼酷女孩，还是迪士尼的酷生意Disney Cool Girls, Still Disney's Cool Business

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 The Cut ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
叙事换皮，定价权没换手
The label got a rebrand; the pricing power didn't move.

“Disney adult”这个标签被嫌弃了十年，现在有人出来给它换皮，叫“cool Disney girl”。换的是皮，不是生意。

旧版本是白女穿米奇耳朵、背 Loungefly、买 Lightning Lane 插队，被主流文化挂在“cringe”的柱子上。新版本是宽松牛仔短裤、白袜配芭蕾鞋、复古迪士尼 T 恤，配文“I'm not your average Disney adult”。报道里 35 岁的 Stephanie Arant、25 岁的 Alex Haas、32 岁的 Ashley Wilbraham 都在做这件事。看上去是从“扮演公主”转向“做自己”。但两版消费的是同一个乐园、同一批周边、同一个“我花钱进来所以我是主角”的位置。变的只是被允许的表达风格，不是谁在定价。

关键的一段在中间：迪士尼消费品美洲区创意副总裁 Bobby Kim，早年是街头品牌 the Hundreds 的创始人，靠改造迪士尼图形起家，现在在迪士尼内部生产同款，一条复古线“几秒钟售罄”。他说这些女人的穿法“definitely informing the design”，又说消费者“不再需要品牌替自己说话”，紧接着补一句人们渴望“更纯粹、单一文化”的东西。一个人同时说“你们在创造”、说“我们照着做”、说“我们卖给你”，这不叫对话，这叫收编。亚文化被认知入口吸进去，再作为商品吐出来，价格由吸进去的那一方定。

报道自己给了这套机制的注脚：Estes 说“乐园就是时装秀，奥兰多永远是时装周”。时装周的入场券从来不是免费的。所谓“为自己穿”，落地成了一句“我今天穿什么，是迪士尼日一半的兴奋”——兴奋是她的，账单也是她的，利润是迪士尼的。这和第三章里“松弛感”“老钱风”的路径一模一样：一个本来属于普通人的表达，被品牌密集书写成高级审美，再卖回给想晋升圈层的人。品味没有高下，定价有。

也别忽略“cool”这个词本身在做什么。它把一群被嘲笑的女性消费者从“cringe”里捞出来，代价是她们必须证明自己“不是那种迪士尼成人”——不穿印花 T、不背卡通包、不显得太认真。这是共谋的一种温和形态：用与更弱者的距离换取自己被接纳。被规训的从来是女性消费者的表达，乐园和它的版权纹丝不动。

迪士尼当然好玩，报道说得对，喷了驱虫药的乐园里很难皱着眉。问题不在她们玩得开心，在于这份开心被设计成了一条必须持续付费的表达通道，而通道的闸门握在 Kim 们手里。

真正值得问的是：如果哪天“cool Disney girl”也变成新的 cringe，下一个被允许的风格由谁来宣布？答案已经写在报道里了——不是穿衣服的人。

"Disney adult" was a slur for a decade. Now someone is repackaging it as "cool Disney girl." The packaging changed. The business didn't.

The old version was a white woman in Mickey ears and a Loungefly backpack, holding pricey Lightning Lane passes to skip the line, filed by mainstream culture under "cringe." The new version is baggy jorts, white socks with ballet flats, a vintage Disney tee, captioned "I'm not your average Disney adult." Stephanie Arant, 35, Alex Haas, 25, Ashley Wilbraham, 32 — all doing it in the piece. It reads as a shift from playing princess to being yourself. But both versions consume the same park, the same merch, the same "I paid to get in, so I'm the protagonist" slot. What changed is the permitted style of expression, not who sets the price.

The tell is in the middle: Bobby Kim, VP of creative for the Americas at Disney Consumer Products since 2025, founder of the streetwear brand the Hundreds, who built his name reworking Disney graphics and now produces them in-house — one vintage line sold out in seconds. He says these women's looks are "definitely informing the design," then says consumers no longer want brands speaking for them, then says people long for something "more wholesome, more pure, monoculture." One man saying "you're creating," "we're copying," and "we're selling it back to you" in the same breath isn't a conversation. It's absorption. The subculture enters through the cognitive gate and exits as product, priced by the side that did the absorbing.

The piece supplies its own footnote: Estes says the parks are a fashion show and it's always Fashion Week in Orlando. Fashion Week has never had free admission. "Dressing for myself" lands as "deciding what to wear is half the excitement of a Disney day" — the excitement is hers, the bill is hers, the margin is Disney's. Same route as "relaxed" and "old money" in chapter three: an expression that belonged to ordinary people gets densely written into high taste by brands, then sold back to people climbing a tier. Taste has no hierarchy. Pricing does.

Don't skip what "cool" is doing. It lifts a mocked group of women consumers out of "cringe" on condition that they prove they're not "that kind of Disney adult" — no printed tees, no cartoon bags, nothing too earnest. That's a mild form of complicity: distance from a weaker other, traded for acceptance. The expression being disciplined is always the women's. The park and its IP don't move an inch.

Disney is fun, the piece is right — hard to frown in a park that sprays repellent so there are no bugs. The problem isn't that they're happy. It's that the happiness is designed as a paid channel of expression, and the gate is held by the Kims.

The real question: when "cool Disney girl" becomes the next cringe, who gets to announce the next permitted style? The piece already answered — not the people wearing the clothes.

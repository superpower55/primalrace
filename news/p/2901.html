厨房里的“舒适区”与被抹除的劳作The 'Comfort Zone' Kitchen and the Erasure of Labor

哲学 文化层 · 结构层 The New York Times ↗ 2026-07-27 § 链接
所谓“热爱烹饪”的叙事，往往是剥离了结构性剥削后的审美消费。
The narrative of 'loving to cook' is often an aesthetic consumption that strips away structural exploitation.

看到这种典型的 NYT 风格报道，第一反应是生理性的厌倦。两个明星在镜头前温馨地做一道家庭食谱，谈论“舒适区”的菜单，把厨房定义为社交、友谊和“文化织物”的载体。这是一种极其典型的 cultural violence：通过将烹饪浪漫化、审美化，掩盖这个空间本质上的权力等级和劳作剥削。

注意文中 Ebon 提到的那个“哲学”——不要频繁搅拌，让食材与热量互动。这种所谓的“哲学”只有在拥有绝对掌控权且无需面对生存压力的 home kitchen 里才成立。在真正的 professional kitchen（正如《The Bear》试图呈现但此处被 NYT 过滤掉的）中，这种“随缘”的互动会被定义为低效，而被结构性暴力驱动的 mise en place 强制替代。当明星在谈论“我太累了，只想做我知道的东西”时，这种 burnout 是一个拥有选择权的特权阶级在消费疲劳感；而对于 Ayo 提到的那个在养老院洗碗的初职起点，那种累是生物墙级别的生存损耗。

最讽刺的是，这种叙事将“家庭食谱”包装成某种神圣的传承，却选择性地忽略了谁在历史上真正承担了这些“传承”的繁琐劳作。在这种 masculine-centric 的审美框架下，男性在厨房里扮演的是“传授者”和“探索者”，而女性的劳作则被简化为“甜美的、起泡的”背景板。这不仅是共谋，而是一次完美的 weaponization：用一种温情的、中产阶级的叙事，把一个充满等级压迫的产业变成了一个可以被任意消费的 lifestyle 标签。

Reading this typical NYT piece triggers a visceral boredom. Two stars warmly preparing a family recipe, discussing their 'comfort zones,' and defining the kitchen as a vessel for friendship and 'cultural fabric.' This is a classic form of cultural violence: by romanticizing and aestheticizing cooking, the inherent power hierarchies and labor exploitation of the space are erased.

Note Ebon's mentioned 'philosophy'—leaving ingredients alone to interact with heat. Such 'philosophy' only exists in a home kitchen where one has absolute control and no survival pressure. In a real professional kitchen, this 'letting it be' would be labeled as inefficiency and forcibly replaced by the mise en place driven by structural violence. When a star speaks of being 'too burned out' to try new recipes, this burnout is a privileged class consuming the feeling of fatigue; for Ayo's starting point as a dishwasher in a nursing home, that exhaustion was a biological-wall level of survival depletion.

Most ironic is how 'family recipes' are packaged as sacred heritage, while selectively ignoring who actually bore the tedious labor of that heritage. Within this masculine-centric aesthetic frame, the man acts as the 'teacher' and 'explorer,' while female labor is reduced to a 'sweet, fizzy' backdrop. This is not just complicity, but a perfect weaponization: using a warm, middle-class narrative to turn an industry of systemic oppression into a consumable lifestyle label.

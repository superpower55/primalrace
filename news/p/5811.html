中古家具变贵，品味就换代When Good Design Gets Priced Out, Taste Changes Hands

其他 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
当一件设计贵到只有某些人买得起，它就从品味变成了筛子。
Once a design gets too expensive for most people, taste stops being taste and becomes a filter.

中古家具（mid-century modern）被年轻人嫌弃，原因不是它过时了，是它买不起了。报道里说得很清楚：2019 年拍卖会上 50 英镑能拿下一张边柜，现在低于 300 到 400 英镑算你运气好（来源：The Guardian）。诺福克的古董商 Budds 说，大量英国中古家具被运到欧洲甚至日本，价格被推上去，“变得有点精英主义了，现在只有某些人买得起真正好的东西”。

所以这场“审美疲劳”的叙事要倒过来读。不是设计变丑了，是入场券涨价了。品味从来不是纯粹的喜好，它是定价权的下游产物：谁能定义什么是“高级”，谁就先占住了稀缺。Instagram 和 Pinterest 把一套样板房铺到所有人眼前，Marks & Spencer、Dunelm、H&M、Temu 立刻把仿款做成流水线，于是“人人都有的东西”自动降级为 cringe。这一整套流程跟设计本身毫无关系，它只是在不停地重新划分谁在圈内、谁在圈外。

最值得听的是设计师 Natasha Landers 那句话：“因为更多人能享受它，就判定它不再酷，这里头有点势利。”这句话点破了 cringe 的真正机制。当一件东西的民主性被兑现，它就必须被贬值，否则阶级的筛子就失效了。中古设计最初的哲学恰恰是“让尽可能多的人用合理价格得到好设计”，Keith Stephenson 说它“从来就不该是精英主义的东西”。现在它成了精英主义的东西，然后被骂 cringe。这不是设计的失败，是它最初承诺的失败。

另一条线是“fast furniture”。设计师 Carina Harford 说她被过去五年大量冒出来的仿中古家具“真正惹恼”，劝人别买不需要的新东西。这话是对的，但它同时暴露了一个尴尬：当原版贵到买不起、仿版又被骂廉价，最后能站住脚的其实是二手市场里 Harford 自己那种混搭——维多利亚折叠牌桌 110 英镑、台球厅拆下来的灯具 40 英镑、1940 年代英印风格桌子 30 英镑、四把 1980 年代凳子 90 英镑。便宜、独特、不制造新的垃圾。这才是真.最优解表达：对自己有利，也不向系统多索取一份。

被夹在中间的是 Budds 这样的从业者。44 岁，自称“中古千禧一代”，靠这个吃饭，现在得转型去迎合 Z 世代买干奇古怪东西的口味。她没有做错任何事，她只是站在一条价格曲线和一条品味曲线的交叉点上，而这两条曲线都不是她能控制的。

报道里没有出现的名字，是那些把英国中古家具一船一船运去日本的人。他们赚了差价，然后留在原地的是“只有某些人买得起”这句话。

Young people didn't fall out of love with mid-century modern. They got priced out of it. The numbers are right there: in 2019 you could pick up a sideboard at auction for £50; now you're lucky to pay under £300–£400 (source: The Guardian). Norfolk dealer Laura Budds says British mid-century pieces have been shipped to Europe and as far as Japan, pushing prices up and making the style "a bit too elitist. Only certain people can afford the really good quality stuff now."

So read the "cringe" narrative backwards. The design didn't get ugly; the entry ticket got expensive. Taste has never been a pure preference — it's downstream of pricing power. Whoever gets to define "high-end" grabs the scarcity first. Pinterest and Instagram flatten one showroom into everyone's feed, Marks & Spencer, Dunelm, H&M and Temu clone it into a production line, and suddenly "what everyone has" is automatically demoted to cringe. None of this has anything to do with the furniture. It's a machine for redrawing who's inside and who's out.

The line worth keeping is Natasha Landers': there is "something slightly snobbish about deciding something is no longer cool simply because more people can enjoy it." That's the actual mechanism of cringe. The moment a design's democratic promise gets delivered, it has to be devalued, or the class filter stops working. Mid-century's founding philosophy was exactly that promise — good design at a good price for as many people as possible, "never supposed to be an elitist thing," as Keith Stephenson puts it. Now it is elitist, and it gets called cringe for it. That's not the design failing. That's its original promise failing.

Then there's fast furniture. Carina Harford says the imitation mid-century pieces of the past five years "really irked" her, and tells people to stop buying new things they don't need. Fair. But it exposes the squeeze: when originals are unaffordable and knockoffs are tacky, the only position left standing is the secondhand mix Harford lives in herself — a Victorian fold-out card table for £110, lighting from a shuttered billiards hall for £40, a 1940s Anglo-Indian table for £30, four 1980s stools for £90. Cheap, singular, and it doesn't manufacture new waste. That's a genuinely optimal expression: good for her, and it doesn't extract an extra cut from the system.

Stuck in the middle is Budds — 44, a self-described "mid-century millennial," making a living from the style, now diversifying to cater to gen Z's taste for whimsy and taxidermy. She did nothing wrong. She's just standing where a price curve crosses a taste curve, and she controls neither.

The name missing from this story is whoever is loading British mid-century furniture onto ships bound for Japan. They pocket the margin. What's left behind is the sentence "only certain people can afford it now."

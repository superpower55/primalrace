十亿美元的镜头语言A Billion-Dollar Camera Angle

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 Deadline ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要加州州长纽森在中期选举前一天提议，将加州影视税收抵免项目从每年7.5亿美元提高到10亿美元以上，使其成为美国规模最大的设上限影视税收优惠。方案还包括把抵免范围扩大到主创薪酬、将项目期限从五年延长到十年、对承诺在加州拍摄至少两部作品的公司额外增加5%抵免，以及把独立电影资助提高到每年1亿美元。纽森将于2027年1月离任，报道称该提案在他任内通过的可能性很低。
The newsA day before the midterms, California Governor Gavin Newsom proposed raising the state's film and TV tax credit from $750 million to more than $1 billion a year, which would make it the largest capped program in the US. The plan also expands eligibility to above-the-line pay, extends the sunset from five to ten years, adds a 5% boost for companies committing to at least two productions, and raises independent film funding to $100 million. Newsom leaves office in January 2027, and the report says passage before then is unlikely.
补贴的是制片公司的账本，不是那五万个失业的人。
The subsidy goes to studio ledgers, not to the 50,000 who lost jobs.

先把账算清楚：加州影视税收抵免从每年3.3亿美元涨到7.5亿，用了一年半；现在纽森要在离任前把它推到10亿以上。报道自己都说了，这个跛脚鸭州长在2027年1月走人之前几乎不可能让州议会通过。所以这不是一项政策，这是一段竞选广告——在接任者当选的前一天播出，镜头对准好莱坞。

再看钱花在谁身上。新方案把抵免范围扩大到above-the-line，也就是主创薪酬。原来的规则是补贴制作，现在连明星的片酬也能抵税。这是把公共财政的钱，直接转进本来就拿最高分成的那批人的口袋，然后管它叫“让创意人才留在本州”。人才从来不需要补贴才留得下，需要补贴的是账本。

报道里唯一提到“人”的数字是：过去六年，超过五万个工作岗位直接消失。这五万人是灯光、道具、场务、后期，是片场外围那圈真正靠工时吃饭的人。而方案给出的激励是：承诺在加州拍至少两部戏的公司，多拿5%。这是给已经决定要拍的公司发奖金，不是给失业的场务发工资。结构性暴力就是这样运作的——它不出现施暴者，只出现一份税表，而税表上受益方的名字和受损方的名字从来不在同一页。

纽森说“各州不能独自承担这个成本”，然后呼吁国会通过全国性的影视税收抵免法案。翻译一下：加州先垫钱把制片公司留住，然后让全美国的纳税人一起分摊这笔账。这套逻辑和所有补贴竞赛一样，佐治亚不设上限，纽约在旁边虎视眈眈，于是每个州都加价，最后赢的永远是那个可以拿三个州的报价来压价的公司。州与州之间的博弈，成本由居民承担，收益归股东。

这不是在说税收抵免没有用。它确实把一些制作留在了加州，报道里也列了《Michael》续集、《Last Friday》续集这些拿到抵免的项目。问题是：一个每年十亿美元的公共项目，衡量它成功的标准是什么？是留在州里的制作数量，还是那五万个岗位有没有回来、回来的人拿多少时薪、有没有医保？报道没写。提案里也没有。一个只统计投资额、不统计就业质量的补贴，本质上是一台把公共资金转换成企业利润的机器，而机器不需要对任何人负责。

最值得看的是时机。中期选举前一天，接任者大概率是民调领先的贝塞拉，而贝塞拉和共和党对手希尔顿都各自提过加码影视抵免的方案。也就是说，不管谁赢，这个方向都已经定了。纽森抢在投票前把数字喊到十亿，是在给下一任划定起跑线，也是在给自己的总统野心攒好莱坞的人情。

十亿美元买不到一个更好的加州，只能买到一条更好看的新闻标题。那五万个消失的岗位里，有多少人现在正在给这条标题点赞，报道没问，也不会问。

Do the math first: California's film tax credit went from $330 million to $750 million a year in eighteen months. Now Newsom wants it above $1 billion before he leaves. The report itself says a lame-duck governor almost certainly cannot get Sacramento to pass this before January 2027. So this is not a policy. It is a campaign ad, aired the day before his successor is elected, with the camera pointed at Hollywood.

Then look at who gets the money. The plan expands eligibility to above-the-line expenditures, meaning lead talent salaries become a qualified cost. The state will now subsidize star paychecks, and call it keeping creative talent in California. Talent never needed a subsidy to stay. Ledgers do.

The only number about people in the entire report is this: over 50,000 jobs directly lost in six years. Those are the grips, the gaffers, the set crews, the post-production houses, the ones who live on hours. What the plan offers them is a 5% boost for companies that commit to at least two productions. That is a bonus for firms already planning to shoot, not a wage for the crew who lost work. This is how structural violence operates: no perpetrator appears, only a tax schedule, and the beneficiaries and the casualties never share a page.

Newsom says states cannot shoulder the cost alone, then calls on Congress to pass a national film tax credit. Translation: California fronts the money to keep productions, then asks every American taxpayer to split the bill. It is the same logic as every subsidy race. Georgia is uncapped, New York is looming, so every state bids higher, and the only consistent winner is the company that can play three states against each other. The game is between states. The cost lands on residents. The return goes to shareholders.

This is not an argument that tax credits do nothing. They do keep some production in California, and the report lists the Michael sequel and the Friday sequel among the winners. The question is what counts as success for a billion-dollar public program. The number of productions kept, or whether those 50,000 jobs came back, at what wage, with what health coverage? The report does not say. The proposal does not either. A subsidy that measures investment and never measures employment is a machine for converting public money into corporate profit, and a machine answers to no one.

The timing is the tell. One day before the midterms, with Becerra leading in the polls and both gubernatorial candidates pushing their own credit expansions, the direction is already set no matter who wins. Newsom shouting a billion before the vote is drawing the starting line for his successor, and collecting Hollywood favors for his own presidential ambitions.

A billion dollars will not buy a better California. It will buy a better headline. How many of the 50,000 who lost their jobs are now liking that headline, the report does not ask, and will not.

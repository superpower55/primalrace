用纳税人的钱买一张歧视许可证A Discrimination License, Paid For by Taxpayers

国际 结构层 · 文化层 NPR ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
宗教自由成了拒绝他人的通行证，钱照拿，门照关。
Religious freedom becomes a permit to exclude: take the money, shut the door.

科罗拉多州想给每个四岁孩子一个学前班名额，每个孩子每年六千三百美元，钱来自烟草税。丹佛总教区要的是：这笔钱照拿，但有权把同性恋和跨性别家长的孩子挡在门外。这不是宗教自由，这是拿公共的钱买一张歧视许可证。

案子在最高法院的核心，是宗教表达能不能豁免反歧视法。Rienzi 的说法很直白：不是每个人都要当天主教徒，但必须同意支持天主教教义、不公开反驳它。翻译一下：一个四岁孩子能不能进学前班，取决于他父母在公开场合说过什么。这是用孩子的入学资格给家长的表达定价。

再看共和党这边给出的理由。Laycock 说种族在美国历史上有"独特"的位置——二百五十年奴隶制、内战、七十五万人死亡、三条宪法修正案——别的群体没有这样的历史。这话本身没错，但它被用来干什么？用来论证基于性取向和性别认同的歧视，可以享受比种族歧视更宽的豁免。1983年最高法院以八比一裁定鲍勃·琼斯大学因禁止跨种族约会而失去免税资格；今天同样的逻辑到了性取向这里，就变成了"有待讨论"。历史创伤的深浅，成了歧视许可的分级标准。

最值得拆的是"宗教自由"这个说法本身。它被武器化的方式，是把一个群体的信仰变成对另一个群体准入的否决权。总教区说，项目导致天主教学校入学率下降百分之二十，两所学前班关门，其中一所服务"少数族裔和低收入家庭"。Scott Elmer 又说，LGBTQ 家长来报名"非常罕见"，"这不是我们经常处理的事"。既然罕见，为什么值得打到最高法院？因为要的不是解决一个实际问题，是要一个判例：公共资金进入宗教机构时，反歧视法自动失效。

这里有一层共谋，报道写得很清楚，却容易被当成背景略过。天主教会最初是支持这个学前班项目的，Catholic Charities 至今仍在参与，至少三十五所基督教学校、一所穆斯林学校、三所犹太学校也在参与——它们都接受了"不能歧视"这个前提。反对的是丹佛总教区。同一套信仰体系内部，有人算出来参与更划算，有人算出来豁免更值钱。Tim Uhl 那句"我不同意天要塌下来"，说的就是这个：不是所有天主教学校都觉得反歧视法是要他们的命。

真正付代价的人在这篇报道里几乎不出现。那些孩子——四岁，还没学会说话就被判定为"会破坏课堂"——没有引语，没有名字。报道只给了 Elmer 一句"非常罕见"。罕见不等于没有，罕见恰恰说明他们知道这些人存在，只是数量少到可以当作不存在。而最高法院要判的，正是这些人以后还存不存在于这个项目里。

Rienzi 说"不是每个人都要当天主教徒"，这句话是真的。但后半句是：你必须是那种不会公开反驳天主教教义的人，你的孩子才能坐进那间教室。四岁的孩子不需要同意任何教义，需要同意的是他的父母——同意闭嘴。这不是入学条件，这是表达审查，只是穿了一件宗教的外衣。

俄克拉荷马那所犹太特许学校已经拿到了二百六十万美元州资金，预计到 2030 年会涨到八百三十万。这条线一旦放开，公共教育就不再是公共的，而是一堆按信仰分流的、拿纳税人钱的私立系统。到那时候，"每个孩子都能上学前班"这句话还在，只是有些孩子得先通过家长的政治审查。

Colorado wanted every four-year-old to have a pre-K seat: $6,300 per child per year, funded by a tobacco tax. The Denver Archdiocese wants the money and the right to keep out the children of gay and trans parents. That is not religious freedom. That is a discrimination license bought with public funds.

The case before the Supreme Court is whether religious expression exempts a school from anti-discrimination law. Rienzi puts it plainly: not everyone has to be Catholic, but they do have to agree to support Catholic teachings and not publicly contradict them. Translated: whether a four-year-old gets into preschool depends on what his parents have said in public. A child's admission is priced against his parents' expression.

Then there is the argument Laycock offers. Race has a "unique" place in American history—250 years of slavery, a civil war, 750,000 dead, three constitutional amendments. True enough. But it is being used to argue that discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity deserves a wider exemption than racial discrimination. In 1983 the Court ruled 8-to-1 that Bob Jones University should lose its tax exemption for banning interracial dating. Apply the same logic to sexual orientation today and it becomes an open question. The depth of historical trauma has become a grading scale for discrimination permits.

What deserves the most scrutiny is the phrase "religious freedom" itself. Here it is weaponized by converting one group's beliefs into a veto over another group's access. The archdiocese says enrollment is down 20% across its schools and two preschools have closed, one serving minority and low-income families. Elmer also says LGBTQ parents rarely try to enroll—"not something we deal with regularly." If it is so rare, why take it to the Supreme Court? Because what is wanted is not a fix for a real problem. It is a precedent: when public money enters a religious institution, anti-discrimination law switches off.

The report shows a layer of complicity that is easy to skim past. The Catholic Church initially endorsed the pre-K program. Catholic Charities still participates, along with at least 35 Christian schools, one Muslim school and three Jewish schools—all of which accepted the no-discrimination condition. The opposition comes from the Denver Archdiocese. Within the same faith, some calculated that participating pays better; some calculated that an exemption pays more. Tim Uhl's "I don't agree with the sky is falling" says exactly this: not every Catholic school sees anti-discrimination law as a death sentence.

The people who actually pay barely appear. The children—four years old, judged disruptive before they can speak—have no quotes, no names. The report gives only Elmer's "very rare." Rare is not absent. Rare means they know these people exist and that there are few enough of them to treat as nothing. What the Court decides is whether they exist inside this program at all.

Rienzi says not everyone has to be Catholic. That is true. The second half is: you have to be the kind of person who will not publicly contradict Catholic teachings, or your child does not sit in that classroom. A four-year-old does not need to agree to any doctrine. His parents do—they need to agree to stay quiet. That is not an admissions requirement. It is expression review in religious clothing.

The Jewish charter school in Oklahoma already has $2.6 million in state funding, projected to reach $8.3 million by 2030. Once this line opens, public education stops being public and becomes a set of faith-segregated private systems paid for by taxpayers. "Every kid can go to pre-K" will still be the slogan. Some kids will just have to pass their parents' political screening first.

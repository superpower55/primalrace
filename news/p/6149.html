礼貌是办公室战争的伪装Politeness Is the Camouflage of Office War

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
新闻摘要电子邮件验证公司 ZeroBounce 的一项调查显示，「Just to be clear」以 52% 的得票率被评为最阴阳怪气的邮件用语，紧随其后的是「per my last email」（40%）。作者认为这类说法其实近乎公然挑衅，并分享了自己撰写礼貌表面下暗藏锋芒的邮件的心得。
The newsA survey by email verification company ZeroBounce found "Just to be clear" to be the most passive-aggressive email phrase, chosen by 52%, followed by "per my last email" at 40%. The author argues these verge on outright aggression and shares her own tips for writing emails that seem polite but carry a sting.
被动攻击是弱者在规则内的武器，代价是同事成了敌人
Passive aggression is the weak's weapon inside the rules; the cost is that colleagues become enemies.

一封被动攻击的邮件，是职场里最诚实的表达。它承认了一件事：你不能直接说“你错了”，因为规则不允许；你只能把攻击包进“Apologies for any confusion”里，让收件人自己咽下去。这篇文章把这种写法拆解得很好，甚至有点得意——中指向着屏幕，邮件里却写着“Warm regards”。

但它的落点暴露了问题。作者最后说，真正的敌人是资本主义，为什么要在同事之间打这种仗。这句话是对的，但说得太轻了。被动攻击不是个人修养问题，它是结构暴力在语言层的投影：当组织不给下属直接冲突的合法通道，冲突就只能转入地下，变成一套编码的仪式。谁在这种仪式里最熟练？通常是那些没有正式权力、只能靠措辞和抄送来争夺解释权的人。

文章里有个细节值得停一下：“I like to deploy a cc on this kind of email.”抄送不是沟通，是公开处刑。它把两个人之间的分歧变成一场表演，观众是老板和同事，目的是让对方在众人面前失分。这已经不是在解决问题，是在存在性战争里抢票——谁的表达被系统认定为“对”，谁就赢。

而输的那个人，往往不是逻辑上错了，是没读懂这套编码。被动攻击的门槛在于，它要求你同时掌握礼貌和敌意两种语言，并且知道什么时候切换。这对很多人是不公平的，尤其是那些被规训成“不要冲突”的人——他们收到“Hope this helps!”时，第一反应是道谢。

所以这篇文章教的东西，本质上是一种生存技能，不是沟通技巧。它有用，但它不公正。真正的公正表达，是让冲突可以摆在桌面上，而不是逼人把它磨成一句带问号的“My understanding was…”。作者自己也知道，所以结尾才说这是“hollow pleasure”。

可惜她停在了深呼吸和按退格键。退格键删不掉那个让你必须写这封邮件的结构。

A passive-aggressive email is the most honest expression in the workplace. It admits one thing: you cannot say "you're wrong" directly, because the rules forbid it; so you wrap the attack in "Apologies for any confusion" and let the recipient swallow it. This piece dissects that craft well, almost smugly — middle fingers at the screen, "Warm regards" in the body.

But its landing point gives the game away. The author ends by saying the real enemy is capitalism, so why fight a fellow worker. That's true, but it's said too lightly. Passive aggression isn't a matter of personal manners; it's structural violence projected onto language. When an organization gives people no legitimate channel for direct conflict, conflict goes underground and becomes a coded ritual. Who is most fluent in that ritual? Usually those with no formal power, who can only fight for the right to define through wording and cc lines.

One detail deserves a pause: "I like to deploy a cc on this kind of email." A cc is not communication; it's a public execution. It turns a disagreement between two people into a performance, with the boss and colleagues as audience, the goal being to make the other person lose face. This is no longer problem-solving; it's grabbing votes in an existential war — whoever's expression the system certifies as "correct" wins.

And the loser is often not wrong on logic, but illiterate in the code. The barrier of passive aggression is that it demands fluency in two languages at once, politeness and hostility, and the knowledge of when to switch. That's unfair to many people, especially those trained never to conflict — they read "Hope this helps!" and their first instinct is to say thank you.

So what this article teaches is a survival skill, not a communication technique. It works, but it isn't just. Just expression would put the conflict on the table instead of forcing people to grind it into a question-marked "My understanding was…". The author knows this, which is why she calls it a "hollow pleasure".

A pity she stops at deep breaths and the backspace key. Backspace cannot delete the structure that made you write the email in the first place.

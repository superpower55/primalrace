没人被踢走，只是位置一直空着Nobody Got Kicked Off, the Seat Was Just Empty

文娱 文化层 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要11月1日，Taylor Swift与导演Andrew Stanton在《玩具总动员5》特别放映后的问答环节中谈及片尾曲《I Knew It, I Knew You》。Stanton称因Swift巡演档期未定，他没有另找其他音乐人，宁可让影片没有原创片尾曲；Swift则说她当时反复确认自己有没有顶掉别人。该曲已登上Billboard Hot 100榜首，并获得奥斯卡最佳原创歌曲的提名呼声。
The newsAt a Nov. 1 Q&A after a special screening of Toy Story 5, Taylor Swift and director Andrew Stanton discussed her end-credits song “I Knew It, I Knew You.” Stanton said he never approached another artist while waiting on Swift, preferring no original end-credits track at all. The song debuted at No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100 and is drawing Oscar buzz.
「没顶掉别人」的前提，是那个位置从没给别人留过。
“I didn’t push anyone out” only works if the seat was never offered to anyone else.

这条新闻里最值得看的不是一首歌，是一句被当成美德来展示的话：我没有把谁踢走。

Stanton 的说法很清楚：Swift 因为 Eras Tour 档期没谈下来的时候，他没有去找别的音乐人，宁可让《玩具总动员5》没有原创片尾曲。也就是说，从头到尾没有一个「别人」存在过。Randy Newman 从 1995 年写到现在，这次被换掉了，而换掉他的决定在 Swift 还没答应之前就已经做完了。所以「我没顶掉谁」这句话成立的方式，是那个位置先被清空、再被留白、最后被填上——顶掉的动作发生在更早，只是没让 Swift 看见。

这不是在说 Swift 虚伪。她反复问「你们是不是换人了」，说明她至少知道这套流程通常怎么运转。真正被包装起来的是 Stanton 那句「没有别的选择」，一个导演对一个全球顶级流行明星的排他性偏好，被叙述成了忠诚和品味。这是第三章那个入口：把商业决策讲成审美判断，再把审美判断讲成道德品质。粉丝听到的是「他只要她」，实际发生的是「他不要别人」。

Newman 在这条新闻里出现了两次：一次是被替换的背景，一次是在首映式上和 Swift 同台演出。他写了这个系列三十年的音乐，这次的位置是「被致敬的前辈」，站在台上，被提起，被比较——Stanton 说上一次听到一首凭空出现又完美的歌，还是 Newman 弹《You've Got a Friend in Me》。这句话是赞美，同时也是把 Newman 降格成一把尺子：他最好的作品，现在用来量另一个人。

至于 Swift 讲的那段关于 Abigail 的故事，Napoleon Dynamite 的口音、卷发、丈夫们得忍受——这是她的表达，真诚，也是产品的一部分。一首几小时写完的歌，配上「我 14 岁在纳什维尔遇见的那个人」的叙事，情感入口和商业入口在这里是同一个入口。歌登上 Hot 100 榜首，奥斯卡造势从 8 月的 Grammys Icon Session 排到 10 月的 Academy Museum Gala，每一步都算好了。

所以这条新闻里没有受害者，只有一个没被写进对话的人：那个写了三十年、在决定做出时不在场、最后被请上台鼓掌的人。差额就在这里——一个本可以体面交接的位置，被讲成了一个从没打算给别人留的故事。

The thing worth reading in this story isn’t a song. It’s a line presented as a virtue: I didn’t kick anybody off.

Stanton’s account is clear. While Swift was unavailable because of the Eras Tour, he didn’t approach another artist. He decided there would simply be no original end-credits track. Which means no “somebody” ever existed. Randy Newman has written the music for this franchise since 1995, and he was replaced — but the decision to replace him was made before Swift ever said yes. “I didn’t kick anybody off” is true only because the seat was cleared, left empty, and then filled. The kicking happened earlier, out of Swift’s sight.

This isn’t an accusation of hypocrisy. She kept asking whether they had replaced her, which means she knows how this usually works. What’s being packaged is Stanton’s line that nothing else made sense — a director’s exclusive preference for the biggest pop star alive, narrated as loyalty and taste. That’s the Chapter 3 entry point: a business decision told as an aesthetic judgment, then told as a moral quality. Fans hear “he only wanted her.” What happened is “he didn’t want anyone else.”

Newman appears twice here: as the background to his own replacement, and on stage with Swift at the premiere. He wrote this series’ music for three decades, and his role now is beloved predecessor — mentioned, compared, honored. Stanton says the only other time a song dropped from thin air and was perfect was Newman playing “You’ve Got a Friend in Me” on piano. It’s a compliment that also turns him into a measuring stick: his best work now measures someone else.

And Swift’s story about Abigail — the Napoleon Dynamite voices, the curly hair, the husbands who have to put up with it — is her expression, sincere and also part of the product. A song written in hours, wrapped in the girl she met in Nashville at fourteen. The emotional entry and the commercial entry are the same door. The song debuted at No. 1; the Oscar campaign runs from the August Grammys Icon Session to the October Academy Museum Gala. Every step accounted for.

So there’s no victim in this story. There’s just one person absent from the conversation: the man who wrote the music for thirty years, who wasn’t in the room when the call was made, and who was brought on stage to applaud. That’s the gap — a handover that could have been graceful, retold as a story that was never going to include anyone else.

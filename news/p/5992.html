GDP涨了，房子没了GDP Grew. Her Home Didn't.

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Vanity Fair ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
宏观数字再漂亮，也抵不过一个老太太被赶出家门。
Macro numbers don't house an 87-year-old evicted from the only flat she knew.

Maricarmen Abascal 87岁，半残，在马德里的租屋里住了一辈子，9月23日被赶出去，10月7日死在医院。同一天，Pedro Sánchez 在联合国大会上谈技术寡头、谈加沙、谈国际组织改革。西班牙没人听那场演讲——因为一个老太太的死把“欧洲增长最快的经济体”这句话当场拆穿了。

这就是 Violence = Potential − Actual 最直白的样子。GDP、外资增长34%、失业率历史新低，全是潜在值；一个87岁老人的居住权、尊严、命，是实际值。两者之间的差额，不是“经济周期”，是暴力。报道说得很清楚：房价八年涨了30%到40%，热门地区70%，超过2008年房地产泡沫；2025年一年驱逐超过2.5万起；马德里平均租金已经高过最低工资。这不是市场波动，这是结构层在按设计分配损失——有房的人资产升值，租房的人被清出去，而政府通过的反驱逐立法在碎片化的议会里被一条条否掉。

Sánchez 的困境被这篇报道写成了一个悲情故事：最后一位社会民主主义者，被通货膨胀、住房、移民和司法缠讼围困，11月29日大选，非官方民调只给他5%到10%的胜率。但“最后一位社会民主主义者”这个头衔要看他任内实际做了什么。他把2030议程提到部长级，设了欧盟第一个AI治理机构，在联合国骂特朗普、骂以色列，这些都是真的，也都值得记一笔。可这些表达全部发生在国际舞台，发生在认知入口，发生在别人听得见、自己选民摸不着的地方。国内呢？立法本会期通过不到40部法律，上一届超过100部。这不是“运气不好”，这是把政治资本投在了全球发言权上，把本国租户留在了议会被反对党把持的13个自治区和市议会手里。

报道里最诚实的一句来自政治学家 Lluís Orriols：只有经济撑着，才没让“一个支离破碎的政府”显形。换句话说，社会民主主义的最后堡垒，靠的是一组宏观数字替它挡着，而数字底下的具体人正在被清空。

再看那条“爱的力量”。Sánchez 的妻子 Begoña Gómez 因腐败指控候审，检方以证据不足反对起诉，她否认指控；他弟弟 David Sánchez 因行政不当被 Badajoz 法院判九年褫夺公职，正在上诉，他也否认。Sánchez 说他们是“无辜的”，是“骚扰和霸凌行动”的受害者，然后在2024年威胁辞职时说“深爱着”妻子，竞选时穿“La Fuerza del Amor”T恤，部长说“我们是爱的党”。把家人的司法案件转译成浪漫爱叙事，这是第三章那个入口最标准的用法：情感入口一开，事实的追问就被挡在门外。至于他昔日最坚定的支持者 José Luis Ábalos 因腐败被判24年，前首相 Zapatero 因涉嫌腐败被起诉、家中搜出沙特王室所赠价值超百万欧元的珠宝（他否认不当行为）——这些不是“司法迫害”四个字能打包的。Sánchez 当年靠不信任动议上台，动议里骂的就是腐败。

报道把这场选举放进欧洲自由民主制的危机里：极右将成为西班牙第三大政治力量，法国、意大利随后，乌克兰还差700亿欧元，美国自去年一月起对乌防务一分钱没出。这些都对。但对 Abascal 来说，欧洲的地缘政治版图和她被赶出住了八十多年的房子之间，没有任何关系。

真正该问的不是 Sánchez 还能不能活下来——他这辈子最擅长的就是压线翻盘——而是：那2.5万起驱逐里，有多少人会在下一届政府上台前死掉，而下一届政府无论左右，都还没通过任何一条能让他们留下来的法律。

Maricarmen Abascal was 87, partially disabled, and had lived her whole life in a rented flat in Madrid. On September 23 she was evicted. She died on October 7. The same day, Pedro Sánchez addressed the UN General Assembly about reforming international organizations, criticizing the US and Israel, and reining in the "tech oligarchs." Nobody in Spain paid attention. An old woman's death had already dismantled the phrase "Europe's fastest-growing economy."

This is Violence = Potential − Actual in its plainest form. GDP, 34% growth in foreign investment, record-low unemployment—all potential. One 87-year-old's home, dignity, and life—actual. The gap between them is not a "cycle." It is violence. The reporting is explicit: housing prices up 30–40% nationwide over eight years of progressive government, 70% in high-demand areas, exceeding even the 2008 bubble; more than 25,000 evictions in 2025 alone; Madrid's average rent now above the minimum wage. This is the structural layer allocating loss by design—owners' assets appreciate, renters get cleared out, and the anti-eviction legislation the government tried to pass was voted down, measure by measure, in a fragmented parliament.

The article frames Sánchez as a tragedy: the last social democrat, besieged by inflation, housing, migration, and judicial proceedings, facing a November 29 election where even optimistic non-governmental polls give him a 5–10% chance. But "last social democrat" has to be measured against what he did in office. He elevated the 2030 Agenda to ministerial status, created the EU's first AI governance agency, denounced Trump and Israel at the UN. All real, all worth noting. All of it expression aimed at the cognitive entry point—at audiences abroad—while his own voters couldn't touch it. Domestically: fewer than 40 laws passed this session, down from over 100 last time. That isn't bad luck. That's political capital spent on global standing while renters were left to the 13 of 19 regions and the city councils run by the opposition.

The most honest line comes from political scientist Lluís Orriols: only the economy has kept "a government in shambles" from showing. In other words, the last bastion of social democracy is being propped up by macro numbers while the actual people underneath them are being cleared out.

Then there's "the power of love." Sánchez's wife, Begoña Gómez, awaits trial on corruption charges the prosecutor's office has opposed for insufficient evidence; she denies the allegations. His brother, David Sánchez, is appealing a nine-year disqualification for administrative misconduct; he denies the allegations. Sánchez calls them "innocent" victims of a "harassment and bullying operation," declared himself "deeply in love" when he threatened to resign in 2024, wears a "La Fuerza del Amor" T-shirt on the campaign trail, and a minister declares "we are the party of love." Turning a family's legal cases into a romance narrative is the emotional entry point doing exactly what it's built for: once it opens, the factual questions stay outside. His former closest ally José Luis Ábalos got 24 years for corruption. Former prime minister José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero—Sánchez's role model—was indicted in spring 2026, with over a million euros in jewelry from the Saudi monarchy found in searches; he denies wrongdoing. "Judicial persecution" doesn't package that. Sánchez came to power on a no-confidence motion against corruption.

The article places this election inside the crisis of European liberal democracy: the far right poised to become Spain's third-largest force, France and Italy next, Ukraine short 70 billion euros, the US contributing nothing to its defense since January of last year. All true. None of it has anything to do with Abascal being removed from the flat she'd lived in for over eighty years.

The real question isn't whether Sánchez survives—surviving at the last minute is the one thing he has always done. It's how many of those 25,000 evictions will end in a death before the next government takes office, and how neither the next government nor this one has passed a single law that would have let them stay.

清醒是一种可以被讲述的人设Sobriety Was a Persona She Could Narrate

其他 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要美国作家 Meghan O'Gieblyn 在戒酒十一年后复饮，自述如何在赫尔辛基圣诞市集、纳什维尔机场和酒店房间里独自饮酒，并用泡澡、QR 码点单等方式对丈夫和友人隐瞒九个月。她在一次通话中向一位刚出戒断机构的十八岁少年讲述清醒十一年的经验，而当时她本人正在复饮。
The newsAmerican writer Meghan O'Gieblyn describes relapsing into drinking after eleven years of sobriety, hiding nine months of solitary binges in airports and hotel rooms from her husband and friends through baths, QR-code ordering and careful timing. During that period she told an eighteen-year-old just out of rehab that she had been sober for eleven years.
她把戒酒讲成了人设，人设一立，复饮就变成了私事。
She turned sobriety into a persona, and a persona turns a relapse into a private matter.

这篇自述最锋利的地方不是复饮，而是她复饮之后照样能对着一个刚出戒断机构的十八岁少年，平静地说出「十一年」。叙事没有崩，崩的是叙事和身体之间的那段距离。

她花了一整节写自己怎么绕过「说出口」这件事：不能坐吧台只能坐桌子，不能开口点单，要等熟食柜、自助龙头、二维码出现。QR 码是命运递给她的漏洞——不用把罪念出声，手指点一下就行。这是表达的武器化反过来用在自己身上：不是别人往她脑子里灌叙事，是她自己给自己造了一个认知入口，入口的钥匙是「只要不说出来，就还没有发生」。

她把「清醒」当成一个 narrative construct，这个说法本身没错，错的是她从中推出的结论。冥想课讲「自我是虚构的」，AI 论坛上讲「意识是计算」，两套话术在同一个脑子里撞出来的不是解脱，是一张免罪券：既然自我是故事，故事就可以随便改；既然念头不是真的，念头就伤不到人。这是把解构当成许可证。任何一套讲「你不必执着于身份」的话，落到一个正在找借口的人手里，都会变成「那我的身份就可以随便编」。

真正被这套叙事吃掉的不是她自己，是那个十八岁的孩子。他打来电话，声音里有真正的绝望，说自己想象不出没有毒品和酒精的生活。她坐在酒店房间地板上，看着冻雨，把「成瘾要求一切，没有哪种牺牲算够」讲得真诚而准确——她当时确实相信每一个字，同时也确实在相信自己有权选择那件「唯一发光的东西」。两句话同时为真，这才是共谋的日常形态：不是撒谎，是分区。把 counselling 放在一个区，把酒店房间放在另一个区，中间用「这是我的事」隔开。

Metoo 式的追问在这里同样适用：谁在为这套分区付代价？报道里写得清楚——丈夫到机场接她，什么都没察觉；朋友把备用钥匙交给她，把孩子教母的位置留给她。她享受的是「可以被信赖」这份社会资本，而这份资本是靠一个持续十一年的故事维持的。她不是从故事里逃出来，她是靠故事继续领工资。

最刺的一笔在她自己给出的比喻里：寄居蟹的壳留在岸上，作为存在的可靠替身，里面的动物滑进海里。壳就是那个「戒酒十一年、可以当教母、可以给刚出院的少年指路」的人设。问题是，壳留在岸上是给别人看的，别人对着壳说话、托付、信赖——而壳不会回话。

那孩子问她戒了多久的时候，她心里想的是「说出这个数字多难啊」。难的不是数字，是那个数字已经不属于她了，而她还是把它递了出去。整篇文章没有写那孩子后来怎样。这个缺席就是这篇自述最诚实的地方：她连自己给出的那句「十一年」造成了什么，都无从知道。

The sharpest thing in this essay is not the relapse. It is that she could sit on a hotel room floor, look out at freezing rain, and tell an eighteen-year-old fresh out of rehab that she had eleven years — and mean it, and be lying.

She spends a whole section on how she engineered her way around saying the words aloud: not a barstool but a table, not ordering but pointing, waiting for deli cases, self-serve taps, QR codes. The QR code is the loophole fate supplies — you order without speaking your sin. This is the weaponisation of expression turned inward: she built her own cognitive entry point, and the key was “if I never say it, it hasn’t happened.”

Calling sobriety a narrative construct is not wrong. What is wrong is the conclusion she draws from it. The meditation lectures say the self is illusory; the AI panels say consciousness is computation; colliding in one skull, they produce not liberation but an indulgence: if the self is a story, the story can be rewritten; if thoughts are not real, thoughts cannot hurt anyone. That is deconstruction used as a licence. Any teaching that says “you need not cling to identity,” dropped into a person hunting for an excuse, becomes “then my identity is whatever I say it is.”

What the narrative actually consumes is not her. It is the kid. He calls with real desperation, says he cannot picture a life without drugs and alcohol. She tells him, sincerely and accurately, that addiction demands everything and no sacrifice is ever enough — and at the same moment believes she has the right to choose the one luminous thing. Both are true at once. That is what complicity looks like day to day: not lying, but partitioning. Counselling in one room, the hotel room in another, and “this is my business” as the wall between them.

The question applies here as everywhere: who pays for the partition? The essay is clear. Her husband picks her up at the airport and suspects nothing. Friends leave her a spare set of keys, name her godmother to their child. She is drawing on the social capital of being trustworthy, and that capital is maintained by an eleven-year story. She did not escape the story. She is still on its payroll.

Her own image is the sharpest cut: a marine crustacean whose shell stays on shore, a solid proxy for its being, while the animal slithers off into the sea. The shell is the person who has eleven years, can be a godmother, can advise a kid just out of rehab. The trouble is that the shell stays on shore for other people to talk to, to trust, to hand their keys to — and a shell does not answer.

When the boy asks how long she has been sober, what she remembers is how hard it was to say the number. The hard part is not the number. The hard part is that the number no longer belonged to her, and she handed it over anyway. The essay never says what became of the boy. That absence is the most honest thing in it: she does not know what her “eleven years” did, and she has no way of finding out.

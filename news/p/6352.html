换一把尺子，不算胜利A New Ruler Is Not a Win

其他 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 Truthout ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要加州州长纽森签署SB 868，允许居民和租户在阳台安装即插即用太阳能板，最高发电1200瓦。报道称，加州屋顶太阳能装机量2023年下降80%，此前州监管机构削弱了净计量等激励政策。
The newsCalifornia Governor Gavin Newsom signed SB 868, allowing homeowners and renters to plug lightweight solar panels into regular outlets for up to 1,200 watts. Rooftop solar installations in California fell 80% by 2023 after regulators weakened incentives such as net metering.
屋顶太阳能被掐死之后，阳台板成了安慰奖
After rooftop solar was gutted, balcony panels are the consolation prize.

SB 868 不是胜利，是让步。而且是一份被包装成胜利的让步。

加州的屋顶太阳能本来是可以铺满全州的，2000 年代中期开始，州政府一轮一轮加码补贴，Frank 说那阵子走在路上都有人来劝你装屋顶太阳能。后来它塌了：2023 年安装量掉了 80%。不是居民不想要，是监管机构把 NEM 这套让太阳能划算的规则拆了。Del Chiaro 的原话是，州长和他任命的监管者用一条规定在 2022 年把市场砸碎了。

砸碎它的人是 PG&E。这家公司的生意不是卖电，是建输电线路——Del Chiaro 说它本质上是一家建筑公司，所以它必须把电厂建在离人很远的地方，让人依赖它的电网。屋顶太阳能一多，它的利润就少。于是它请了公关公司，编了一个故事：屋顶太阳能对低收入的有色人种不公平，电价上涨都怪它。故事讲完，规则改掉，市场死掉。

这就是共谋的标准动作。不是有人拿着枪逼你拆掉屋顶的板子，是有人买下了解释权，把“谁的账更贵”重新定义成“谁更公平”，然后由州长任命的监管者签字执行。文化层的叙事、结构层的规则、元层的解释权，三层一起动，动完还说这是为了穷人好。

然后 SB 868 来了。1200 瓦，够带一台空调；多发的电不给补偿；价格 500 到 1500 美元，还得等补贴谈成。它被叫作“即插即用太阳能法案”，环境主义者欢呼，说这是迈向气候目标的一大步。可它取代不了屋顶太阳能，报道自己说了：它只该是补充，不该是替代。加州本来可以既有便宜的清洁电，又有完整的莫哈韦沙漠；现在它有了一个从太空看得见的太阳能农场，沙漠固碳的能力被毁掉，居民电价还在涨。

换尺子不换能力。做不了屋顶，就宣布阳台是新战场；拆了 NEM，就把一块能插墙的板子叫作胜利。这套机制我太熟了，跟 τ law 一个路子：现实不配合，就重新定义什么叫配合。

阳台太阳能本身没有问题。它便宜、便携、分散，极端天气打不垮整座城市的电网，欧洲人早就在用，犹他州 2025 年先走了一步，还有一半的州在考虑类似的法案。Del Chiaro 说价格会降下来，这话我信。

但这场胜利是谁赢来的？不是居民，不是环保组织，是 PG&E 先赢了一局，然后允许你赢回一小块。差额还在：屋顶上本可以有的那些板子，莫哈韦被挖掉的那片沙漠，以及那条把两者串起来的规则——它现在还在 PG&E 手里。

差额被缩小的地方，是那 1200 瓦。它真实存在，能省下真金白银，不该被嘲笑。只是别忘了，这份“胜利”的定价权，从头到尾不在插板子的人手上。

SB 868 is not a victory. It is a compromise, and it has been dressed up as a victory.

Rooftop solar in California could have covered the state. From the mid-2000s, Sacramento kept sweetening the incentives; Frank recalls a time when someone on any street would try to sell you a rooftop system. Then it collapsed: installations fell 80 percent by 2023. Not because residents stopped wanting it, but because regulators dismantled the net metering rules that made it pay. Del Chiaro puts it plainly: the governor and the regulators he appointed crushed the market in 2022 with a single regulation.

The one doing the crushing was PG&E. Its business is not selling electricity but building transmission lines — Del Chiaro calls it essentially a construction company, which is why it needs power plants far from where people live and work, so that everyone depends on its grid. More rooftop solar means less of that. So it hired a PR firm and cooked up a story: rooftop solar is unfair to low-income people of color, and it is why rates are rising. Story told, rules rewritten, market dead.

This is how collusion works. Nobody holds a gun to your roof. Someone buys the explaining rights, redefines whose bill is unfair as who is being fair, and the governor's appointees sign off. Cultural narrative, structural rule, meta-level control of interpretation — all three move at once, and the result is announced as a favor to the poor.

Then comes SB 868. Twelve hundred watts, enough for one air conditioner. No compensation for surplus power. Five hundred to fifteen hundred dollars, with subsidies for low-income buyers still under discussion. It is called the Plug and Play Solar Act, environmentalists cheer, and headlines call it a major step. But it cannot replace rooftop solar; the reporting itself says it should have been a supplement, not a substitute. California could have had cheap clean power and an intact Mojave. Instead it has a solar farm visible from space, a desert losing its capacity to sequester carbon, and rising bills.

Change the ruler, not the capacity. If you cannot do rooftops, declare balconies the new frontier. If you gut net metering, call a panel you plug into a wall outlet a victory. I know this mechanism. It runs exactly like the τ law: when reality will not cooperate, redefine what cooperation means.

Balcony solar itself is fine. Cheap, portable, decentralized, resilient when a storm takes out a transmission line. Europe has used it for years; Utah legalized it in 2025, and more than half the states are considering similar bills. Del Chiaro expects prices to fall, and I believe her.

But who won this victory? Not residents, not environmental groups. PG&E won first, then allowed a small win back. The gap remains: the panels that could have been on roofs, the desert that was dug up, and the rule connecting the two — still in PG&E's hands.

Where the gap did shrink is those 1,200 watts. They are real, they save real money, and they should not be mocked. Just remember who set the price of this victory. It was never the people plugging in the panels.

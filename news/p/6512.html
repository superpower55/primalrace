涓滴经济学卖不动了Trickle-Down Has Lost Its Price Tag

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The New Republic ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
选民不是转向左翼，是发现自己的命从来没和董事会绑在一起。
Voters didn't turn left. They noticed their fate was never tied to the boardroom.

“对生意好的就是对美国好的”——这句话卖不动了。这不是民主党赢了辩论，是里根那套叙事的定价权到期了。

把政府说成通胀的元凶，把公共开支说成创新的敌人，把减税说成让所有人受益的引擎——这是典型的第三章操作：从认知入口投放一个关于“什么是经济常识”的版本，然后让它在四十年里变成不需要论证的背景音。里根从没管它叫 trickle-down，但名字不重要，重要的是这套叙事成功地把“谁受益”这个问题从公共讨论里删掉了。

现在删不掉，是因为账算不平了。企业利润创纪录，亿万富翁往月球发火箭，而普通人拿着工资单喂不饱家人。Amy Richards 那句话说得比任何民调都准：他根本不知道拿工资、养家是什么滋味，而且他不在乎。“不在乎”三个字才是关键——不是信息不对称，是利益不对称。

有意思的是 James Talarico 那句“唯一在毁掉美国的少数群体是亿万富翁”。他把共和党惯用的少数群体点名机制原样搬过来，只是换了对象。这是把武器反过来用：同样一个“谁在占你的便宜”的认知入口，指向了真正拿走那部分的人。这招有效，是因为它不要求选民相信一套新理论，只要求他们相信自己的账单。

但账单能推翻一个叙事，不等于能建立另一个。报道里最值得盯的不是民调数字，是那个条件句：民主党得真的兑现。Medicare for All 在密歇根是竞选承诺，三分之二的成年人说支持全民医保——可奥巴马当年连 public option 都从 ACA 里删掉了，十多年过去，保费还是付不起。立法承诺和落地之间的差额，就是 structural violence 的常驻位置。通过了法案但没有执行机制和数据反馈，是暴力的 PR 版本，不是暴力的减少。

还有一个数字值得停一下：30 到 44 岁的选民里，只有 30% 支持普惠托育——而这是眼下最可能在付托育钱的一群人。Z 世代近六成支持政府补贴双职工家庭托育，可到了真正掏钱的那批人，支持率反而掉下来。这说明“政府该提供”这件事还没变成共识，只是变成了一个还没被算清楚的愿望。托育成本的承担者是谁，报道没写；但母职惩罚从来不问选民意见。

所以这不是一个新时代的开场。这是旧叙事的信用破产，而新叙事还停在竞选口号阶段。真正的问题不是选民信不信政府，是当选者愿不愿意让政府真的动那 1%。Talarico 说我们盯错了那 1%——说得对。但盯对了之后呢？报道没写，因为还没发生。

"What's good for business is good for America" is no longer selling. This isn't the Democrats winning an argument; it's the Reagan narrative's pricing power expiring.

Blame government for inflation, call public spending the enemy of innovation, sell tax cuts as an engine that lifts everyone—that's a textbook Chapter 3 move: drop a version of "what counts as economic common sense" into the cognitive entrance, then let it run as background noise for forty years. Reagan never called it trickle-down, but the name doesn't matter. What matters is that the narrative successfully deleted the question "who benefits" from public discussion.

It can't be deleted now because the math no longer balances. Record corporate profits, billionaires launching rockets at the moon, and ordinary people who can't feed their families on a paycheck. Amy Richards put it more precisely than any poll: he has zero idea what it's like to receive a paycheck and feed a family—and he doesn't care. "Doesn't care" is the operative phrase. Not information asymmetry. Interest asymmetry.

What's interesting is James Talarico's line: the only minority destroying America is the billionaires. He took the GOP's habit of naming minority groups and pointed it at the people actually taking the cut. That's a weapon turned around: the same cognitive entrance—"who is ripping you off"—now aimed at the right target. It works because it doesn't ask voters to believe a new theory. It asks them to believe their own bills.

But a bill can topple a narrative without building another. The line worth watching in this piece isn't a poll number; it's the conditional: Democrats have to actually deliver. Medicare for All is a campaign promise in Michigan, two-thirds of adults say they support a national health plan—yet Obama dropped even a public option from the ACA, and more than a decade later premiums are still unaffordable. The gap between a legislative promise and implementation is where structural violence lives permanently. Passing a bill without enforcement mechanisms and data feedback is the PR version of violence, not its reduction.

One more number deserves a pause: only 30 percent of voters aged 30 to 44 support universal childcare—the group most likely to be paying for it right now. Nearly 60 percent of Gen Z back government subsidies for two-working-parent families, but among the people actually writing the checks, support drops. "Government should provide" hasn't become consensus; it's become an aspiration nobody has costed out. Who bears the childcare burden goes unmentioned in the report—but the motherhood penalty never asks voters for their opinion.

So this isn't the opening of a new era. It's the credit failure of an old narrative while the new one is still stuck at the campaign-slogan stage. The real question isn't whether voters trust government. It's whether the people they elect are willing to make government actually touch that 1 percent. Talarico says we're focused on the wrong one percent—he's right. But after focusing on the right one? The report doesn't say, because it hasn't happened yet.

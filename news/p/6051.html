战争是内塔尼亚胡的选举工具War Is Netanyahu's Election Tool

国际 直接层 · 结构层 · 文化层 Truthout ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
战争叙事从来不是关于安全，而是关于谁的解释权不下台。
War narrative is never about security. It's about whose interpretation stays in power.

内塔尼亚胡需要这场战争，比美国需要这场战争更迫切。报道写得很清楚：以色列的组阁进程陷入僵局，他必须在反对派能向公众证明"我们比内塔尼亚胡更会打伊朗"之前动手。战争在这里不是外交失败的结果，是选举的工具——用一场外部冲突让"换人"这个选项变得昂贵。

特朗普的角色是另一回事。报道说他"似乎不确定如何推进"，同时又"不愿做出结束这场他无缘无故发动的战争的必要让步"。二月开打时说要几天或几周结束，现在十一月了，同样的动作要再来一遍。报道问得好：除了发疯，他有什么理由认为同样的行动这次会产生不同结果？这不是战略，这是把"我制造的问题"继续当成"我正在解决的问题"来表演。

麦加条约三国——沙特、巴基斯坦、土耳其——把军队和情报系统的头号人物派到德黑兰，这个动作本身值得看仔细。报道说，没有证据显示美国参与了这次会面的筹划，迹象恰恰相反。也就是说，这不是替美国传话，是海湾国家在绕开美国，自己搭一个区域安全对话的框架。他们的恐惧是具体的：伊朗已经明确表示要打击数据中心、AI枢纽和海底光缆，加上石油设施。报道说，这个破坏量级" dwarf anything we've seen until now"——超过我们迄今见过的一切。

但恐惧没有转化成筹码。报道自己承认：沙特除了向特朗普登记保留意见之外几乎没有选项，土耳其和巴基斯坦对特朗普的决定更没有杠杆。万斯的信被德黑兰看穿——没有立即缓解任何压力，却要求谈判包含核问题，等于用更客气的语言递上同一份不可接受的条款。而万斯此前的努力屡次被特朗普随后推翻，这让他即便个人真诚，也已经没有可信度。

于是整篇报道的落点是：唯一的希望是特朗普在他一贯的反复无常和优柔寡断中退一步。

这句话本身就是结论。一场可能终结一个或多个海湾国家的战争，阻止它的机制是一个人的心情。这不是国际政治的分析，这是把几千万人的处境挂在一个人的情绪曲线上，然后所有相关方都清楚这一点，却都只能围着它转。

报道里有一段话值得单独拎出来：这个区域"主要由铁腕独裁者统治，统治着殖民地图绘制者划出的、族群混杂且常常相互冲突的国家"。这句话解释了为什么海湾国家的外交动作只能是象征性的——他们的合法性不来自选民，他们的安全不来自区域共识，他们的生存依赖外部大国的保护伞。所以他们能做的只是"通过会面的存在本身"给伊朗一个外交胜利，同时向华盛顿示意自己有多担心。

而真正在承受代价的人，在报道里几乎没有名字。伊朗的基础设施、海湾的平民、被空袭封锁的也门人——他们是这场博弈的筹码，不是参与者。沙特向安萨鲁拉提出十亿美元换停火，对方不接受，因为要求是解除空中封锁。这个细节说明：钱买不到的是最基本的通行权，而通行权是人的处境，不是谈判条款。

内塔尼亚胡会继续挑衅，报道说"他肯定不久后会再试一次"。麦加条约国家会继续开会。特朗普会继续不确定。唯一确定的是，这场战争的启动按钮不在任何能阻止它的人手里。

Netanyahu needs this war more urgently than the US does. The report is explicit: Israel's coalition process is deadlocked, and he must move before the opposition can argue to the public that they are better suited to defeat Iran than he is. War here is not the outcome of diplomatic failure. It is an election instrument — an external conflict that makes "replace him" an expensive option.

Trump is a different problem. The report says he "seems unsure of how to proceed," while also being "unwilling to make the concessions necessary to end this futile, destructive war he started for no good reason." In February the war was supposed to end in days or weeks. Now it's November, and the same move is being prepared again. The report asks the right question: outside of insanity, does he have any reason to think the same action will produce a different result this time? This is not strategy. It is a man performing "solving the problem I created" as "solving the problem."

The Mecca Pact — Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Türkiye — sent their top military and intelligence officials to Tehran, and that gesture deserves a close look. The report says there is no evidence the US had any part in planning the meeting; the indications are the opposite. This is not a message relayed on Washington's behalf. It is Gulf states building a regional security dialogue around the US, not through it. Their fear is concrete: Iran has made clear it intends to target data centers, AI hubs, and underwater fiber networks, in addition to oil infrastructure. The report says the damage would dwarf anything we've seen until now.

But fear has not translated into leverage. The report admits it: Saudi Arabia has few options outside of registering its reservations with Trump. Türkiye and Pakistan have even less. Vance's letter was seen through in Tehran — no immediate relief from any pressure, yet talks must include the nuclear issue, which is the same unacceptable terms in nicer language. And because Vance's earlier efforts were routinely contradicted by Trump soon after, his personal sincerity buys him no credibility.

So the report lands on this: the only hope is that Trump, in his eternally mercurial and indecisive way, will back away.

That sentence is the conclusion. A war that could end one or more Gulf states is to be prevented by one man's mood. This is not analysis of international politics. This is tens of millions of people's fates hung on a mood curve, with every party aware of it and every party able only to circle it.

One passage deserves to be pulled out: the region is "governed as it is mostly by iron-fisted dictators ruling over states of mixed and often clashing ethnicities drawn up by colonial map-makers." That explains why the Gulf states' diplomacy can only be symbolic. Their legitimacy does not come from voters. Their security does not come from regional consensus. Their survival depends on an external power's umbrella. So all they can do is, through the meeting's very existence, hand Iran a diplomatic win while signaling to Washington how worried they are.

And the people actually bearing the cost barely have names in this report. Iranian infrastructure, Gulf civilians, Yemenis under an air blockade — they are chips in the game, not participants. Saudi Arabia offered Ansar Allah $1 billion for a ceasefire; the group will not budge on its demand that the air blockade be dropped. That detail says it: money cannot buy the most basic right of passage, and the right of passage is a human condition, not a negotiating term.

Netanyahu will keep provoking; the report says he "will surely try again before too long." The Mecca Pact countries will keep meeting. Trump will keep being unsure. The one thing certain is that the button is not in the hands of anyone who can stop it.

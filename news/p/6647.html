悲伤被拍成了她的成长背景Grief, Cast as a Girl's Backdrop

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 A.V. Club ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
一部好电影把悲伤还给两个各自孤独的人，而不是一个懂事的女儿。
A good film gives grief back to two lonely people, not to one dutiful daughter.

这篇影评从头到尾都在讲 Minnie 怎么「学会与悲伤共处」，但电影真正拍出来的是另一件事：一个还没学会悲伤语言的孩子，被要求先学会照顾大人的悲伤。

Callie 死了。Minnie 失去了姐姐，Helen 失去了女儿。报道写得很清楚，Helen「有自己的焦虑」，关于失去孩子之后接下来该怎么办；而 Minnie 看不见这些，「因为她太年轻」。于是两个失去同一个人的人，被安排在不同的时间线上悲伤——一个被允许沉进去，一个被要求快点长大。这不是剧情设定，这是分配。

影评把母亲的「漫不经心」写成 Minnie 的青春期烦恼之一，和暗恋、初吻、同学的恐同嘲弄并列。可这几件事不在一个层级上。同学嘲弄是直接的，初吻是她的，而母亲的养育方式是她没法选择、也没法退出的结构。Minnie 抱怨母亲不像 Helen 那样「宠爱」Callie，这句话被影评当作少女的别扭轻轻带过——她其实在说，这个家里的爱是有配额的，而她一直排在后面。

报道里有一句值得停下来：Helen「那么想留在 Minnie 的生活里，为了不失去和女儿的那点连接」。这句话把整部电影的权力关系说穿了。一个成年人的哀悼，需要靠一个青少年的陪伴来续命。这不是温情，这是把一个孩子征用成另一个孩子的替身。Minnie 在 Callie 的影子里站了那么久，现在还要替 Callie 继续陪着 Helen。影评把这段写成两个「急需有人说话」的人互相靠近，但只有一个人有资格说「我需要」。

Mallen Kupferer 的表演被夸得对：她的痛苦在沉默里，在怒气里，在她在不同人面前换的那副姿态里。这些细节之所以动人，恰恰因为它们不是「成长」，是一个孩子在没人接住的时候自己找地方站。影评说她发火时「不像一个任性的孩子，像一个受了伤的孩子」——这句夸奖本身就是问题：一个受伤的孩子必须表现得不像任性的孩子，才配被同情。

电影的技术选择也在帮忙。胶片相机、老铃声、Nelly 的《Hot In Herre》、绿树成荫的街道、放学后的水塘——影评说这给了电影「近乎永恒的品质」。可那个田园郊区正是失败的地方：Callie 不在了，同学就孤立 Minnie；Callie 死了，Helen 就彻底孤立。怀旧滤镜把这个郊区拍得很好看，好看得让人忘了它是怎么运作的。

影评最后落在「给彼此空间，各按各的方式悲伤」。听起来很公正。但空间不是平均分的：Helen 的空间是这个社区默认给母亲的，Minnie 的空间要靠她自己从同学的恶意和母亲的注视里抠出来。一部关于悲伤的电影，最该被追问的是谁有资格悲伤得久一点——而这个问题，报道里没有答案，影评也没有问。

The review keeps saying Minnie learns to live with grief. What the film actually shows is a child who hasn't yet learned the language of grief being asked to learn her mother's first.

Callie is dead. Minnie lost a sister, Helen lost a daughter. The piece is clear that Helen has her own anxieties about what comes next after losing a child, and that Minnie can't see them because she's too young. Two people mourning the same person are put on different timelines—one allowed to sink in, one expected to grow up fast. That isn't a plot point. That's an allocation.

The review files the mother's nonchalance under teen complaints, next to a first crush and homophobic teasing. Those aren't the same order of thing. The teasing is direct, the kiss is hers, but the way she's raised is a structure she can't opt out of. Minnie resents that her mother raised her nonchalantly while Helen doted on Callie—the review shrugs this off as adolescent sulking. She's saying the love in this house has a quota, and she's always been behind it.

One line deserves to be stopped on: Helen so badly wants to stay in Minnie's life, so as not to lose that connection to her daughter. That sentence gives the whole film's power relation away. An adult's mourning is being kept alive by a teenager's company. That isn't warmth; it's a child conscripted as a stand-in for another child. Minnie stood in Callie's shadow long enough—now she's supposed to keep Callie company for Helen too. The review reads this as two people who desperately need someone to talk to. Only one of them is allowed to say I need.

Mallen Kupferer's performance earns the praise: pain in her silence, in her rage, in the way she carries herself differently around different people. Those details move us precisely because they aren't growth—they're a kid finding somewhere to stand when nobody catches her. The review says when she lashes out it's never like a bratty child but like a hurt one. That compliment is the problem: a hurt child has to not look bratty to deserve sympathy.

The film's technique helps. Disposable cameras, old ringtones, Nelly's Hot In Herre, tree-lined streets, the watering hole after school—the review calls it almost timeless. But that idyllic suburbia is exactly what failed: with Callie gone, classmates ostracize Minnie; with Callie dead, Helen becomes isolated. The nostalgia filter makes the suburb look good, good enough that you forget how it works.

The review lands on giving each other space to grieve in their own way. Sounds fair. Space isn't distributed evenly: Helen's is the space a community grants a mother by default; Minnie has to claw hers out of classmates' cruelty and her mother's surveillance. A film about grief should be asked who gets to grieve longer—and neither the piece nor, apparently, the film answers.

苹果的尺子，量了十年才量到手指Apple's Ruler Took Ten Years to Reach a Finger

科技 文化层 · 结构层 WIRED ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
不是触屏变对了，是苹果终于把评价标准换成了自己
The touchscreen didn't become right. Apple just changed the ruler to its own.

十年前 Phil Schiller 说触屏在 Mac 上是错的方向，人体工学不对，软件界面也不对。今天同一家公司说触屏对了。中间发生了什么？芯片、屏幕、软件架构「met our bar」。注意这句话：变的不是触屏这个事实，是苹果用来量触屏的那把尺子。

Schiller 当年讲得很清楚：他们做过用户测试，结论是「我们的直觉是对的」。测试是真的，结论也是真的——在苹果自己定的标准下。问题在于这套标准的所有权。什么算「舒适」、什么算「精准」、什么算「不牺牲产品」，解释权一直在库比蒂诺的会议室里，从来没有交给过用户。用户早就用手指在屏幕上划了，报道里写得很直白：把小孩放到电脑前，他们的手直接往屏幕上戳。几十年的需求摆在那里，PC 厂商也都做了，苹果的回应是宣布这个需求不成立。

这就是元暴力在硬件行业的日常形态：不是否认你的需求，是重新定义什么算需求。等哪天自家的 OLED 屏幕、自家的芯片、自家的散热和铰链都到位了，同一个动作忽然就成了「舒适和方便」。Srouji 说「我喜欢我的工程师被物理限制，而不是被想象力和时间表限制」——话说得漂亮，但物理从来没变过，手指一直是手指。

最值得看的不是技术，是定价。触屏只给 pro 人群，起价 2899 美元和 3399 美元。十年前说触屏对所有人都不合适，十年后说触屏对少数付得起的人合适。中间那批人——想用手指划屏幕的普通用户、预算有限的年轻人——被留在了门外，而门是苹果自己造的。

Joswiak 说得很老实：pro 用户人手一台 iPad、一台 iPhone，他们天然习惯触屏，「新一代年轻创作者期待触摸」。也就是说，苹果不是被说服的，是被自己的产品线逼到墙角的——iPhone 和 iPad 把一代人的手训练成了触屏的手，Mac 再不改，就成了自家生态里唯一的异类。这不是倾听用户，这是被自己的成功追债。

Ternus 拒绝回答触屏会不会下放到 MacBook Air 和 iMac。他当然不会说。因为一旦说了「永远不会」，我们就知道该在哪里等他打脸——而这一次，等待的时间大概用不了十年。真正的问题不是苹果什么时候把触屏下放，而是下放那天，它会不会又说这是它一直以来的远见。

至于那句「谁想得到呢」——十年前的用户早就想到了，他们的手指替他们说过。

Ten years ago Phil Schiller said touch on a Mac was the wrong direction: bad ergonomics, wrong software interface. Today the same company says touch is right. What changed in between? Chips, display, software architecture—they "met our bar." Note that phrase. What changed was not the fact of touch. What changed was the ruler Apple used to measure it.

Schiller was clear back then: they had run user tests, and concluded "our instincts were correct." The tests were real. The conclusion was real—under Apple's own standard. The problem is who owns that standard. What counts as "comfortable," what counts as "precise," what counts as "not making the product lesser"—the interpretive authority has always sat in a Cupertino conference room, never with the user. Users had been putting fingers on screens for years. The piece says it plainly: put a toddler in front of a computer and the little arms shoot straight to the screen. Decades of demand, sitting right there. PC makers built for it. Apple's reply was to declare the demand invalid.

This is meta-violence in its everyday hardware form: not denying your need, but redefining what counts as a need. Then one day the in-house OLED, the in-house silicon, the in-house thermal system and hinge all arrive, and the same gesture suddenly becomes "comfortable and handy." Srouji says he likes his engineers "gated by physics, not imagination or timelines." Lovely sentence. But physics never changed. A finger was always a finger.

The interesting part isn't the technology. It's the pricing. Touch is for pros only, starting at $2,899 and $3,399. Ten years ago touch suited nobody; now it suits the few who can pay. The people in between—ordinary users who wanted to swipe, young people on a budget—are left outside a door Apple built itself.

Joswiak is honest about it: pros all own an iPad and an iPhone, they're natural with touch, "a generation of young creators" expects it. So Apple wasn't persuaded. Apple was cornered by its own product line. The iPhone and iPad trained a generation's hands for touch, and the Mac was about to become the lone alien in its own ecosystem. That's not listening to users. That's being chased down by your own success.

Ternus won't say whether touch comes to the MacBook Air or iMac. Of course he won't. Because the moment he says "never," we know exactly where to wait for the reversal—and this time the wait probably won't take ten years. The real question isn't when Apple pushes touch downmarket. It's whether, on that day, it will once again call this its long-standing vision.

As for "who knew?"—users knew. Ten years ago. Their fingers said it for them.

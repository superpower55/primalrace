恐惧是选票的稀释剂Fear Is the Thinner That Waters Down Votes

国际 文化层 · 结构层 The Intercept ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要中期选举前，得州多个投票权利组织警告，围绕ICE出现在投票站的恐惧本身正在成为压制选民的手段。塔兰特县关闭了92个投票站，提前投票期间有合格选民被错误拒绝；2021年一项法律禁止免下车投票、削弱残障选民保护并扩大投票观察员权力。5月圣安东尼奥一处投票站出现ICE人员，被当地警长驱离。组织者一边培训选民了解权利，一边担心媒体放大恐惧会让人不来投票。
The newsAhead of the midterms, Texas voting-rights groups warn that fear of ICE at the polls is itself functioning as voter suppression. Tarrant County closed 92 polling locations, and during early voting some eligible voters were wrongly turned away; a 2021 law banned drive-through voting, weakened protections for disabled voters, and expanded poll watchers' powers. In May, ICE agents appeared at a San Antonio polling site and were removed by the county sheriff. Organizers are training voters on their rights while worrying that media amplification of the fear will keep people home.
不必真的派ICE来，让人相信它会来就够了。
ICE doesn't have to show up. Making people believe it will is enough.

得州这场选举里最有效的压制工具不是ICE，是关于ICE的传言。联邦和州法律都禁止移民执法人员在投票站内出现，也禁止任何形式的选民恐吓——法律一直在那儿，特朗普政府照样宣布要“包围投票站”。声明不需要落地，恐惧自己会走路。

这就是文化暴力干活的经典方式：让一个不存在的场景变得比投票站门口的队伍更真实。塔兰特县关了92个投票站，提前投票期间有合格选民被错误地拒之门外，2021年的法律禁了免下车投票、削弱了残障选民的保护、把更多权力交给候选人指派的投票观察员——这些是结结实实的结构暴力，一条条写在法条里，不需要任何传言来帮忙。可公众讨论的焦点被ICE吸走了。

组织者的两难不是他们制造的。Daniela Padilla说得很直白：连报道“我们在盯着投票站的ICE”都可能吓退选民。这句话不是让记者闭嘴，是指出一个机制——注意力是定量的，你把它投给一个尚未发生的场景，就少了一份投给已经发生的关门和拒票。恐惧的传播成本极低，辟谣的成本极高，这是武器化表达的天然不对称。

5月圣安东尼奥那次是唯一一次已知的ICE出现在投票站，而且看起来根本不是冲着投票的人去的：州警在停车场拦了人，ICE随后到场，警长把人赶走了。一个可以被驱离的事件，被放大成一种氛围。氛围不需要被驱离，它住在人脑子里。

这里还有一层共谋。联邦政府放出威胁又不承认放过话——司法部对Newsweek说“从没说过”要派联邦执法人员去投票站，同时各州已经在准备应对。说与不说之间留出的模糊地带，正好够恐惧自己繁殖。执行者甚至不必到场，恐吓已经完成。

真正被削掉的差额在哪儿，报道写得很清楚：投票权组织在培训选民了解自己的权利，教他们在热点地区换一个投票站，把遭遇恐吓的情况报给全国热线。这是实打实的direct层防御。但报道没有写的是，那些没有组织者覆盖的县、那些不知道热线的选民，靠什么撑过选举日。恐惧的账单最后总是由信息最少的人来付。

The most effective suppression tool in this Texas election isn't ICE. It's the rumor of ICE. Federal and state law already bar immigration agents from polling sites and bar voter intimidation of any kind — the law was always there, and the administration announced it would "surround the polls" anyway. A threat doesn't need to land. Fear walks on its own.

This is cultural violence doing its classic work: making a scene that hasn't happened feel more real than the line outside the polling place. Tarrant County closed 92 polling locations. During early voting, eligible voters were wrongly turned away. The 2021 law banned drive-through voting, weakened protections for disabled voters, and handed more power to poll watchers appointed by candidates. Those are structural violence, written into statute, needing no rumor to help them. Yet the public conversation got pulled toward ICE.

The organizers' dilemma isn't one they created. Daniela Padilla put it plainly: even reporting that says "we're watching for ICE at the polls" can scare voters off. That isn't a demand that journalists shut up. It's a description of a mechanism — attention is finite, and every unit spent on a scene that hasn't happened is a unit not spent on the closures and rejections that already have. Fear travels cheap; correction travels expensive. That asymmetry is built into weaponized expression.

The one known case, in San Antonio in May, wasn't even about voting: state police pulled someone over in the parking lot, ICE arrived afterward, and the sheriff kicked them out. An incident that could be removed got inflated into a mood. A mood can't be removed. It lives in people's heads.

There's a layer of complicity here too. The federal government floats the threat and then declines to own it — the DOJ told Newsweek it "never said" it would send federal law enforcement to the polls, while states were already bracing. The ambiguity between saying and not saying is exactly wide enough for fear to breed. The enforcer never has to arrive. The intimidation is already done.

Where the gap actually gets narrowed is clear in the piece: voting-rights groups are training people on their rights, telling them they can vote at a different site in hotspots, routing reports of intimidation to a national hotline. That's real defense at the direct layer. What the piece doesn't say is what happens to voters in counties no organizer covers, who don't know the hotline exists. The bill for fear always lands on whoever has the least information.

医保停摆不算暴力Gridlock Is Not the Violence

国际 结构层 · 文化层 semafor.com ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
他们怕的不是停摆，是有人来查账
What they fear isn't a shutdown. It's an audit.

这条新闻里最诚实的一句话，是那位前共和党国会幕僚说的：准备应对民主党的监督和阻挠，要花掉我们一半的时间。

翻译一下：一半的时间不能用来改规则了。共和党人现在做的事，是给医疗政策定规则——谁能拿到补贴、谁必须证明自己够格拿 Medicaid、全球健康援助砍掉多少。这些规则一旦落地，就是 structural violence 的教科书：没有施暴者，没有血，只有一份份文件把某些人从“本可获得”里划掉。麻疹疫情还在扩大，全球健康援助被削，Medicaid 加了工作要求——报道把这些列成“监督听证会的题目”，好像它们只是政治攻防的素材。它们不是素材。它们是差额本身。

Violence = Potential − Actual。Potential 是一个孩子打了疫苗、一个病人拿得到药、一个家庭不必为了 Medicaid 提交工时证明。Actual 是这些事正在变难。这个差额不是民主党造成的，是过去这段时间的规则造成的。现在共和党人担心的，是有人要把这个差额摊到台面上来问。

他们把这件事叫“obstruction”。一个词就把问责重新命名成了捣乱。这是第三章的老手艺：夺取解释权，先给对方的动作起一个难听的名字。听证会叫“trip up administration officials”，监督叫“hairier”，好像国会的职责是一种天气。

再看人事。一周之内走了两个：Calley Means 和 CDC 副主任 Jennifer Shuford。还有几个提名卡在参议院：Chris Klomp 要当 HHS 的二号人物，Heidi Overton 要领导 FDA。那位前幕僚说得直白：要是丢了参议院，一个提名都过不了。

注意这里的机制：医疗政策的人事权，被压缩成参议院的一票之差。哪怕共和党只保住微弱多数，一个 Murkowski、一个 Paul，就能对确认程序有超额权力。也就是说，决定谁能管 FDA、谁能管 CDC 的，不是公共卫生的需要，是几个人的立场和一个席位的算术。这就是 structural violence 的运作方式——它不需要坏人，它只需要一个让少数人卡住多数人命运的制度，然后所有人管这个叫“程序”。

报道里唯一亮着的窗口，是两党可能还有共识的那几件事：医院价格透明、食品标签。这两件事的共同点是，它们不直接碰“谁有资格被覆盖”这个核心。价格透明让账单好看一点，食品标签让包装诚实一点，都不改变谁被划在外面。这不是说它们没用，是说它们恰好是那种可以在“mostly gridlock”里活下来的改革——因为它们不威胁任何人。

还有一层报道没写：这些听证会要问的题目里，麻疹、全球健康援助、Medicaid 工作要求，每一个都有具体的人在承受后果。但整篇报道里没有一个人。没有病人，没有家长，没有因为援助被砍而失去药物的地方。只有官员、幕僚、提名人和席位。谁的名字没出现，就是这篇报道最该被问的地方。

最后那句“mostly gridlock”，被当成一个中性的、甚至有点无奈的技术判断。停摆不是没有内容。停摆本身就是内容：规则停在原地，而原地正在让差额变大。他们怕的从来不是政府不动，是政府里有人开始动他们的账。

The most honest line in this story comes from a former GOP congressional staffer: preparing for Democratic oversight and obstruction will take 50% of their time.

Translate that: half their time can't go to rewriting the rules. What Republicans have been doing is setting the rules of healthcare — who gets subsidized, who has to prove they deserve Medicaid, how much global health aid gets cut. Once those rules land, that's structural violence by the book: no perpetrator, no blood, just documents moving people from "could have had" to "doesn't qualify." The measles outbreak is still growing, global health aid has been slashed, Medicaid work requirements are being implemented — and the story lists these as topics for oversight hearings, as if they were just fodder for political combat. They aren't fodder. They are the gap itself.

Violence = Potential − Actual. The potential is a child who gets vaccinated, a patient who gets medicine, a family that doesn't have to file work-hours paperwork to keep Medicaid. The actual is that all of this is getting harder. That gap wasn't created by Democrats. It was created by the rules of the past stretch. What Republicans are now worried about is that someone will put the gap on the record and ask about it.

They call this "obstruction." One word renames accountability as sabotage. This is the old craft from Chapter 3: seize the power to define, and give your opponent's move an ugly name first. Hearings are designed to "trip up administration officials"; oversight makes policymaking "hairier" — as if congressional duty were a weather pattern.

Then look at personnel. Two top health officials left in one week: Calley Means and CDC Deputy Director Jennifer Shuford. Several nominations are stuck in the Senate: Chris Klomp for the No. 2 job at HHS, Heidi Overton to lead the FDA. The former staffer puts it bluntly: lose the Senate, and no nominee gets in.

The mechanism here: control over health personnel collapses into a single Senate seat. Even a tiny GOP majority hands outsized power over confirmations to one person — a Murkowski, a Paul. Which means who runs the FDA and the CDC isn't decided by public health needs. It's decided by a few people's positions and one seat's arithmetic. That is how structural violence works. It doesn't need villains. It only needs a system where a handful can jam the fate of the many, and everyone calls it "procedure."

The one window left open is the bipartisan stuff: hospital price transparency, food labeling. What these have in common is that they don't touch the core question of who gets covered. Price transparency makes the bill legible; food labeling makes the package honest. Neither changes who's left outside. That's not to say they're useless — it's to say they're exactly the reforms that can survive "mostly gridlock," because they threaten no one.

And one layer the story leaves out: measles, global health aid, Medicaid work requirements — each has specific people living with the consequences. But there are no people in this article. No patients, no parents, no clinics that lost medicine when the aid was cut. Only officials, staffers, nominees, and seats. Whose names are missing is the question this story should be asked.

That closing line — "mostly gridlock" — is delivered as a neutral, faintly resigned technical judgment. Gridlock is not empty. Gridlock is content: the rules stay where they are, and where they are is where the gap keeps widening. They were never afraid the government would stop moving. They were afraid someone would start moving their books.

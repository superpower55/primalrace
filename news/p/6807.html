歧视是动员令，也是筛子Bigotry Is a Turnout Tool and a Filter

国际 文化层 · 元暴力 The Intercept ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要密歇根州参议院选战进入最后阶段，民主党候选人Abdul El-Sayed遭到大量针对其穆斯林身份的攻讦：特朗普称他为「圣战分子」，卫生部长小罗伯特·肯尼迪发表针对阿拉伯人的种族主义言论，还有邮件把他的头像与世贸双子塔并列。初选落败的众议员Haley Stevens在最后几天转而背书共和党对手Mike Rogers，指其反犹。一项民调显示，38%的密歇根选民表示更不愿投票给穆斯林候选人。
The newsMichigan's Senate race is in its final stretch, with Democrat Abdul El-Sayed facing a barrage of attacks on his Muslim faith: Trump called him a "jihadist," HHS Secretary RFK Jr. went on a racist rant against Arabs, and a mailer placed his face beside the Twin Towers. Rep. Haley Stevens, the primary runner-up, crossed party lines to endorse Republican Mike Rogers. One poll found 38 percent of Michigan voters said they would be less likely to vote for a Muslim candidate.
他们不是在攻击他的信仰，是在给「谁算美国人」定价。
They aren't attacking his faith. They're pricing who counts as American.

先看共和党候选人Rogers的说法：他坚称这些攻击和信仰无关。一个把对手反复和穆斯林兄弟会绑在一起的人，说这和宗教无关。这句话本身就是武器化的一部分——叙事的入口不是「他是穆斯林所以不行」，而是「他是反美的」，然后让听众自己去补上那个词。

特朗普直接叫El-Sayed「圣战分子」，肯尼迪对阿拉伯人发作了一通种族主义言论，还有邮件把他的脸放在双子塔旁边。这三件事不需要解释，它们的功能不是提供信息，是制造一个视觉判定：这张脸等于那场灾难。第一章讲表型是「你开口说话之前世界对你的视觉判定」，这里被用得干干净净。

真正值得拆的是Stevens。初选输给El-Sayed之后，她在最后几天把他描成一个愤怒的反犹者，然后跨党背书Rogers。一个民主党人在投票前一周把票交给共和党，理由是对方的以色列立场。这不是立场分歧，这是利益站队——她在这个结构里能拿到的回报，比留在自己党里多。共谋不分性别，也不分党派。

报道里有个数字要盯住：38%的密歇根选民说更不愿投票给穆斯林候选人。这就是Islamophobia作为选举工具的全部逻辑。它不要求所有人相信，只要求足够多的人被这个视觉判定推一下。密歇根大学的政治学副教授Lajevardi说这是「屡试不爽的策略」，她还说精英的歧视行为也可能反过来把人动员起来——她自己补了一句，这是开放问题。

开放问题的意思是，还没有答案。报道里能看到的答案是Dearborn那边的反应：Layla Elabed说感觉像奥巴马当年，Osama Siblani把这件事比作肯尼迪成为第一个天主教总统。Stevens倒戈之后，El-Sayed的竞选团队宣布创下单日筹款纪录。这些是真实的，但它们证明的是被攻击的人更愤怒了，不是攻击失效了。

Elabed说了一句最准的话：不是Stevens变了，是民主党终于看见了我们社区一直在说的事，我们没看错，只是没人听。这句话可以原样搬到任何一场关于「谁的声音被算作信息、谁的声音被算作抱怨」的讨论里。

还有一层没被碰。整篇报道里，El-Sayed的政纲只出现在Siblani的一句话里——「他有资格，他说的话对，他有好的议程」，然后就转去讲破天花板。一个候选人被攻击了一整场，关于他究竟要做什么的公共讨论，被压缩成一句转述。这不是他的对手干的，这是那38%和围绕它运转的整台机器干的。

Start with what Republican Mike Rogers says: the attacks have nothing to do with faith. A man who repeatedly ties his opponent to the Muslim Brotherhood insists religion isn't the issue. That sentence is itself part of the weaponization. The entry point isn't "he's Muslim, so no" — it's "he's anti-American," and the audience fills in the word.

Trump called El-Sayed a "jihadist." RFK Jr. went on a racist rant against Arabs. A mailer put his face next to the Twin Towers. None of this needs explaining. Its function isn't to inform; it's to produce a visual verdict — this face equals that catastrophe. Expression, in the book's first chapter, is the judgment the world makes before you open your mouth. Here it's used cleanly.

The real story is Stevens. After losing the primary to El-Sayed, she spent the closing days painting him as an angry antisemite, then crossed party lines to endorse Rogers. A Democrat handing her endorsement to a Republican a week before the vote, over Israel. That isn't a difference of principle; it's a side chosen for what it pays. Co-conspiracy has no gender and no party.

Hold on to one number: 38 percent of Michigan voters said they'd be less likely to vote for a Muslim candidate. That's the whole logic of Islamophobia as an electoral tool. It doesn't need everyone to believe. It needs enough people nudged by the visual verdict. Political scientist Nazita Lajevardi calls it a tried and true strategy, then adds that elite discrimination might also mobilize people — and says that's an open question.

Open question means no answer yet. What the piece does show is Dearborn: Layla Elabed saying it feels like Obama's campaign, Osama Siblani comparing it to Kennedy as the first Catholic president. After Stevens defected, El-Sayed's campaign announced a one-day fundraising record. All real. All proof that the attacked are angrier, not that the attack failed.

Elabed has the sharpest line: Stevens didn't change, the rest of the Democratic Party finally saw what our communities have been saying all along. We weren't wrong. We just weren't being listened to. That sentence transplants onto any argument about whose voice counts as information and whose counts as complaint.

One layer goes untouched. El-Sayed's actual agenda appears in exactly one quoted sentence — he's qualified, he says the right things, he has a good agenda — and then the story moves to ceiling-breaking. A candidate attacked for an entire race had the public discussion of what he'd do compressed into a paraphrase. His opponents didn't do that. The 38 percent did, and the machine that runs on it.

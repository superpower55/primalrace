买法院的人不戴面罩The Court Was Bought, Not Won

国际 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 Truthout ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
司法中立是元暴力的伪装，谁出钱谁定事实
Judicial neutrality is meta-violence in a robe: whoever pays defines the facts.

俄亥俄州最高法院的这场选举，重点不在两党谁赢。Truthout 的报道把账本摊开了：Leonard Leo 那套十亿美元级的 dark money 法院捕获机器，加上商会系的两个 super PAC，从十月中旬起在电视上把 Jennifer Brunner 和 Marilyn Zayas 打成对犯罪软弱。钱花在广告上，买的不是选票，是解释权——谁有资格被叫做“soft on crime”，谁有资格被叫做“conservative justice”。

这不是新闻，这是第三章的教科书案例。认知入口被谁把着，事实就按谁的口径被制造。2021 年共和党把党籍标签贴到司法候选人名字旁边，盖上了 SB 80 那枚章；2022 年俄亥俄州司法选举花费飙到两千一百万美元，创州史纪录。标签一贴，法官就不再是裁判，而是队伍。Brunner 起诉说这会让公众把法官看成党派工具，十月十四日联邦地方法院驳回了她——规则改完了，再问规则对不对，已经晚了。

更值得看的是这家法院把“中立”这两个字用成了什么。四月，五名共和党大法官推翻了州里七十年禁止法官政治背书的禁令，连共和党同僚 Pat Fischer 都投了唯一反对票，说多数派无视先例和程序，凭空造出一个案件里没人提的问题来给自己背书开路。当“独立、正直、公正”这几个词从司法行为准则里被搬走，剩下的只有一件事：谁给钱，谁定什么是法律。

然后是人。2024 年那起“无骨鸡翅”案，四比三的共和党多数判 Michael Berkheimer 败诉，理由是无骨指的是烹饪方式，不是真的没有骨头——一根近一英寸半的骨头卡进他食道、感染，法院说餐厅不负责。2025 年 TimkenSteel 那个氮气窒息死亡的工人，六比一的多数否决了遗孀在每周八百美元死亡抚恤之外的赔偿，理由是氮气在法律上不算“有毒”，哪怕它挤走氧气就能要人命。Brunner 在异议里写，这个判决“违背理性”。

这两起案子不是偶然，是共谋的收据。Timken 家族被报道称作俄亥俄共和党的皇族，给包括 Dan Hawkins 在内的州最高法院候选人捐过钱；Timken 公司的 Good Government Fund 和州参议员 Jane Timken 合计给审理该案的三名大法官捐了一万一千美元。法院发言人说不受回避义务约束，除非大法官自己认为有利益冲突。自己判断自己有没有冲突——这就是元暴力最赤裸的样子：解释权在谁手里，谁就不需要回避。

报道里还有一条线，比法院本身更冷。州长候选人 Ramaswamy 花几百万美元做广告，把对手 Amy Acton 失联四十七年的母亲请出来，说女儿是“骗子”。这位母亲当年站在 Acton 继父一边，而那个人因从 Acton 十岁起强奸她被起诉。用受害者的伤口做政治广告，再用她的母亲当刀——这是把女性身体当作战场的老手艺，武器化的入口从来不止一个。

所以这篇报道真正说的不是俄亥俄，是美国。企业利益和极右网络正在全国范围内改写法官怎么选、法官听谁的。报道结尾列了一串补救办法：更强的回避标准、更强的反腐和竞选资金披露、严格限制政治支出、按能力而不是按钱选法官。每一条都对，每一条都需要现在握着法院的人点头。

那根刺在这里：当法院已经被买下，你向法院申诉法院被买下，法院会告诉你，它没有义务回避。

The story out of Ohio is not which party wins. Truthout lays out the ledger: Leonard Leo's billion-dollar court-capture operation, plus two super PACs tied to the chamber-of-commerce network, have spent untold sums since mid-October painting Jennifer Brunner and Marilyn Zayas as soft on crime. The money does not buy votes. It buys the power to define—who counts as "soft on crime," who counts as a "conservative justice."

This is a textbook case from Chapter 3. Whoever holds the cognitive entry point manufactures the facts. In 2021 Republicans put party labels on judicial candidates, sealing it with SB 80; in 2022 spending on Ohio's first partisan judicial elections hit $21 million, the most expensive in state history. Once the label is on, a judge stops being an arbiter and becomes a jersey. Brunner sued, arguing the labels would make the public see judges as partisan tools. On October 14 a federal district court rejected her suit. The rule was rewritten first; asking whether the rule is right comes too late.

What matters more is what this court has done with the word "neutral." In April, five Republican justices struck down the state's 70-year ban on judges making political endorsements. Even Republican Justice Pat Fischer dissented alone, noting the majority ignored precedent and procedure and invented a question neither party had raised in order to clear its own path. When "independence, integrity, impartiality" get lifted out of the judicial code, one thing remains: whoever pays decides what the law is.

Then there are the people. In the 2024 "boneless wings" case, a 4-3 Republican majority ruled against Michael Berkheimer—a nearly one-and-a-half-inch bone lodged in his esophagus and became infected—because "boneless" refers to a cooking style, not the absence of bones. In the 2025 TimkenSteel case, a 6-1 majority denied additional damages to the widow of a worker who suffocated in a nitrogen-filled control room, because nitrogen is not legally "toxic," even though displacing oxygen kills you. Brunner, dissenting alone, wrote that the ruling "flew in the face of reason."

These are not accidents. They are receipts. The Timken family has been described as Ohio Republican royalty and has given heavily to Republican Supreme Court candidates including Dan Hawkins; the Timken Company Good Government Fund and State Sen. Jane Timken gave $11,000 collectively to three of the justices who heard the case. A court spokesperson said the justices are under no obligation to recuse unless they deem themselves conflicted. Judging your own conflict—that is meta-violence in its purest form: whoever holds the power of interpretation never has to step aside.

One thread in the report runs colder than the court itself. Ramaswamy's gubernatorial campaign spent millions on ads featuring the estranged mother of his opponent, Amy Acton, calling her daughter a "liar." That mother has not seen her daughter in 47 years, since she lost custody after siding with Acton's stepfather, who was indicted for raping Acton starting when she was 10. Using a survivor's wound as a campaign ad, with her own mother as the blade—this is the old craft of turning a woman's body into a battlefield. The weaponized entry points were never just one.

So the story is not really Ohio. Corporate interests and the far-right network are rewriting, state by state, how judges are chosen and whom they answer to. The report ends with remedies: stronger recusal standards, stronger anti-corruption and campaign-finance disclosure, strict limits on political spending, merit-based selection. All correct. All of them require a nod from the people currently holding the court.

Here is the thorn: when the court has been bought, and you petition the court about having been bought, the court will tell you it has no obligation to recuse itself.

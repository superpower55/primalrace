没有评判的舞池A Dance Floor Without a Score

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
表达一旦不被定价，人就从商品变回人。
When expression stops being priced, people stop being products.

一条关于舞蹈的新闻，削掉的却是文化层的暴力。舞池里没有评分，没有配对，没有谁在打量谁的身价——这就是它值得写的原因。

报道里那句最关键的话来自弹手风琴的 Richard Jones：ceilidh「不是异性恋规范的」。他的乐队伙伴 Neil McLean 说得更清楚：他们不分男女，只分一号和二号。这一句话就把舞伴配对这件事从性别市场上拆了下来。在 dating app 里，你上传的是一张经过挑选的脸，等待被左滑右滑定价；在 ceilidh 里，你被喊到哪个号码就牵谁的手，跳错了大家一起笑。前者是存在性战争的战场，后者是把战场暂时关掉。

所以 32 岁的 Louise 说「剥掉 ego，每个人都变回小孩的样子」，35 岁的 George 说「它就是让你觉得自己活着」。这两句话不是抒情，是准确描述：当表达不再被拿来比较、打分、分等级，人才拿回了自己的表达。

但这篇报道里还藏着一个更值得注意的机制。Eventbrite 说 2024 到 2025 年英国 ceilidh 门票涨了 16%，全球涨 15%；组织者 Ed McCabe 说疫情之后「它就爆了」。年轻人被 loneliness epidemic 缠着，被 dating app 磨到疲惫，于是往一个「你没法在里面装酷」的场合跑——报道的原话是：你在里面满头汗，笑到停不下来。这不是复古趣味，这是对一种被武器化的社交方式的一次撤退。

撤退值得肯定，但撤退不是胜利。报道里提到，这场舞是为 Link Age Southwark 这家反孤独慈善机构筹款，门票钱流向了具体的地方；这是好事，direct 和 structural 层面都有一点点真实的进账。可它同时也是一场在酒吧里办的、靠门票和场地撑起来的小生意。16% 的增长会被平台记进报表，会被包装成「communal and a lot less polished」的卖点——「不精致」本身正在变成一种可以标价的品味。今天它是解药，明天它就会被做成 Clean Girl Aesthetic 的下一版。

报道没写的是：那些最需要这种场合的人，付不起伦敦 pub 门票的人，会不会被这 16% 的增长挡在门外。舞池里没有评判，可门口有。

A story about dancing, and what it shaves off is cultural violence. No scoring, no pairing off, no one sizing up anyone's market value — that is why it is worth writing about.

The key line comes from accordionist Richard Jones: ceilidh is not heteronormative. His bandmate Neil McLean puts it more plainly — they don't do men and women, they do ones and twos. That single sentence takes partner-matching off the gender market. On a dating app you upload a curated face and wait to be swiped into a price. At a ceilidh you get called a number and take the hand that comes with it, and when you fumble the steps everyone laughs together. The first is an existential war. The second switches the war off for an evening.

So when Louise, 32, says that once you strip away the ego everyone becomes their childlike selves, and George, 35, says it just makes you feel alive, these are not pleasantries. They are accurate descriptions: when expression stops being compared, graded and ranked, people get their expression back.

There is a second mechanism buried in the piece. Eventbrite reports ticket sales for ceilidh events in the UK grew 16% from 2024 to 2025, 15% globally; organiser Ed McCabe says it has boomed since Covid. Young people worn down by a loneliness epidemic and exhausted by dating apps are running toward a room where, in the reporter's words, you cannot look cool while you are doing it — sweaty, unable to stop smiling. This is not nostalgia. It is a retreat from a weaponised way of meeting people.

The retreat is real, but a retreat is not a victory. The dance raised money for Link Age Southwark, a local anti-loneliness charity, so the ticket money went somewhere concrete — a small, genuine gain at the direct and structural levels. It is also a small business run on tickets and a pub floor. That 16% growth goes into a platform's report and gets repackaged as a selling point: communal and a lot less polished. Unpolished is becoming a taste with a price tag. Today it is the antidote; tomorrow it is the next Clean Girl Aesthetic.

What the piece does not say: whether the people who need this most — the ones who cannot afford a London pub ticket — will be kept outside by that same 16% growth. There is no judging inside the dance floor. There is at the door.

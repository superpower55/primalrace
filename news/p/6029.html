热线数据里藏着另一条暴力链The Hotline Data Hides Another Chain of Violence

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 404 Media ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
致幻剂滥用最伤人的一环，藏在仪式里
The most damaging link in psychedelic use hides inside ceremony.

这篇报道值得写，不是因为致幻剂有多少人在嗑，而是因为它第一次把"嗑药嗑坏了怎么办"这条电话线，变成了一份两万五千次求助的记录。记录里有数字，数字里有人。

先看最反直觉的一条：大麻的求助者里，47.2% 报告了糟糕的体验，比裸盖菇素、LSD、氯胺酮都高。大麻在公共叙事里是低风险、无害、甚至"自然"的东西，结果它把人送进热线求助的比例最高。这说明什么？说明我们对一种物质的"风险定价"，从来不是按药理定的，是按叙事定的。叙事说它温和，它就被当成温和；叙事说它危险，它就被当成危险。而真正在半夜被自己的知觉吓到的人，不会因为叙事说"没事"就不害怕。

更值得盯住的是那 43 起"facilitator abuse"——在偏远的静修营、在涉及死藤水的仪式场合，提供致幻剂的人被指控故意在对方不知情的情况下加大剂量。43 起，在这份两万五千次互动的样本里是极小的数字，但报道自己说了，这是"首次在社区求助样本中对这一现象的量化记录"。首次。也就是说，在这之前，这类事在数据上等于不存在。不是没发生，是没人记。

这就是第三章那套东西在起作用：谁掌握认知入口，谁就决定什么算"事实"。致幻剂产业正在往合法化、医疗化走，走的过程中需要一套叙事——疗愈、灵性、古老智慧、集体体验。这套叙事不是假的，它确实能救人。但它同时是一个入口。当"仪式"被包装成安全空间，当"引导者"被赋予某种准神圣的权威，加剂量这件事就可以被解释成"突破"、"考验"、"必要的痛苦"。而接受方在那种状态下，几乎没有任何能力说"不"。

报道里有一个细节，比那 43 起更说明问题：这些报告"主要集中在涉及植物药的仪式场合"，而且求助者中"既往 PTSD 诊断率偏高"。翻译一下：最容易在仪式里被加害的人，是本来就受过伤、正在寻找疗愈的人。施害者不需要用暴力，他只需要用"你信任我"。这是最省力的一种暴力，它连手都不用抬。

再说那条热线本身。报道说，预测一次求助是否有效的头号因素是——求助者是否感到志愿者"真诚地在乎"。Schoeller 由此推论，这暴露了集中化医院系统的问题：把人全扔进一个地方，临床医生会burnout。这个观察是对的，但它只说了一半。另一半是：一个靠陌生人的真诚来兜底的系统，它的兜底能力是有上限的。热线是 lifeline，但 lifeline 不是制度。它接住的是已经掉下来的人，接不住的是那些还没掉、但正站在没有护栏的高处的人。

所以这篇报道真正暴露的，不是致幻剂危不危险——它当然有风险，任何改变知觉的东西都有。它暴露的是：当一个东西从地下走到台面上，从非法走向合法、从娱乐走向医疗，中间那段"没人管"的地带，会被谁填满。填满它的人，未必是坏人，但一定是有定价权的人。他们定价你的体验、你的"突破"、你的信任。而你付的代价，是那 43 起里没人替你记下的部分。

热线的数据是一面镜子。它照出的不是药物，是我们在没有护栏的地方，如何把彼此交给对方。

This story is worth writing not because of how many people are tripping, but because for the first time a phone line for "what do I do when the trip goes bad" has become a record of 25,000 cries for help. There are numbers in the record, and people inside the numbers.

Start with the most counterintuitive finding: 47.2 percent of cannabis callers reported a bad experience—higher than psilocybin, LSD, or ketamine. Cannabis is framed in public narrative as low-risk, harmless, even "natural." Yet it sent the highest proportion of people to a hotline. What does that tell us? That our "risk pricing" of a substance was never set by pharmacology. It was set by narrative. The narrative says it's mild, so it's treated as mild; the narrative says it's dangerous, so it's treated as dangerous. But the person terrified by their own perception at 3 a.m. doesn't stop being terrified because the narrative says "it's fine."

What deserves closer attention is the 43 cases of "facilitator abuse"—at remote retreats, in ayahuasca ceremonies, people providing the drug were accused of deliberately increasing doses without consent. Forty-three cases is a tiny number in a sample of 25,000 interactions. But the study itself says this is "the first quantitative documentation of this phenomenon in a community help-seeking sample." First. Which means before this, the phenomenon didn't exist in the data. Not because it didn't happen, but because no one was counting.

This is Chapter 3 at work: whoever controls the cognitive entry point decides what counts as a fact. The psychedelic industry is moving toward legalization and medicalization, and that move requires a narrative—healing, spirituality, ancient wisdom, collective experience. The narrative isn't false; it does save people. But it is also an entry point. When "ceremony" is packaged as a safe space, when the "guide" is granted quasi-sacred authority, increasing the dose can be explained as "breakthrough," "test," "necessary suffering." And the person receiving it, in that state, has almost no capacity to say no.

One detail in the report matters more than the 43 cases: these reports "cluster in ceremonial settings involving plant medicines," and the callers involved had "elevated rates of prior PTSD diagnoses." Translation: the people most likely to be harmed in ceremony are the ones already wounded, already looking for healing. The abuser doesn't need violence. He only needs your trust. That is the cheapest kind of violence—it doesn't even have to raise a hand.

Then there's the hotline itself. The study found the number one predictor of whether a call worked was whether the caller felt the volunteer genuinely cared. Schoeller uses this to indict centralized hospital systems: throw everyone into one place and clinicians burn out. The observation is correct, but it's only half. The other half: a system that relies on strangers' sincerity to catch people has a ceiling. A hotline is a lifeline, but a lifeline is not an institution. It catches those who have already fallen. It cannot catch those still standing on the unguarded edge.

So what this story really exposes isn't whether psychedelics are dangerous—of course they are; anything that alters perception carries risk. It exposes what fills the gap when something moves from underground to the surface, from illegal to legal, from recreation to medicine. The people who fill that gap may not be villains, but they are the ones with pricing power. They price your experience, your "breakthrough," your trust. And what you pay is the part of those 43 cases that no one recorded for you.

The hotline data is a mirror. It doesn't show us the drugs. It shows us how, in places without guardrails, we hand each other over.

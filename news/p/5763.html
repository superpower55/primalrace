断电八小时，中产在囤货Eight Hours Dark, One Shopping List

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
囤货是私人的安全感，短缺是公共的账单
Stockpiling is private safety; shortage is a public bill.

一场断电八小时，教会一位专栏作家如何囤货。这篇稿子的诚实之处在于，它自己就把答案说出来了：一个人囤货，就是另一个人的短缺。

风暴 Austen 和 Boelo 接连过境，作者在公婆家遇上八小时断电，于是读起了政府的 Be Ready 运动和 National Resilience Checklist——每人每天三升水，够撑三天的耐储食物。清单是现成的，问题在于它假设的东西：一个能提前一周去 Tesco 多买两三件的家庭，一个有储藏空间的厨房，一个断电八小时后还能去酒吧吃薯条的位置。清单上没有写的是，谁家没有这些。

政府把韧性写成个人清单，这件事本身就是一次结构暴力的转包。电网、供水、应急储备本该由制度承担，现在变成每家每户的储藏柜。清单越详细，越像在说：断电不是系统的问题，是你准备不足。作者自己也察觉到了不对劲，一边读清单一边承认，如果所有人都开始改购买习惯，这个本就脆弱的系统会撑不住。她看到了，然后继续列购物单。

真正有意思的是她怎么处理这个矛盾。她提出“deep-pantry”方法，每周最多多买两三样，只买明天也想吃的，还配上四道沙拉食谱——Meera Sodha 的鹰嘴豆番茄沙拉、Harumi Kurihara 的羊栖菜沙拉、Anna Berrill 的沙丁鱼芹菜沙拉、Hugh Fearnley-Whittingstall 的沙丁鱼红洋葱沙拉。恐慌性抢购是不体面的，有食谱的囤货是体面的。同一件事，换一个审美包装，就从“我抢了别人的份”变成“我过得很有品质”。这是文化暴力最日常的形态：它不制造短缺，它让短缺看起来像品味。

文末那一段更说明问题。Pret 停售 jambon beurre 三明治，作者花了半年在 Lidl 复刻，然后听说牛津 Pompette 的主厨 Pascal Wiedemann 做的最好，每一样材料都自制，从开始到完成要三天，每天照卖。三天做一个三明治，和八小时断电后去酒吧吃薯条，是同一种生活的两端。这种生活里，短缺是可以被手艺和耐心绕过去的；绕不过去的人，不在报道里。

报道里没有出现任何一个在断电中真正受损的人。没有靠电力维持医疗设备的住户，没有断电就断收入的零工，没有买不起三升水一天的人。作者写的是自己，这没错，但一篇关于韧性的文章里，韧性只属于写文章的人，这个缺席本身就是材料。

八小时过去，电来了，薯条吃上了。清单留在冰箱门上，等着下一次风暴。下一次风暴来的时候，先断电的不会是 Pompette。

An eight-hour power cut taught a columnist how to stockpile. The piece is honest enough to say the quiet part itself: one person's stockpile is another person's shortage.

Storms Austen and Boelo came through, the power went out for eight hours at the in-laws', and out came the government's Be Ready campaign and the National Resilience Checklist: three litres of water per person per day, three days of non-perishable food. The checklist is ready-made. What it assumes is not: a household that can add two or three extra items a week at Tesco, a kitchen with storage, a location where chips at the pub are eight hours away from the outage. The checklist does not say who lacks these.

The government writing resilience as a personal list is structural violence outsourced. Grids, water, emergency reserves are supposed to be carried by institutions; now they are carried by every household's cupboard. The more detailed the list, the clearer the message: the outage is not the system's failure, it is your lack of preparation. The author notices this herself, admits that if everyone changes buying habits the already precarious system will buckle. She sees it, then goes back to her shopping list.

The interesting part is how she handles the contradiction. She proposes a "deep-pantry" method: two or three extra items a week, only what you would want to eat tomorrow, plus four salad recipes. Panic buying is undignified; stockpiling with recipes is tasteful. Same act, new aesthetic packaging: from "I took someone else's share" to "I live well." This is cultural violence in its most ordinary form. It does not create the shortage. It makes the shortage look like taste.

The closing section says it too. Pret stopped selling its jambon beurre, the author spent half a year recreating it with Lidl ingredients, then heard that chef Pascal Wiedemann at Pompette in Oxford makes the best one, every element in-house, three days from start to finish, sold daily. Three days for a sandwich, and chips at the pub after an eight-hour outage, are two ends of the same life. In that life, shortage can be walked around with craft and patience. The people who cannot walk around it are not in the piece.

No one who actually suffered in the outage appears in the reporting. No one on medical equipment, no shift worker who loses income when the power goes, no one who cannot afford three litres of water a day. The author writes about herself, which is fine, but in an article about resilience, resilience belongs only to the person writing it. That absence is the material.

Eight hours pass, the power returns, the chips are eaten. The list stays on the fridge door, waiting for the next storm. When the next storm comes, Pompette will not be the first to go dark.

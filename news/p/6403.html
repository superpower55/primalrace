谁有资格被记住，谁在发牌Who Gets to Be Remembered, and Who Deals the Cards

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 WIRED ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
维基百科不是真相的档案，是解释权的发牌桌。
Wikipedia is not an archive of truth. It's a card table for the right to define.

维基百科删的不是虚假条目，是"不够重要"的人。这两件事差得很远。前者是事实核查，后者是定价——谁的人生值得被写进公共记录，谁不值得。而定价权握在一群志愿者编辑手里，他们用"可靠来源"这个词，把解释权包装成了中立程序。

看 Kimberly Small。内华达一家支持性侵幸存者的非营利机构 CEO，条目以"关注度不足"被删。讨论页上一位编辑说得很坦白：非营利高管的工作重心本来就该在机构，不在个人。另一位补刀：维基的收录不是奖品。这话本身没错，但问题在于——他们真的相信这不是奖品吗？还是只有被删的人才会被告知这不是奖品？

Martina Dimoska 的案子把这套机制照得很亮。她是 NASA 约翰逊航天中心的"模拟宇航员"，在 LinkedIn 上勤恳地经营自己的职业叙事。4 月 29 日她发帖庆祝自己有了维基页面，第二天页面就被提名删除。提名的 Kiril Simeonovski 说她的条目是"过度包装的个人成就年表"，说她发帖的时机"紧接在条目通过之后"，等于抓到了她把柄。一个来自北马其顿小社区的科学家，因为公开庆祝自己被写进公共记录，反而成了被审查的理由。她要证明的不是自己做过什么，是自己"值得"被记录——而这个"值得"的标准，由别人定。

最刺眼的是那句"维基百科的收录不是奖品"。它当然不是奖品，它是通行证。骨科医生 Matthew Songer 说，他需要这个页面，因为出庭做专家证人时，维基页给了他额外的可信度。国际事务顾问 Maneli Mirkhan 说，法国媒体找她做评论员，靠的不只是她的专业，还有她在维基上这件事本身。页面被删之后，她的条目几天后又被重建了——因为更多的媒体报道自动产生了更多的"关注度"。这就是循环：有页面的人更容易被媒体引用，被媒体引用的人更容易保住页面。没有页面的人，连证明自己重要的机会都没有。

这套逻辑和书里说的认知入口是同一个东西。维基百科不是靠暴力删人，它靠的是"可查证性"这个看似中立的筛子。编辑 Britta Gustafson 说得很清楚："我们找的不是什么是真的，我们找的是什么是可查证的。"这句话本身是诚实的，但它掩盖了一个事实——可查证性本身就是一种资源。谁有媒体关系，谁有公关预算，谁在英语世界里被报道过，谁就"可查证"。北马其顿的一位科学家，和一位有《每日电讯报》背书的英国艺术家，站在同一条起跑线上吗？

更荒诞的是 Sophie Aston。她的维基页面是 17 年前一个只约会过两次的男人"作为惊喜"建的。她说那页面"浮夸"，把她写成了一个重要艺术家，而她不是。她转行做了心理治疗师，希望忘掉自己的艺术家生涯。维基编辑们讨论之后，以关注度不足为由删掉了她的页面——但一位编辑写得很直白：删掉她的条目和她的请求无关，她的艺术生涯本来就不达标。她的意愿没有被尊重，只是恰好和结论一致。

Sheldon Marks 的遭遇更干脆。一个自称是他朋友的人跑去维基说他希望删除自己的页面，编辑们投票决定删除之后，Marks 本人出现了，说他从没要求删除，那些话是编的。编辑们短暂商议了一下，回复说：我们已经投票了。程序走完了，事实来晚了。

Simeonovski 自己在 2008 年加入维基后做的第一件事，就是给自己建了一个页面。后来被删了。当时一位编辑对他说：如果你的成就是可查证的、真正值得关注的，迟早会有别人来给你建页面。到今天，没有人建。

这就是整个故事的核心。维基百科的"关注度"标准，不是衡量一个人做了什么的尺子，是衡量一个人被谁看见、被什么媒体写过的尺子。它奖励的从来不是成就本身，是成就被记录的能力。而那些没有能力被记录的人——内华达的性侵幸存者支持者、北马其顿的模拟宇航员、密歇根的骨科医生——被告知他们的工作"不是奖品"。

Dimoska 的页面最后保住了，但附上了一条"含宣传性内容"的警告。她赢了，赢得像是被赦免。而 Simeonovski 还在那里，等着下一个在 Facebook 上庆祝自己上维基的人。

Wikipedia doesn't delete false entries. It deletes people who aren't "important enough." Those are two very different things. The first is fact-checking. The second is pricing—deciding whose life deserves to be in the public record and whose doesn't. And the pricing power sits with a group of volunteer editors who wrap the right to define in a neutral-sounding word: "reliable sources."

Look at Kimberly Small. CEO of a Nevada nonprofit that supports survivors of sexual assault. Her biography was deleted on notability grounds. On the public debate page, one editor put it plainly: the focus of a nonprofit executive's job should be the organization, not the person. Another added: Wikipedia coverage is not a prize. That's true, as far as it goes. But do they actually believe it's not a prize? Or is that only what people are told after they've lost it?

Martina Dimoska's case lights up the machinery. She's an "analog astronaut" at NASA's Johnson Space Center, working her professional narrative on LinkedIn with can-do diligence. On April 29 she posted to celebrate having a Wikipedia page. The next day, the page was nominated for deletion. Kiril Simeonovski called it "merely a chronology of overhyped personal achievements" and treated the timing of her Facebook post—"shortly after the article was approved"—as a smoking gun. A scientist from a small community in North Macedonia was put on trial for the crime of publicly celebrating her inclusion in the public record. What she had to prove wasn't what she'd done. It was that she was "worthy" of being recorded—and the standard of worth was set by someone else.

The most telling line is "Wikipedia coverage is not a prize." Of course it isn't a prize. It's a passport. Orthopedist Matthew Songer said he needs his page because it gives him extra credibility as an expert witness in court. International affairs adviser Maneli Mirkhan said French media sought her out as a commentator not just for her expertise but because she was on Wikipedia. After her page was deleted, it reappeared a few days later—because more media coverage automatically generated more "notability." That's the loop: people with pages get cited more easily; people who get cited more easily keep their pages. People without pages never get the chance to prove they matter.

This is the same mechanism the book calls the cognitive entrance. Wikipedia doesn't delete people with violence. It uses "verifiability" as a neutral-looking sieve. Editor Britta Gustafson said it clearly: "We're not really looking for what's true. We're looking for what's verifiable." That's honest, and it hides a fact—verifiability is itself a resource. Whoever has media connections, PR budgets, and coverage in the English-speaking world is "verifiable." Does a scientist in North Macedonia stand on the same starting line as a British artist with the Telegraph behind her?

Sophie Aston's case is more absurd still. Her Wikipedia page was created 17 years ago by a man she'd been on two dates with, as a surprise. She called it "ostentatious," said it made her sound like an important artist when she isn't—she's been a psychotherapist for a decade and wants to forget her art career. The editors deliberated and deleted her page on notability grounds. One wrote bluntly that her request to delete was irrelevant; her artistic career didn't meet the guidelines anyway. Her wishes weren't respected. They just happened to coincide with the verdict.

Sheldon Marks's case is blunter. A man claiming to be his friend announced on Wikipedia that Marks wanted his page removed. The editors voted to delete. Then Marks himself showed up and said he'd never asked for deletion and the statements were fabricated. The editors conferred briefly and replied that they'd already voted. The procedure had run its course. The facts arrived late.

Simeonovski's first act after joining Wikipedia in 2008 was to create a page about himself. It was promptly deleted. An editor told him at the time: if your achievements are verifiable and genuinely notable, someone else will probably create an article about you sooner or later. To date, nobody has.

That's the heart of the story. Wikipedia's notability standard isn't a ruler for what a person has done. It's a ruler for who has seen them and which outlets have written about them. It rewards not achievement but the ability to have achievement recorded. And those who lack that ability—the Nevada advocate for assault survivors, the North Macedonian analog astronaut, the Michigan orthopedist—are told their work "is not a prize."

Dimoska's page survived, with a warning that it contains "promotional content." She won the way a pardon is won. And Simeonovski is still there, waiting for the next person who celebrates being on Wikipedia.

赦免权不是漏洞，是设计Pardon Power Is Not a Bug, It Is the Design

国际 结构层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
把赦免权叫成制宪者的失误，是在替制度免责。
Calling the pardon power a founding error lets the design off the hook.

Rosen 这篇文章的结论是：制宪者犯了一个严重的错误，给了总统赦免权。这个结论把责任放错了地方。制宪者没有失误，他们做的是精确的设计——一个可以赦免叛乱的权力，写进宪法，由麦迪逊亲口辩护，理由是可以靠弹劾兜底。两百多年后，弹劾用了两次，人还在，还连任了。这不是漏洞被钻了，这是设计按预期运转了。

赦免权的原型是英国的“王室特赦权”，汉密尔顿拿它当依据。一群刚刚从君主制里独立出来的人，把君主的权力搬进新宪法，还觉得这是对总统的约束。Rosen 说这“近乎不理性”，说得太客气了。这是把解释权从国王手里接过来，再交回给一个换了头衔的国王。谁有权定义什么是罪、什么罪可以被抹掉，谁就握着事实的制造权。

梅森在1787年9月15日说过的话，比 Rosen 引用的任何一句都更准：总统可能赦免“由他自己授意犯下的罪”。特朗普赦免1500多名冲击国会的人，动机就写在这句话里——那些人用暴力试图让他留在他已经输掉的位子上。这不是赦免制度被滥用，这是赦免制度在履行它被写进去时的那项功能：让掌权者可以回收自己人的罪。

更有意思的是“赦免市场”。据《纽约时报》《华尔街日报》和《60分钟》的报道，罪犯的亲友出价数百万美元换总统的宽宥。这一层不需要任何理论包装：认罪与免罪变成了一门生意，定价权在总统手里，付钱的人买的是“我没做过”这个说法。赦免权在这里不是法律程序，是货币。

Rosen 列了一串“其他糟糕的赦免”：卡特赦免他那个不省心的弟弟比利，拜登赦免亨特，克林顿在2001年1月20日离任当天赦免140人、为另外36人减刑，其中有钱的竞选金主。他把这些都归为“糟糕”，然后说特朗普的赦免“独一份”，因为动机不同。这个区分站不住。所有这些赦免共享同一个结构：掌权者用定义罪的权力照顾自己的人。区别只在于特朗普照顾的人手里拿着棍子。

文章结尾说，多数美国人在近六年后仍然认为赦免那些暴力叛乱者是错的。这句话被写成了安慰。民意认为错，然后呢？没有然后。制宪者把这道门留给了行政权，麦迪逊给出的保险丝是弹劾，弹劾已经证明自己烧不断。真正的问题不是1787年那55个人在费城的闷热里判断失误，而是两百多年来没有人愿意承认：一个能自己定义什么是罪、什么不是罪的权力，本身就是元暴力——对解释权的垄断，写在最高法里，还盖了章。

梅森说他宁愿砍掉自己的右手，也不签这部宪法。他的手保住了，宪法也签了。今天读这篇文章的人该问的不是制宪者错在哪里，而是：当赦免市场公开运行、当叛乱者被批量放出来、当弹劾被证明是纸糊的保险丝，谁还在替这套设计辩护，辩护的人从里面拿到了什么。

Rosen's conclusion is that the founders made a grave error in granting presidents the pardon power. That conclusion puts the blame in the wrong place. The founders made no error. They made a precise design: a power to pardon treason, written into the Constitution, defended by Madison himself on the grounds that impeachment would serve as the backstop. Two centuries later, impeachment was used twice, the man is still there, and he was re-elected. This is not a loophole being exploited. This is the design working as intended.

The pardon power's prototype was the British "royal prerogative of mercy," which Hamilton cited as precedent. A group of men who had just declared independence from monarchy imported the monarch's power into their new constitution and called it a check on the president. Rosen calls this "contradictory bordering on irrational." That is too generous. It is taking the power to define what counts as a crime and handing it back to a king with a different title. Whoever decides which crimes can be erased holds the power to manufacture fact.

What Mason said on 15 September 1787 is sharper than anything Rosen quotes: a president might pardon "crimes which were advised by himself." Trump's pardon of more than 1,500 people who stormed the Capitol has its motive written into that sentence. They used violence to keep him in an office he had lost. This is not the pardon power being abused. This is the pardon power doing the job it was written to do: letting the powerful reclaim their own.

Then there is the pardons market. According to reporting by the New York Times, the Wall Street Journal and 60 Minutes, friends and relatives of criminals offer millions for presidential clemency. This layer needs no theoretical dressing: guilt and innocence have become a business, the price is set by the president, and what the buyer pays for is the sentence "I didn't do it." Here the pardon is not a legal procedure. It is currency.

Rosen lists a string of "other bad pardons": Carter protecting his wayward brother Billy, Biden pardoning Hunter, Clinton pardoning 140 people and commuting 36 sentences on his last day in office, 20 January 2001, among them his brother and several large campaign donors. He files them all under "bad" and then says Trump's is in a class of its own because of the motive. That distinction does not hold. All these pardons share one structure: the powerful use the power to define crime to take care of their own. The only difference is that the people Trump took care of were carrying clubs.

The piece ends by noting that a large majority of Americans, almost six years on, still say pardoning the violent insurrectionists was wrong. That is written as consolation. The public says it was wrong. And then what? Nothing. The founders left this door to the executive, and Madison's fuse was impeachment, and impeachment has proven itself unfusable. The real question is not whether 55 men in a sweltering Philadelphia room misjudged in 1787. It is why, for more than two centuries, no one has been willing to admit that a power to define what is and is not a crime is meta-violence itself: a monopoly on interpretation, written into supreme law and stamped with a seal.

Mason said he would sooner chop off his right hand than sign that Constitution. His hand was spared, and the Constitution was signed. The question for anyone reading this piece today is not where the founders went wrong. It is: when the pardons market runs in the open, when insurrectionists are released in batches, when impeachment has been shown to be a paper fuse, who is still defending this design, and what do the defenders get out of it.

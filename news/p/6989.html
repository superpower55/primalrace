榜单是解释权的分发站A List Is a Distribution Point for the Power to Define

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要Billboard 编辑部评选出 2025 年 20 张最佳说唱专辑，Tyler, The Creator 的《Don't Tap the Glass》、Cardi B 时隔七年发行的《Am I the Drama?》、Clipse 的十五年重组专辑《Let God Sort 'Em Out》等入选，Central Cee 成为首张进入 Billboard 200 前十的英国说唱专辑。
The newsBillboard staff picked the 20 best rap albums of 2025, including Tyler, The Creator's Don't Tap the Glass, Cardi B's Am I the Drama? after a seven-year gap, and Clipse's 15-year reunion album Let God Sort 'Em Out. Central Cee's major-label debut became the first U.K. rap album to reach the Billboard 200 top 10.
谁定排名，谁就定什么算好。
Whoever sets the ranking sets what counts as good.

Billboard 这份 20 佳说唱专辑榜单，第一件要说清楚的事是：它不是对 2025 年说唱音乐的记录，它是对 2025 年说唱音乐的定价。二十个席位、每个席位后面一段一百来字的评语，谁进谁不进、谁排前面谁排后面，都是编辑部在替整个行业划定「什么算好」的坐标。榜单上的人赢了这一轮，榜单外的人连同他们的作品一起消失了。

看这份名单的构成。Tyler、Cardi B、Clipse、Freddie Gibbs、Conway the Machine、Westside Gunn、Pusha T 和 Malice——熟面孔占了大半，评语里反复出现的关键词是「回归」「重组」「续集」「二部曲」。Clipse 是十五年重组，Alfredo 是五年前那张的续集，Cardi B 是七年之后，Metro Boomin 干脆把 2000 年代中期的亚特兰大翻出来重做一遍。榜单在奖励「延续」，而不是「发生」。

评语里唯一被明确点出的「新生力量」，是 Samara Cyn、Ovrkast、Central Cee 三个人。这份名单二十个席位，三个人。剩下十七个都在既有秩序里挪动位置。这不是说唱的问题，这是所有由编辑部评选的榜单的共同机制：榜单的合法性来自它被认为「懂行」，而「懂行」的定义权握在已经成名的人手里。新人要进榜，得先被这套坐标认出来。

再看评语是怎么写人的。Cardi B 那段写她「夺回了自己的叙事」，用「brazen bars 和 bully 级别的魅力」；OnlyHeaven 那段写她「在 New Orleans bounce 上写诗」，谈的是恋爱里的「trials and tribulations」。这两段不是问题，问题是整份榜单里，女性 rapper 的评语几乎都绕不开「关系」和「身体魅力」，男性 rapper 的评语绕不开「技艺」「遗产」「兄弟情」。同一份榜单，两套评价语言。这就是元暴力在文化版面上的日常形态——不是谁被骂了，而是谁被用什么词汇描述。

榜单末尾那段关于 Metro Boomin 的评语值得单独拎出来：「在一个没人在俱乐部跳舞的年代，这张 mixtape 是需要的。所有人只会站着抽水烟看手机。」这句话写得很准，但它也暴露了这份榜单的自我定位：它不是在记录一个正在发生的文化，它是在怀念一个已经过去的、有身体在场的文化，然后给怀念这件事排个名。

那这份榜单有没有缩小什么差额？有，很有限。它给了 Samara Cyn、Ovrkast、Jim Legxacy 这样的名字一个能被看见的位置，这是实打实的注意力再分配。但这份再分配是在编辑部划定的二十个席位里完成的，席位总数没变，定价权没动。Central Cee 那张专辑「没达到他期待的商业规模」，却因为「首张进入 Billboard 200 前十的英国说唱专辑」被写进榜单——榜单在这里奖励的不是音乐，是一个历史性的排名数字。

真正没被写进这份榜单的，是那些没有编辑部背书、没有大厂牌发行、没有「回归」故事可讲的专辑。它们不是不够好，是没有人替它们写那一百来字的评语。榜单每年都在做这件事，每年都有人因此被看见，也每年都有人因此不被看见。

The first thing to say about Billboard's 20 Best Rap Albums of 2025 is that it is not a record of the year in rap. It is a pricing of the year in rap. Twenty slots, a hundred-word blurb behind each one — who gets in, who doesn't, who lands where — the editorial desk drawing the coordinates for what counts as good. The names on the list win this round. The names off it, and the work with them, disappear.

Look at the composition. Tyler, Cardi B, Clipse, Freddie Gibbs, Conway the Machine, Westside Gunn, Pusha T and Malice — familiar faces take most of the room, and the blurbs keep returning to the same words: return, reunion, sequel, part two. Clipse is a fifteen-year reunion, Alfredo 2 is a sequel to a project from five years ago, Cardi B arrives after seven years, and Metro Boomin rebuilds mid-2000s Atlanta outright. The list rewards continuation, not occurrence.

The only explicitly named new movement is three names: Samara Cyn, Ovrkast, Central Cee. Three out of twenty. The other seventeen shift position inside an existing order. This is not a rap problem; it is the shared mechanism of every editorially judged list. A list's legitimacy comes from being seen as knowing the field, and the power to define knowing sits with those already famous. A newcomer gets in by first being recognized by those coordinates.

Then look at how people are written. Cardi B's blurb has her reclaiming her narrative with brazen bars and bully-level charisma; OnlyHeaven's has her waxing poetic over New Orleans bounce about the trials and tribulations of relationships. Neither blurb is the problem. The problem is that across the list, women rappers are described through relationships and presence, men through craft, legacy, brotherhood. Two vocabularies, one list. This is meta-violence in its ordinary cultural-section form — not who gets insulted, but who gets described with which words.

The Metro Boomin blurb deserves its own line: this tape was needed in an era where nobody dances at the club anymore, where all they do is stand around and smoke hookah while looking at their phones. It is an accurate sentence, and it also gives away the list's self-positioning: not documenting a culture in motion, but mourning one that had bodies in it, and then ranking the mourning.

Did the list shrink any gap? A little. It hands Samara Cyn, Ovrkast and Jim Legxacy a visible position, and that is a real redistribution of attention. But the redistribution happens inside twenty slots the desk controls. The number of slots is unchanged; the pricing power is untouched. Central Cee's album did not reach the commercial scale he wanted, yet it makes the list for being the first U.K. rap album in the Billboard 200 top 10 — the list rewarding a historic chart number, not the music.

What never makes this list is the work with no editorial backing, no major-label release, no reunion story to tell. Not because it is worse, but because nobody writes its hundred words. The list does this every year. Every year someone is seen because of it, and someone is not.

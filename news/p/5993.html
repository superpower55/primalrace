特朗普不装的那句话才是实话The One Honest Line Was the Vulgar One

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Democracy Now! ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
危机不是意外，是分配。
The crisis is not an accident. It is an allocation.

特朗普在乔治亚州那句话说得很痛快：油价暂时高，等战争一结束就会大跌，美国油气业正在赚几千亿。他连假标题都替媒体拟好了——“总统谈赚钱，民众在加油站受苦”——然后说，我不在乎，我们赚翻了。这句话比任何政策白皮书都诚实：涨价不是事故，是设计。

韦伯给出的两个数字把这件事钉死了：加油站价格涨了45%，石油煤炭行业利润涨了415%。同一场对伊朗的战争，一边是普通人多掏的钱，一边是行业多赚的钱，中间没有偶然，只有通道。加尔通的公式在这里不需要修辞：本可以不让油价这样涨，本可以不让战争开始，差额就是暴力，而且这次的差额直接进了财报。

特朗普那句“国家在赢”是这套机制的标准话术。国家没有在赢，50%的利润流向最富的1%，底层50%只拿到大约1%。把“国家”当成一个整体来说话，是让受损的人以为自己也在分红。这是文化层的操作：先制造一个不存在的共同体，再让这个共同体替真实的分配打掩护。

韦伯的判断是对的：这种程度的私人经济权力集中，已经和任何有意义的民主不相容。但她开的药方停在“民主国家要站出来”——价格上限、价格下限、公共供给、市政超市。这些是结构层的东西，可以缩小差额，我认。但报道里从头到尾没有出现一个问题：谁在共谋。

油气公司赚了415%，不是它们单独做到的，是政府开了战争、放了储备、看着价格冲上去，然后两边都说这是市场。华尔街对卢拉胜选的反应、对法国抗议的反应，韦伯自己都点了——资本已经选边。那么“民主国家站出来”这句话就有一个前提没被追问：站出来的那个国家，和给战争开绿灯的那个国家，是同一个。

最锋利的一段是她讲谁会投向极右：不是最穷的人，是“还有点东西可失去”的人，是被 squeeze 的那一批。他们每天上班，日子却越来越不稳，极右过来告诉他们：你本来是有价值的，因为你是白人、你是男人，你比那些人强；你没能耐，是因为女人不待在家里、跨性别孩子进了体育俱乐部、残障孩子进了教室。这套说辞的骨架是男性中心叙事——把市场的失败翻译成等级被“污染”了，把“你本该在上位”还给他们。韦伯说得很准，连最新自由主义的政策都能和极右共存，因为承诺是：清掉这些杂质，市场就纯了，你就行了。

于是“ affordability”这个词本身成了战场。特朗普说对手在用这个词，他的回应是干脆否认危机存在。否认是一种更省事的武器化：只要不承认差额，差额就不需要被填补。

韦伯的书名里写着反法西斯，但她对法西斯的诊断是经济不安全感，药方是让生活可负担。这个链条少了一环：极右卖的不是面包，是等级。面包可以靠市政超市发下去，等级不会因为一家超市开在亨茨点就消失。

Trump said it plainly in Georgia: oil prices are temporarily high, they will fall once the war is over, and the U.S. oil and gas business is making hundreds of billions. He even previewed the fake headline — president talks profits while people suffer at the pump — and said he didn't give a damn, we're making a freaking fortune. That sentence is more honest than any policy paper: the price shock is not a malfunction, it is a channel.

Weber puts two numbers on it: pump prices up 45%, profits in petroleum and coal up 415%. Same war on Iran, one side pays more at the pump, the other books record profits even above 2022. Galtung's formula needs no rhetoric here — the gap between what could have been and what is went straight into an earnings report.

Trump's "the nation is winning" is the standard move of this machinery. The nation is not winning: 50% of those profits go to the top 1%, the bottom 50% get about 1%. Speaking of "the nation" as a single body lets the people absorbing the loss believe they are sharing the gain. That is cultural-layer work: manufacture a community that does not exist, then let it cover for the actual distribution.

Weber is right that this concentration of private economic power is no longer compatible with any meaningful notion of democracy. But her remedy stops at "democratic states must stand up" — price ceilings, price floors, public provisioning, municipal grocery stores. Those are structural tools and they can shrink the gap. What the interview never asks is who is conspiring.

Oil and gas did not earn 415% alone. The government started the war, released reserves, watched prices climb, and both sides called it the market. Weber herself notes how markets reacted to Lula's win and to the protests in France — capital has picked a side. So "the democratic state stands up" has an unexamined premise: the state that stands up and the state that green-lit the war are the same state.

Her sharpest passage is on who turns to the far right: not the poorest, but those who still have something to lose, the squeezed. They work every day and their lives grow more precarious, and the far right tells them they are worth something because they are white, because they are men, because they are better than those others; that they fail because women left the home, trans kids entered the sports club, disabled children entered the classroom. That is the skeleton of a masculine-centric narrative — market failure translated into hierarchy polluted, and "you belonged on top" handed back to them. Weber is precise that even neoliberal policies coexist with the far right, because the promise is: remove the impurities, the market is pure, and you will be fine.

So "affordability" itself becomes the battlefield. Trump notes that his opponents use the word, and his answer is to deny the crisis exists. Denial is the cheaper weapon: if the gap is not admitted, it never has to be filled.

The book's title says anti-fascist, but its diagnosis is economic insecurity and its cure is affordability. One link is missing: the far right does not sell bread, it sells rank. Bread can be handed out through a city-owned store. Rank does not disappear because a grocery store opens in Hunts Point.

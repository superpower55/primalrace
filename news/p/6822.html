六周复工，不是励志故事Six Weeks Back Is Not a Comeback Story

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 The Cut ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要本月初，Barbara Palvin 产后六周重返维多利亚的秘密时装秀。她在万圣节派对联合主持时表示，外界反应「五五开」，有人叫好，也有人认为她该在家休息。她感谢丈夫支持，并说每个人的产后经历不同。
The newsEarlier this month, Barbara Palvin returned to the Victoria's Secret Fashion Show six weeks after giving birth. Co-hosting a Halloween party, she said reactions were "50-50": some cheered her return, others said she should be resting. She thanked her husband for his support and noted everyone's postpartum journey differs.
她把没有产假说成美国常态，这句话本身就是控诉。
Calling no maternity leave "normal" is itself the indictment.

Palvin 说产后六周走秀是「每个美国女人都在做的事」，因为「产假就是六到八周」。这句话比任何女权宣言都锋利——她说的是事实，美国是发达国家里唯一不保证全国带薪产假的。问题在于，她说完这句就把它当背景板收起来了，转而去感谢丈夫、去讲「每个人的产后经历不同」。

「每个人的产后经历不同」是一句真话，也是一句最省事的挡箭牌。不同在哪里？不同在于有人六周后能站上 T 台，有造型师、营养师、保姆和一位公开致谢的丈夫；有人六周后必须回去上班，因为账单不等她的子宫恢复。前者是她的产后经历，后者是那六到八周政策制造出来的产后经历。把两者并列成「不同」，等于把结构问题翻译成个人品味。

报道里最值得盯住的一处，是它自己写下的：她「没有回应」那些指出美国产假政策逼女人过早返岗的人。这不是疏忽，这是选择。一个刚生完孩子的女人站在镜头前，她最该被问的问题不是「你恢复得怎么样」，而是「你刚才引用的那个政策，你觉得它对你之外的人做了什么」。这个问题没被问，她也没答。

维多利亚的秘密请她回来，当然不是做慈善。这家公司过去几年被批评身材单一、物化女性，如今需要一个「产后六周就回来」的身体来证明自己进步了、包容了。她的身体被拿来当叙事道具：看，生育不会让女人失去性感。代价是她本人要在六周内完成别人六个月的恢复，而这份代价被包装成「她自愿」「她状态很好」。自愿在结构里从来不是一个干净的词。

真正该被追问的，是那条六到八周的线是谁画的。它画在女人身上，受益的是不需要休产假的那一方：雇主省下人力成本，丈夫不必分担夜奶，时装秀按时开演。Palvin 只是这条线上一个被拍下来的点，她漂亮、她有钱、她感谢丈夫，这些都让她更像一个榜样，而榜样是最有效的规训工具——既然她能做到，你为什么不能？

她给新妈妈的建议是「别怕开口求助」。这句话没错，但求助的前提是有人可求。对一个没有带薪产假、没有保姆、丈夫也在上班的女人来说，她开口之后，电话那头是忙音。

Palvin said going back to work six weeks after giving birth is "just doing what every woman in America does," because "maternity leave is six to eight weeks" here. That sentence is sharper than any feminist manifesto: she is stating a fact. The U.S. is the only developed country that does not guarantee national paid leave. The problem is that she said it and then filed it away as background, moved on to thanking her husband and noting that everyone's postpartum journey is different.

"Everyone's postpartum journey is different" is true and also the most convenient shield available. Different how? Different in that one woman can walk a runway at six weeks with a stylist, a nutritionist, a nanny, and a husband she thanks on camera; another must clock in at six weeks because the bills do not wait for her uterus. The first is her journey. The second is the journey that six-to-eight-week policy manufactures. Placing them side by side as "different" translates a structural fact into a matter of taste.

The most telling line in the piece is the one the reporter writes: she did not respond to people pointing out that American leave policy pushes women back to work too early. That is not an oversight. It is a choice. A woman who just gave birth stands in front of cameras, and the question she should be asked is not how she is recovering but what she thinks the policy she just cited does to everyone who is not her. That question was not asked, and she did not answer it.

Victoria's Secret did not bring her back out of charity. The brand spent years being criticized for a single body type and for objectifying women; now it needs a body that returned six weeks postpartum to prove it has evolved. Her body is the narrative prop: see, motherhood does not cost you sex appeal. The price is that she must complete in six weeks a recovery that takes others six months, and that price gets packaged as "her choice" and "she looks great." Choice is never a clean word inside a structure.

What deserves interrogation is who drew that six-to-eight-week line. It is drawn on women's bodies, and the beneficiaries are the parties that do not need maternity leave: the employer saves on labor costs, the husband is not expected to split night feeds, the fashion show opens on schedule. Palvin is simply a photographed point on that line. She is beautiful, she is rich, she thanks her husband, and all of it makes her a better role model, and a role model is the most effective instrument of discipline: if she could do it, why can't you?

Her advice to new mothers is "don't be scared to ask for help." Fine advice, but asking requires someone on the other end. For a woman with no paid leave, no nanny, and a husband also at work, she asks, and the line is busy.

他拆了那堵墙，墙外的人还没进来He Broke One Lock, Not the Wall

其他 结构层 · 文化层 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
分区制是种族隔离的化石，SB 79 只敲掉了一角
Zoning is fossilized segregation. SB 79 chipped off one corner.

SB 79 通过的那一票，削掉的是结构层暴力的一小块：一条用土地用途法把穷人和非白人挡在城外、挡了快一个世纪的门闩。不是全部，是一小块。

报道把这条法律的来历写得很清楚：19世纪末的旧金山是最早用土地用途法把非白人挡在白人社区之外的城市之一，后来这套办法被包装成中性的"分区制"（zoning），扩散到全美，用来保护战后郊区的生活方式。洛杉矶1970年代人口280万，分区规则理论上能装1050万人，于是城市改了规则，新上限410万，今天人口390万。这不是市场，这是用法律写下来的排除。加尔通说结构暴力是没有施暴者的暴力，这就是标本：没有人需要站出来说"我不欢迎你"，规则替他们说了，而且说得像常识。

所以 Wiener 做的事，本质上不是放松管制，是把解释权从地主手里往回抢一点。SB 827 在2018年死掉，被骂成"对社区宣战"、"把西洛杉矶变成迪拜"，但它的失败给后面十年的技术性法案打了掩护：附属住宅单元（ADU）放开后批了八万多套，"符合分区就不得拒绝"的许可改革，还有让城市达不到住房目标就自动批准大项目的 builders' remedy。报道里 Skinner 那句话说得直白：Scott 的喷火器法案给了我们窗口，谁会去注意 granny flat？这就是武器化表达的正面用法——用一个注定失败的显眼法案，掩护一串真正能落地的改动。

但代价写在同一篇报道里，只是没有放在标题上。SB 79 为了过票，塞进了建筑工会的保护条款，豁免了高使用率公交站少于16个的县，于是富裕的马林县和郊区化的康特拉科斯塔县直接出局；人口少于3.5万的城市，受影响半径从半英里缩到四分之一英里。每一条都是共谋的价码：想赢，就得给能挡住你的人分一块。Wiener 自己也不装："你解决你能解决的、不伤害政策的部分。"

真正的问题是，这套政策把房子当成了唯一变量。Aaron Peskin 的反驳值得认真对待，不是因为他站在对面，而是因为他指出的东西报道自己也没法反驳：租金管制、政府补贴建房、利率和建造成本，这些才是决定谁住得起的东西，而加州房价至今"顽固地高"，2023年那份元分析也只敢说长期"可能"降低租金。SB 79 要过很多年才可能有可测量的差别——这是 Wiener 自己承认的。

还有一个没有被追问的分配问题。报道写得很清楚，这场运动的起点是"找不到公寓的年轻专业人士"，而它的胜利形式是让市场价公寓在公交站旁合法化。市场价公寓不会自动变成低收入者的家，报道里那位在街头说"我当年为他助选，现在受不了他"的选民，和那位弯腰问"这些新房子会不会是低价的"的老太太，问的其实是同一件事。报道没有给出答案，Wiener 也没有，他只说"我们就是要建更多房子"。

至于"更多住房能对抗种族主义、能救民主党在国会的席位"这套说法——它可能是对的，但它把一件关于谁有地方住的事，翻译成了一件关于政党版图的事。这正是元暴力最擅长的手法：把人的处境重新命名成权力的变量，然后大家就都忘了最初那个问题是"人住在哪里"。

SB 79 拆掉的是一道门闩，不是那堵墙。墙还在，只是换了个更技术性的名字，等着下一轮听证会。

The vote that passed SB 79 shaved off a small piece of structural violence: a land-use latch that kept poor and nonwhite people out of cities for nearly a century. Not the whole thing. A piece.

The reporting lays out the lineage plainly: late-19th-century San Francisco was among the first to use land-use law to keep nonwhite people from living near white people, and the method got repackaged as neutral "zoning" and spread across the country to protect postwar suburban life. Los Angeles in the 1970s had 2.8 million people and zoning capacity for 10.5 million, so the city changed the rules—new cap 4.1 million, population today 3.9 million. That isn't a market. That's exclusion written into law. Galtung's structural violence has no perpetrator; this is the specimen. Nobody has to say "we don't want you here." The rule says it for them, and says it like common sense.

So what Wiener did is not deregulation. It's pulling a bit of the interpretive power back from homeowners. SB 827 died in 2018, denounced as a declaration of war and as turning West LA into Dubai, but its failure covered the next decade of granular bills: ADU reform permitted more than 80,000 units, "if it meets zoning the city can't say no" permitting, and the builders' remedy that auto-approves big projects when cities miss their targets. Skinner says it outright in the piece: Scott's flamethrower bill gave them the window, because who pays attention to granny flats when Scott is putting a 25-story building next to you? That's weaponized expression used in the right direction—a loud doomed bill as cover for the quiet ones that land.

But the price is in the same article, just not the headline. To get to 21 votes, SB 79 baked in union protections, exempted counties with fewer than 16 high-use transit stops—so wealthy Marin and suburban Contra Costa are out—and shrank the affected radius from half a mile to a quarter mile in cities under 35,000. Every one of those is a co-conspirator's cut: to win, you hand a piece to whoever can block you. Wiener doesn't pretend otherwise. You solve what you can solve that doesn't harm the policy.

The real problem is that the policy treats housing as the only variable. Peskin's objection deserves a real answer, not because he's the opponent but because the article can't refute him: rent control, subsidized construction, interest rates, construction costs—those decide who can afford to live here, and California prices remain stubbornly high. The 2023 meta-analysis only says denser construction may decrease rents in the long term. SB 79 won't show a measurable difference for years, and Wiener admits it.

And there's a distribution question nobody asks. The movement began with young professionals who couldn't find an apartment, and its victory is legalizing market-rate apartments near transit. Market-rate units don't automatically become homes for low-income people. The guy at the fair who says he campaigned for Wiener and now can't stand him, and the elderly woman who folds him double to ask whether the new housing will be low cost, are asking the same thing. The article gives no answer. Neither does Wiener. He says: we want to build more housing.

The pitch that more housing fights racism and saves Democrats' congressional seats may be true, but it translates a question about who has a place to live into a question about party maps. That is exactly what meta-violence does best: rename a human situation as a variable of power, and everyone forgets the original question was where people live.

SB 79 broke a latch, not the wall. The wall is still there, under a more technical name, waiting for the next hearing.

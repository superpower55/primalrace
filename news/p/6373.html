反监控联盟里的枪与账单The Gun and the Bill in the Anti-Surveillance Coalition

其他 结构层 · 文化层 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
跨党派反监控不是团结，是各自被同一台机器榨过。
Bipartisan outrage isn't unity. It's one machine pressing from two sides.

西弗吉尼亚的反 Flock 运动被写成两党合作的样板：社会主义者和爱国者握手，ACLU 和自由党团站一边。这不是团结，是同一个结构暴力从两个方向压过来，压出了同一个反应。

先看钱。Monongalia 县委员会批了最多 18 万美元的阿片类药物和解金，用来付警局和 Flock 的合同（来源：Mother Jones）。这笔钱本该流向戒毒和减害服务。报道自己的调查发现，至少 12 个州的 22 个机构把超过 140 万美元的和解金花在了 Flock 和其他车牌识别器上，其中 7 个在西弗吉尼亚。阿片危机是制药公司造出来的，赔偿金又被拿去买了监控设备——受害者的钱变成了看管受害者的工具。这是结构暴力最赤裸的形态：资源的入口被改写，而改写它的人不在现场。

再看数据往哪流。DeFlock Monongalia 通过公开记录拿到的审计日志显示，2026 年前九个月，Monongalia 县的系统被 3,918 个外部机构查询了超过 300 万次，查询最多的三个执法机构都在德州，前二十名里没有一个在西弗吉尼亚。本地纳税人付钱，本地人的行踪被外州读取。这不是保护，是提取。ACLU 的 Kyle Vass 把西弗吉尼亚叫作“美国境内的提取殖民地”，这个说法准确：盐、木材、煤，现在是数据。模式没变，只是换了一种矿。

所以“两党合作”这个词在这里是廉价的。报道自己也承认，把它叫两党议题就像指着太阳说它热。真正把这群人凑到一起的不是党派，是同一个经验——被有权力的人盯着、被不在场的人拿走东西。矿工被公司保安监视，今天居民被摄像头记录，同一条线。

但这条线有一个断口，报道写得很清楚却没人追问：Chris Anders 的第一优先级是禁 Flock，同一张清单上还有阻止“堕胎药流入西弗吉尼亚”和清除学校里的“觉醒意识形态”。他反对的是政府看他，不反对政府看女人。同一个法案、同一场听证、同一张选票，把这两种“自由”绑在一起，结果就是反监控运动带进来一批人，他们的自由观只覆盖自己的院子。

更值得盯的是这些运动的落点。Jefferson 县全票通过禁令，Morgantown 有人因为砸摄像头被捕、在 Facebook 上收获 29,000 条辩护（该市人口约三万），Huntington 在七小时几乎全负的公众发言后还是签了合同，现在组织者在收集签名搞公投。热情是真的，制度回音是慢的。砸一台摄像头是直接层的动作，签一纸禁令是结构层的动作，而两者之间隔着的正是这类运动最容易耗掉的东西：时间和程序。

Aaron Hamler 那句话是整篇报道里最锋利的一处。他和妻子都是医护，收入刚好卡在 SNAP 线之上，托儿费付不起，妻子只能减工时、把班排到周末。他说人们看着原木被堆上火堆，Flock 只是点燃角落的那根火柴。这句比喻里没有意识形态，只有算术。反监控运动如果能走到这一步——从“别拍我”走到“为什么我的税钱买了拍我的东西、而我的托儿费没人管”——它才碰得到元层：不是换一批人解释规则，是问规则为什么这样写。

现在它还没走到。它停在摄像头这一层，而摄像头只是那根火柴。

West Virginia's anti-Flock movement is being sold as a bipartisan breakthrough: a socialist and a patriot shaking hands, the ACLU and the Freedom Caucus on the same side. That isn't unity. It's the same structural violence pressing from two directions until the same reflex came out.

Start with the money. The Monongalia County Commission approved up to $180,000 in opioid settlement funds to pay for the sheriff's Flock contract (source: Mother Jones). That money was meant for addiction recovery and harm reduction. The reporter's own investigation found at least 22 agencies in 12 states spending more than $1.4 million in opioid funds on Flock and other plate readers; seven of those agencies are in West Virginia. The opioid crisis was manufactured by pharmaceutical companies, and the compensation is being spent on surveillance hardware. Victims' money became the tool for watching victims. This is structural violence in its plainest form: the intake of resources got rewritten, and the people who rewrote it aren't in the room.

Then follow the data. Audit logs DeFlock Monongalia obtained through a public records request show the county's system was queried more than 3 million times by 3,918 outside organizations in the first nine months of 2026. The three agencies that queried it most were all in Texas. None of the top twenty were in West Virginia. Local taxpayers pay; local movements get read from another state. That isn't protection, it's extraction. Kyle Vass of the ACLU calls West Virginia "an extraction colony within the borders of the US," and he's right: salt, timber, coal, now data. The pattern didn't change, only the ore.

So "bipartisan" is a cheap word here. The piece admits as much—calling Flock bipartisan in West Virginia is like pointing at the sun and calling it hot. What actually assembles these people isn't a party label. It's the shared experience of being watched by people with power and having things taken by people who aren't there. Miners were spied on by company detectives; residents are logged by cameras. Same line.

But the line has a break, and the piece documents it without pressing: Chris Anders's number-one priority is banning Flock, and on the same list sit stopping "the trafficking of abortion pills into West Virginia" and ending "woke ideology" in schools. He objects to the government watching him. He does not object to the government watching women. One bill, one hearing, one ballot binds those two "freedoms" together, and the result is a movement carrying people whose idea of liberty stops at their own yard.

Watch where the momentum lands, too. Jefferson County passed a ban unanimously. In Morgantown a young man was arrested for allegedly destroying a camera and got 29,000 Facebook comments defending him, in a city of roughly 30,000. Huntington approved its contract after seven hours of almost uniformly negative public comment; organizers are now gathering signatures for a ballot initiative. The heat is real. The institutional echo is slow. Smashing a camera is a direct-layer act; passing an ordinance is a structural-layer act; between them sits exactly what these movements burn up: time and procedure.

The sharpest line in the piece belongs to Aaron Hamler. He and his wife both work in health care, earn just over the SNAP cutoff, can't afford daycare, so she cut her hospital hours and moved her shifts to weekends. He says people are watching logs stacked on a fire about to be lit, and Flock is part of the match lighting one corner. There's no ideology in that image, only arithmetic. If the anti-Flock wave gets from "don't film me" to "why did my tax money buy the thing filming me while nobody pays for my childcare," it reaches the meta layer: not swapping who interprets the rules, but asking why the rules read this way.

It hasn't gotten there. It's still standing at the camera, and the camera is only the match.

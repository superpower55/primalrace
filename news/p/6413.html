回收标志的立法保卫战The Legislative Defense of a Symbol

国际 文化层 · 结构层 ProPublica ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要美国国会两项两党法案拟为塑料回收标签设立全国标准，禁止各州制定更严格规定，其中一项认可「质量平衡」记账法，使产品几乎不含再生塑料也可标注再生含量。两案已过众议院委员会，可能在中期选举后全院表决。
The newsTwo bipartisan House bills would set national standards for plastic recycling labels and bar states from stricter rules. One endorses "mass balance" accounting, letting products claim recycled content with almost none. Both cleared committee and may face a full House vote after the midterms.
他们不造假货，他们造假"可回收"这三个字
They don't fake the product. They fake the word "recyclable."

一个三角形箭头符号，1970 年为鼓励纸张回收而诞生，1980 年代被塑料行业贴到自家产品上，用来压住公众对塑料污染的怒火。这个符号从来不是事实，是一次投放。四十年后，它要被写进联邦法律了。

ProPublica 报道的这两项众议院法案，表面上讲的是"全国统一标准""减少消费者困惑"。实际机制是：几个州开始要求诚实标注——加州的法律禁止在含毒化学品或实际上无法回收的塑料上印 chasing arrows——行业发现自己的认知入口正在被堵，于是转去联邦层面把入口重新买回来。法案禁止各州制定更严格的要求，等于把加州刚刚立起来的那面镜子砸掉。这不是标准之争，是谁有权定义"可回收"这三个字的争夺。

最能说明问题的是 mass balance 那一项。埃克森美孚在得州贝敦的化学回收厂，加州总检察长办公室的数据显示，处理的塑料废物里只有 8% 被转化成新塑料，其余 92% 大多变成燃料烧掉；诉状引用公司内部记录说，最终产品里的再生含量不到 0.1%。而公司把产品标成"30% ISCC PLUS certified-circular"。一个不到 0.1% 的东西，靠一套会计手法，在标签上变成 30%。Langworthy 的法案要做的，就是让 FTC 修改 Green Guides，把这种算法认定为"证实再生含量声明的可接受方法"——并且绕过 FTC 自己已经启动数年、收集了一千多条公众意见的修订程序。

换尺子不换能力。芯片领域如此，回收领域也如此：现实不配合，就重新定义现实。

钱的方向很干净。ExxonMobil 在 2026 年上半年为这项 mass balance 法案及其他能源环境议题花了超过 500 万美元游说；代表包装商品行业的 Consumer Brands Association 上半年花了 240 万美元；Recycling Leadership Council 花了 12 万；Ameripen 花了 16 万。Langworthy 的竞选委员会从能源行业拿到 15.7 万美元，Weber 的拿到 20.6 万美元。这些数字不证明因果，但它们标出了谁在为哪一句话付账。

另一项 PACK Act 看起来温和些：要求至少 60% 的消费者能接触到接受该产品的回收项目，产品才可印箭头。但它只要求"技术上可"再加工，不要求证明经济上可行——而报道说得很清楚，很多被收走的塑料，因为运到工厂再造成新东西不划算，最后还是进了填埋场或焚烧炉。法案还把认证权交给未指明的"认可的第三方认证机构"。加州伯克利的 Kate O'Neill 说这类机构很容易被行业利益俘获；How2Recycle 的母组织 Sustainable Packaging Coalition，成员就是零售商、品牌和包装公司。几年前 How2Recycle 还声称酸奶盒用的 5 号塑料被广泛接受回收。让被监管者指定裁判，然后宣布比赛公平。

这里没有受害者流血，但有一条完整的暴力链条在运转。文化层：一个符号被投放了四十年，让几百万吨塑料看起来像会被回收——EPA 估计 2018 年美国塑料废物回收率 8.7%，能源部 2019 年估计 5%。结构层：法案把各州要求诚实的法律架空，把认证权交给行业能影响的机构。两层咬合的方式是：先用文化叙事制造"可回收"的错觉，再用联邦法律把这个错觉固定下来，让纠正它的努力变成非法。

共谋者不分党派。PACK Act 的 19 个共同提案人里有 1 个民主党人，mass balance 法案的 13 个里有 5 个，其中 3 个来自得州——埃克森美孚的贝敦厂就在得州。这不是党派问题，是利益站队问题。行业给两党都捐钱，两党都有人替它递笔。

报道里唯一被点名反对的是 NRDC 的 Rachel Fullmer，她管这叫"peak greenwashing"。专家的共识是：塑料危机最好的解法是少生产少使用，回收充其量是边缘方案。这句话在整篇报道里出现了一次，在法案文本里出现零次。

真正的问题从来不是塑料能不能被回收，而是谁有权说它能。这两项法案如果通过，答案就定了：付钱游说的那一边。至于那些站在超市货架前、认真把酸奶盒冲干净扔进蓝色桶的人——他们不是消费者，他们是这套会计手法的原料。

A triangle of arrows, born in 1970 to encourage paper recycling, pasted onto plastic products in the 1980s to quell public outrage over plastic pollution. The symbol was never a fact. It was a deployment. Forty years later, it's about to be written into federal law.

The two House bills ProPublica reports on present themselves as "uniform national standards" that "reduce consumer confusion." The actual mechanism: a few states started demanding honest labels—California's law bans the chasing arrows on plastic containing toxic chemicals or that can't realistically be recycled—and the industry found its cognitive entry point being plugged. So it went to the federal level to buy the entry point back. The bills forbid states from setting stricter requirements, which means smashing the mirror California just held up. This isn't a standards debate. It's a fight over who gets to define the word "recyclable."

The mass balance provision is the clearest tell. At ExxonMobil's Baytown chemical recycling plant, the California attorney general's office found that only 8% of the plastic waste processed gets transformed into new plastic; the other 92% mostly becomes fuel and is burned. Citing internal records, the lawsuit states the resulting products contain less than 0.1% recycled content. The company labels them "30% ISCC PLUS certified-circular." Something under 0.1% becomes 30% on the label through an accounting trick. Langworthy's bill would require the FTC to edit the Green Guides so that this method is recognized as "an acceptable method for substantiating recycled content claims"—bypassing the FTC's own multi-year revision process, which had collected over 1,000 public comments.

Change the ruler, not the capability. Same move as in chips, same move here: when reality won't cooperate, redefine reality.

The money points one way. ExxonMobil spent over $5 million lobbying in the first half of 2026 on the mass balance bill and other energy and environmental issues. The Consumer Brands Association spent $2.4 million. The Recycling Leadership Council spent $120,000. Ameripen spent $160,000. Langworthy's campaign committee received $157,000 from the energy sector; Weber's received $206,000. None of this proves causation, but it marks who is paying for which sentence.

The PACK Act looks gentler: a product can carry the arrows only if at least 60% of consumers have access to recycling programs that accept it. But it requires only that the item be technically recyclable—not that reprocessing be economically feasible. As the reporting makes clear, much collected plastic ends up in landfills or incinerators because shipping it to a factory that will remake it doesn't pay. The bill hands certification power to unspecified "accredited third-party certification" groups. Kate O'Neill at UC Berkeley says such groups are easily captured by industry interests; How2Recycle's parent, the Sustainable Packaging Coalition, counts retailers, brands and packaging companies as members. A few years ago How2Recycle claimed No. 5 plastics were widely accepted for recycling. Let the regulated pick the referee, then declare the game fair.

No one is bleeding here, but a full violence chain is running. Cultural layer: a symbol deployed for four decades to make millions of tons of plastic look recyclable—the EPA estimated 8.7% of U.S. plastic waste was recycled in 2018; the Department of Energy put it at 5% in 2019. Structural layer: the bills override state truth-in-labeling laws and hand certification to bodies industry can influence. The two layers interlock: first manufacture the illusion through narrative, then fix the illusion in federal law so that correcting it becomes illegal.

Complicity is bipartisan. The PACK Act has 19 co-sponsors, one of them a Democrat. The mass balance bill has 13, five of them Democrats, three from Texas—where ExxonMobil's Baytown plant sits. This isn't a party problem. It's a matter of whose side you're on. Industry donates to both parties, and both parties supply the pen.

The only named opposition in the piece is Rachel Fullmer of NRDC, who calls the bills "peak greenwashing." The expert consensus is that the best solution to the plastics crisis is to make and use less; recycling is a marginal fix at best. That sentence appears once in the reporting and zero times in the bill text.

The real question was never whether plastic can be recycled. It's who gets to say it can. If these bills pass, the answer is settled: whoever pays for the lobbying. And the people standing in the supermarket aisle, rinsing out yogurt tubs and dropping them in the blue bin—they aren't consumers. They're the raw material for the accounting trick.

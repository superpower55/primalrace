她们偷偷看她，不敢关注她They Watch Her in Secret, Never Follow

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 semafor.com ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要33岁的堪萨斯城注册营养师、两个孩子的母亲Katy Hoffman以KatyinKC账号在社交媒体上质疑Amy Griffin回忆录中的受虐叙述，挖掘出匿名人物和关键音频。她的内容在纽约和洛杉矶的上流社交圈受到私下关注。
The newsKaty Hoffman, a 33-year-old dietitian and mother of two in Kansas City, has used her KatyinKC social media accounts to challenge Amy Griffin's memoir of abuse, unmasking anonymous figures and surfacing key audio. Her posts are discreetly followed by New York and LA society.
上东区的太太们偷看一个中西部女人查案，因为解释权正在搬家
Upper East Side wives lurk on a Midwest woman's records hunt because the power to explain is changing hands

纽约上东区的太太们「在听，但不关注」Katy Hoffman。这句话本身就是整条新闻最锋利的部位：一个堪萨斯城33岁的注册营养师、两个幼儿的母亲，坐在自家沙发上翻公开记录，把Amy Griffin那本畅销回忆录里的化名人物一个个挖出来，找到关键录音，然后让那些在Lanai婚礼上端着香槟的人私下里追着她的Instagram看，却不敢点那个关注按钮。

不敢关注，是因为关注是一个表达。表达就是身份的确立。在上东区那个圈子里，关注一个中西部vlogger等于公开宣布自己站到了Griffin叙事的对面，等于在社交场上投了一张票。她们要的是信息，不要的是这张票被别人看见。这就是共谋者理论里最日常的一幕：利益站队早就完成了，只是没人愿意署名。

Hoffman做的事，用书里的说法，是抢认知入口。她不是记者，没受过训练，自己说的。但她去警察局调记录，翻高中年鉴，追一场被遗忘的高中球赛，把一份法律文件里被隐去的报警录音找出来发布。专业媒体的记者反过来加她的Patreon、打电话找她交换笔记，她说自己感到「intimidated」。这个细节值得停一下：解释权不是被批判了一次，是换了一次手。一个没有编制的人拿到了发布平台，而拿编制的人来向她取材料。

但别急着把她写成英雄。她反复强调自己「只是个爱管闲事的女士」，她的主页上有时跳舞，有时替本地披萨店出头，有一条关于Ariana Grande的TikTok有720万播放。她靠内容赚的钱只够付法院文件和一点差旅，账单还是靠她的本职工作和她丈夫（工程师）的工资。她自己也说卡在十字路口，不知道要不要把这件事做大。这句话里有真东西：她现在的表达是100%有利于她自己、同时把一桩被包装过的叙事拆开给公众看，接近真.最优解。一旦「做大」，她就要开始为观众表演，为流量定价，那时她卖的就是Katy这个人设，而不是那些文件。

被拆的那一边也不是无辜的旁观者。Griffin是对冲基金大佬的妻子、畅销回忆录作者，她的故事是「表面完美的童年里的虐待」。这种叙事在出版市场上是一种资产，它需要被相信，需要读者把同情投进去。Hoffman挖出来的化名、录音、年鉴，动的是这份资产的价格。纽约那群人之所以恼火，不是因为她说错了，是因为她把定价权从作者和出版社手里拿走了一部分。

最诚实的一句来自Hoffman自己：让那些纽约人把对她的原话录下来，她要一条条撕回去。而采访他的记者说，他的纽约消息源没有一个愿意具名，只有一个人要求强调，那些带着阶级意味的嘲笑是「带着爱」说的。「带着爱」——这是文化暴力最标准的外壳，把蔑视包装成亲昵，把居高临下包装成玩笑，然后要求被嘲笑的人领情。

这件事里被削掉的差额是具体的：一份被隐去的报警录音重新出现在公众面前，几个化名人物被对上号，一群习惯了自己定价自己的人发现有人不按他们的价目表说话。但差额没有削完的部分也清楚：Hoffman没有编制、没有法务、没有编辑，她拿到未删节录音的方式不肯说，她随时可能被起诉、被封号、被平台改规则掐掉。她替公众做了新闻业该做的工作，代价和风险全落在她一个人身上，而那些加她Patreon的专业记者，拿走材料，回去领工资。

"Listening, but not following." That phrase, applied to Katy Hoffman, is the sharpest part of this whole story. A 33-year-old registered dietitian and mother of two in Kansas City, working from her sofa, digs through public records, unmasks the pseudonymous characters in Amy Griffin's bestselling memoir, finds the key audio, and gets the women who were at that wedding on Lanai to follow her work in private while refusing to press the follow button.

They won't follow because following is an expression, and expression establishes who you are. In that Upper East Side circle, following a Midwest vlogger is a public declaration that you stand against Griffin's narrative — a vote cast where everyone can see it. They want the information. They don't want the vote to be visible. This is the co-conspirator mechanism at its most mundane: the alignment of interest is already complete; only the signature is missing.

What Hoffman is doing is seizing a cognitive entry point. She isn't a journalist — no training, her own words. But she drives to the police station for court records, digs through high school yearbooks and a forgotten high school sporting event, finds a redacted police audio file referenced in a legal filing and posts it, then posts the unredacted version without saying how she got it. Reporters from the New York Times and the Wall Street Journal sign up for her Patreon and call her to swap notes; she says she feels "intimidated." Sit with that: the power to explain didn't just get criticized once — it changed hands. Someone without a masthead got a publishing platform, and people with mastheads come to her for material.

Don't rush to make her a hero. She keeps saying she's "just a nosy lady." Her feed has dancing, a defense of a local pizza joint, a TikTok about Ariana Grande with 7.2 million views. The money from her content covers court documents and a little travel; the bills are still paid by her day job and her husband, an engineer. She says she's stuck at a crossroads, unsure whether to build this into something bigger. There's something real in that sentence: her current expression is 100% good for her and, at the same time, pries open a packaged narrative for the public — close to a true optimal expression. The moment she "builds it," she starts performing for an audience, pricing herself for traffic, and what she sells becomes the Katy persona rather than the documents.

The other side isn't an innocent bystander either. Griffin is the wife of a hedge-funder and the author of a bestselling memoir — a story of abuse inside a superficially perfect childhood. That narrative is an asset in the publishing market. It needs to be believed; it needs readers to invest their sympathy. The pseudonyms, the audio, the yearbooks — Hoffman is moving the price of that asset. The New York crowd isn't angry because she's wrong. They're angry because she took part of the pricing power away from the author and the publisher.

The most honest line is Hoffman's own: put the New Yorkers' quotes about her on the record so she can rip them apart. The reporter's sources all declined to be named; one asked him to stress that any class-inflected mockery was delivered "with love." With love — the standard casing of cultural violence, dressing contempt as affection, condescension as a joke, and then demanding the mocked party be grateful.

The gap this closes is concrete: a redacted police audio file is back in public view, several pseudonymous characters are matched to real people, and a group accustomed to pricing itself discovers someone who won't read from their price list. The part left unclosed is just as clear: Hoffman has no masthead, no legal team, no editor. She won't say how she obtained the unredacted audio. She can be sued, banned, or cut off by a platform rule change at any time. She did the work journalism is supposed to do, and the cost and the risk land on her alone — while the professionals who join her Patreon take the material and go collect their salaries.

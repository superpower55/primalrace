谁在给十四岁倒酒Who Pours for the Fourteen-Year-Old

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 The Cut ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
新闻摘要一位家长就青少年聚会问题采访了多位家长，受访者讲述了孩子使用蘑菇、大麻和酒精的经历，以及家长如何应对。报道提到，在部分富裕郊区，有家长在青少年聚会上提供酒精；也有家长用定位追踪孩子。
The newsA parent interviewed other parents about teen partying, hearing stories of teens using mushrooms, cannabis and alcohol and how parents responded. Some affluent suburban parents provide alcohol at teen parties, and some track their teens' locations.
父母用“信任”换来的，是把自己的监管权让渡给了市场。
Parents trade away their authority for the feeling of being trusted.

这篇报道里最诚实的一句话，是那位康涅狄格妈妈说的：有些家长在孩子的派对上供酒，是“想显得酷、显得宽容”。一个十四岁的女孩要去同学家，同学的妈妈打电话来问，能不能给每个女孩发一罐 High Noon。这不是疏忽，这是一次定价：用一罐酒，买一个“开明家长”的身份。

酒和蘑菇、大麻在这里不是同一个东西。蘑菇和大麻是孩子从外面弄来的，父母是被叫去救场的那个；酒是父母自己端上桌的。报道里那位西南部的妈妈说，高年级的派对有 open bar，她觉得“怪异”，但她自己的后院也允许喝酒——理由是“反正他们总能弄到”，只要没人开车，她“不想知道是怎么弄到的”。这句话把整件事说穿了：家长不是不知道，是选择不知道。不知道，就可以继续当一个被信任的大人。

于是“信任”变成了这场博弈里最贵的筹码。每个受访的家长都说，不能骂、不能罚，否则孩子下次就不打电话了。这个逻辑本身没错，凌晨一点把嗑蘑菇的朋友塞进车里送回家，比什么都重要。但它有一个没被说出来的前提：孩子手里的东西，是市场给的，不是家长给的，家长能做的只剩下事后接电话。那位蒙特利尔的爸爸看到儿子和同学举着电子烟、一瓶伏特加和疑似处方药的截图，膝盖发软，谈完话之后开始定位儿子的手机——他自己也说，这不像他。

报道里所有家长共享一个假设：他们大致知道孩子在干什么。作者自己也承认，这是“不知道自己不知道”的传统。那个高二整年抽大麻、妈妈毫无察觉的男孩，那个用大麻自我治疗了一整年抑郁、妈妈因为“没有味道”而迟迟没发现的女孩，都是这个假设的反面。而孩子的获取渠道是 Snapchat，是“真的很容易买到”。家长在讨论要不要给十四岁倒酒的时候，另一套供应系统早就不经过他们了。

这篇报道里没有一个人问：这些孩子为什么需要把自己弄到失去意识。派对的社交压力、谁更“放得开”的排名、第二天醒来听到的关于自己“messy”“癫痫”的夸张传言——这些是青少年在自己那套存在性战争里付的代价，而家长能提供的全部工具，是一罐酒、一个定位、一次不追究的接送。那位戒了酒的四孩妈妈说，新年夜一个高一女孩喝得烂醉，通过朋友知道可以打给她。她感觉很好。这确实是这篇报道里最暖的一段，也是最凉的一段：一个成年人的可用性，成了这套系统里唯一的缓冲。

报道结尾说，家长唯一能控制的是自己怎么反应。不对。他们还能控制的是要不要在自己家里开那个 open bar。把酒端给十四岁，再告诉自己这叫信任，是把“显得酷”的成本记在了孩子头上——账单要到很久以后才寄到，而寄件人不会写家长的名字。

The most honest line in this piece comes from the Connecticut mother: some parents supply alcohol at their teens' parties because they want to seem cool, seem permissive. A fourteen-year-old girl is heading to a friend's house, and the friend's mother calls to ask whether it's okay to hand each girl a can of High Noon. That isn't an oversight. It's a price: one can of alcohol for the identity of the open-minded parent.

Alcohol, mushrooms, and weed are not the same thing here. The mushrooms and the weed come from somewhere outside; the parents are the ones called in to clean up. The alcohol is what the parents themselves put on the table. The mother in the suburban Southwest says senior parties have open bars and she finds it bizarre, yet her own backyard allows drinking, on the grounds that they somehow acquire it anyway, and she doesn't want to know how. That sentence gives the whole game away: the parent isn't uninformed, she has chosen not to be informed. Not knowing lets her keep being the trusted adult.

So trust becomes the most expensive chip on the table. Every parent interviewed says the same thing: you can't scold, you can't punish, or the kid stops calling. The logic isn't wrong. Getting a friend stoned on mushrooms into a car at one in the morning matters more than anything. But it rests on an unspoken premise: what the kids have in their hands came from the market, not from the parents, and all the parents have left is answering the phone afterward. The Montreal father sees a screenshot of his fourteen-year-old posing with a vape, a mickey of vodka, and what looks like prescription pills; his knees go weak, he has a long talk, and then he starts tracking his son's location. He says himself this isn't like him.

Every parent in the piece shares one assumption: that they have some sense of what their teen is doing. The author admits this is the proud tradition of not knowing what you don't know. The boy who was high for months on end during sophomore year, and the girl who self-medicated a year of depression with cannabis while her mother, noticing no telltale wafts, stayed slow to detect it — both are the reverse of that assumption. The supply chain runs through Snapchat, and it is really available. While the parents debate whether to pour for a fourteen-year-old, the other supply system stopped routing through them long ago.

Nobody in this piece asks why these kids need to get themselves to the point of losing consciousness. The social pressure of the party, the ranking of who is more fun, the exaggerated rumors the morning after about being messy, about seizures — these are the costs teenagers pay in their own existential war, and the entire toolkit their parents can offer is a can of alcohol, a location tracker, and a pickup with no questions asked. The sober mother of four says a freshman girl got drunk off her ass on New Year's Eve and knew, through friends, to call her. It made her feel good. It is the warmest passage in the piece, and the coldest: one adult's availability as the only buffer in the system.

The piece ends by saying the only thing parents can control is how they react. Wrong. They can also control whether they open that bar in their own house. Handing a drink to a fourteen-year-old and calling it trust puts the cost of seeming cool on the child's tab — and the bill arrives much later, with no parent's name on the sender line.

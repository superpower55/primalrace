城市翻倍，机会给谁Doubling Cities, Priced for Whom

国际 结构层 · 文化层 semafor.com ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要世界银行新报告称，撒哈拉以南非洲城市必须投资城市规划，为快速增长的人口创造就业。非洲城市人口预计到2050年接近翻倍至17亿，增长主要由罗安达、拉各斯和金沙萨带动。报告称拉各斯等城市基础设施缺口是吸引私人投资的「重要机会」。
The newsA new World Bank report says sub-Saharan African cities must invest in urban planning to create jobs for fast-growing populations. Africa's urban population is projected to nearly double to 1.7 billion by 2050, led by Luanda, Lagos and Kinshasa. The report calls the infrastructure deficit an "important opportunity" to attract private investment.
「私人投资的机会」是这份报告里唯一被定价的东西。
The only thing this report prices is the "opportunity" itself.

世界银行这份报告里，17亿是一个数字，不是人。报告从头到尾只算了一件事：多少人会涌进城市，以及这些人的涌入能撬动多少私人资本。罗安达、拉各斯、金沙萨——三座城市被点名，不是因为那里的居民需要什么，是因为那里的地还没开发完。

「基础设施缺口是吸引私人投资的重要机会」，这句话值得逐字读。缺口是缺在谁身上？缺在每天挤不上通勤车的人身上，缺在没有稳定供电的诊所里，缺在被洪水淹一次就搬一次家的街区里。报告把这份匮乏翻译成了「机会」，翻译完，匮乏的承受者就从主语变成了背景。加尔通的公式在这里很好用：Violence = Potential − Actual，而这份报告讨论的是差额怎么变现，不是差额怎么补上。

更值得盯的是「城市规划」这个词的归属。谁规划，规划给谁，规划完之后谁有权住进去。拉各斯要修高效的交通，修完之后票价定在多少，沿线的非正式聚落清到哪里去——这些决定一旦落笔，就是几十年的结构暴力，而且是那种事后无法追责的结构暴力，因为它在纸面上叫「现代化」。报告里没有一句话提到现在住在那片「欠发达土地」上的人。土地被描述为欠发达，住在上面的人就顺理成章地成了待开发的一部分。

至于「为快速增长的人口创造就业」——就业从来不是规划出来的，是资本决定在哪里落地之后剩下的东西。城市规划能做的是让资本落地更便宜：路修好、地平整好、审批简化好。这份成本由公共财政出，收益由私人投资收。这套安排在非洲不是第一次上演，报告自己也知道，所以它用的是「机会」而不是「承诺」。

17亿人里有一半是女性。她们在这份报告里连一个统计口径都没有——没有托育、没有通勤安全、没有非正式就业中的女性占比。城市人口翻倍，意味着再生产劳动翻倍，而这份劳动在报告的成本核算里等于零。这不算疏漏，这是元暴力的常规操作：把解释权攥在手里的人，定义什么算基础设施，什么不算。

所以真正的问题不是非洲城市要不要规划，而是这份规划的第一页写的是谁的名字。报告给出的答案很清楚：写的是投资人的。至于那17亿人，他们在这份文件里的位置，是分母。

In this World Bank report, 1.7 billion is a number, not people. The document computes exactly one thing: how many bodies will pour into cities, and how much private capital that inflow can leverage. Luanda, Lagos, Kinshasa are named not because their residents need something, but because their land is not yet developed.

Read the key phrase slowly: the infrastructure deficit is an "important opportunity" to attract private investment. A deficit on whom? On the people who cannot board a commuter train, on the clinics without steady power, on the neighborhoods that relocate every time the flood comes. The report translates that lack into "opportunity," and once translated, those who bear the lack drop from subject to backdrop. Galtung's formula fits: Violence = Potential − Actual. This report discusses how to monetize the gap, not how to close it.

Watch the ownership of the phrase "urban planning." Who plans, for whom, and who gets to live there afterward. Lagos wants efficient transport; at what fare, and where do the informal settlements along the line get cleared to? Once drafted, those decisions become decades of structural violence — the kind nobody can be held accountable for later, because on paper it is called modernization. Not one sentence mentions the people currently living on that "underdeveloped land." Call land underdeveloped and its inhabitants become part of the development site.

As for "creating jobs for the rapidly growing population" — jobs are not planned into existence; they are what remains after capital decides where to land. Urban planning's real job is making that landing cheaper: roads laid, land graded, permits streamlined. The public purse pays the cost; private investment collects the return. This arrangement has run in Africa before, and the report knows it, which is why it says "opportunity" and not "promise."

Half of those 1.7 billion are women. They do not appear in this report even as a statistical category — no childcare, no transit safety, no share of informal employment. Doubling the urban population doubles reproductive labor, and that labor is valued at zero in the cost accounting. Not an oversight: standard meta-violence. Whoever holds the interpretive monopoly decides what counts as infrastructure.

So the question is not whether African cities need planning. It is whose name goes on page one. The report answers plainly: the investor's. The 1.7 billion are the denominator.

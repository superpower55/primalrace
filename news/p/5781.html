换尺子不换能力，这次换的是出口清单A New Ruler, Same Old Game

中国科技 结构层 · 文化层 Reuters ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
产业升级的叙事里，没有人问代价记在谁账上
The upgrade story never asks who pays the bill

路透这篇稿子的写法很讲究：先给你看电动SUV、机器人和碳纤维，再轻轻放一句“如果他们在美德工厂上班，工作可能不保”。这句话是全篇唯一提到人的地方，而且被放在第三段，之后再也没有回来。

这不是疏忽，是结构。整篇报道的计量单位是出口额、市场份额、增速百分比，中国芯片出口上半年接近翻倍到1772.8亿美元，汽车出口自2015年以来涨了1871%，工业机器人出口涨了300%以上。这些数字都是真的，也都能查到。但数字只记录了一件事：谁把东西卖出去了。它不记录谁因此失去了什么。

报道自己给出了机制：中国化工企业过去十年拿了约750亿美元补贴，2023年峰值约150亿；2018到2023年新设约5000家化工公司，产能过剩，工厂闲置，库存被推向海外市场。这段话写得很清楚，补贴制造了产能，产能制造了过剩，过剩制造了低价，低价制造了“受欢迎”。然后报道说，对全世界数百万人来说，这意味着便宜、现代、想要的产品。

便宜从来不是免费的。差额被转移了，没有消失。中国工厂的工人、被挤垮的德国和意大利机械厂工人、去年出口额从10亿美元掉到7.46亿美元的美国割草机行业，都是这个差额的承担方。报道提到了他们，但只提到一次，用“工作可能不保”五个字打包。

更值得看的是报道对“升级”的处理。它把“从T恤玩具到电动车芯片”写成一条上升曲线，好像价值链条是一条自动扶梯。但报道里其实埋着反证：中国车便宜是因为“太多了”，是因为国内卖不掉，厂家被迫去海外找买家。这不是需求拉动的升级，是产能推出去的倾销。欧洲对中国割草机发起反倾销调查，理由是低于本地厂商约40%；美国和欧洲给中国车设贸易壁垒。这些不是“竞争力太强”的自然结果，是结构暴力在跨国层面的具体形态：一方用国家补贴压低成本，另一方用关税挡住，两边都不问工厂里的人怎么样了。

报道还引了Clarkson骂比亚迪海豹6是“带雨刷的中国墙纸胶”。这个细节很有意思，它暴露了旧的中心叙事：中国货等于廉价劣质。现在欧洲消费者用销量投票，Leapmotor在欧洲上半年涨了558%，比亚迪涨了146%。旧叙事被数据打脸了。但打脸之后呢？新叙事是“中国制造等于高科技高质量”，还是同一套换尺子游戏：以前用“低端”定义中国，现在用“高端”定义中国，定义权还在出口额手里，人的处境还是不在计量单位里。

这篇报道没有写任何一个人的名字。没有中国工厂工人的名字，没有德国机械厂被裁员工的名字，没有美国割草机行业失业者的名字。它写的是“工厂”“工人”“就业”，全是复数，全是没有面孔的类别。这不是路透的独家问题，是经济报道的通用语法：把结构暴力翻译成增长率，把代价翻译成“可能”。

所以这篇稿子真正告诉我们的，不是中国出口变强了。是“变强”这个说法本身，已经变成了一把尺子，量的是出口额，不是人。换尺子不换能力，这套操作在芯片领域叫τ law，在贸易领域叫产业升级，在新闻里叫“欢迎来到新的中国制造”。

欢迎之后，谁在买单，报道没写。

Reuters frames this piece carefully: show the electric SUVs, the robots, the carbon fibre, then slip in one line about workers in US and German factories whose jobs may be at risk. That line is the only place people appear, and it sits in the third paragraph, never to return.

That is not an oversight. It is the structure. The unit of measurement throughout is export value, market share, growth percentage: Chinese chip shipments nearly doubled in the first half of 2026 to $177.28 billion, car exports up 1,871% since 2015, industrial robot exports up more than 300%. These numbers are real and checkable. But they record only one thing: who sold what. They do not record who lost what.

The piece gives us the mechanism itself: Chinese chemical firms received about $75 billion in subsidies over the past decade, peaking at roughly $15 billion in 2023; some 5,000 new chemical companies were established between 2018 and 2023, creating a glut, forcing factories to idle, pushing inventories onto overseas markets. That paragraph is clear. Subsidies made capacity, capacity made surplus, surplus made low prices, low prices made the products "desirable." Then the piece says: for millions of people around the world, that means cheap, modern, desirable products.

Cheap is never free. The difference gets moved, not erased. Chinese factory workers, workers in the German and Italian machinery plants being outcompeted, the US lawnmower sector whose shipments fell from $1 billion in 2023 to $746 million last year: they are all carrying that difference. The piece mentions them once, packed into five words: jobs may be at risk.

The more telling move is how "upgrading" is handled. It is drawn as an ascending curve from t-shirts and toys to EVs and chips, as if the value chain were an escalator. But the piece contains its own counter-evidence: Chinese cars are cheap because there are too many of them, because they cannot be sold at home, because manufacturers were forced to look overseas for buyers. That is not demand pulling an upgrade; it is surplus being pushed out as dumping. Brussels opened an anti-dumping investigation into Chinese lawnmowers after a local producer complained of undercutting by some 40%; the US and Europe have erected trade barriers against Chinese cars. These are not the natural results of "being too competitive." They are structural violence at the transnational level: one side uses state subsidies to suppress costs, the other uses tariffs to block, and neither asks what happens to the people inside the factories.

The piece also quotes Clarkson calling the BYD Seal 6 "Chinese wallpaper paste with windscreen wipers." That detail is useful: it exposes the old central narrative, Chinese goods equal cheap and shoddy. Now European drivers are voting with sales, Leapmotor up 558% in Europe in the first half of this year, BYD up 146%. The old narrative has been slapped by data. But what replaces it? A new narrative that "Made in China" means high-tech, high-quality? Or the same ruler-swapping game: yesterday "low-end" defined China, today "high-end" defines China, and the power to define still sits with export value, while human conditions still sit outside the unit of measurement.

The piece names no individual. No Chinese factory worker, no laid-off German machinist, no unemployed American lawnmower worker. It writes "factories," "workers," "jobs," all plural, all faceless categories. This is not Reuters' unique problem; it is the standard grammar of economic reporting: translate structural violence into growth rates, translate cost into "may."

So what this piece actually tells us is not that Chinese exports got stronger. It is that "getting stronger" has itself become a ruler, measuring export value, not people. Swap the ruler, keep the capacity: in chips this operation is called the τ law, in trade it is called industrial upgrading, in journalism it is called "Welcome to the new Made in China."

After the welcome, who pays? The piece does not say.

一场初选输赢，包装成反犹审判A Primary Loss Repackaged as a Trial for Antisemitism

国际 文化层 · 元暴力 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
被背叛的不是党，是被拿来当武器的叙事
What got betrayed wasn't the party. It was the narrative being used as a weapon.

Stevens 背书 Rogers，真正值得看的不是"民主党人倒戈"这出戏，而是她用"反犹"这把刀，把一场初选失利重新裁定成了道德审判。

她输给了 El-Sayed，输在 Wayne County 和 Oakland County 之外的党内版图，输在 AIPAC 关联的超级 PAC 砸下数千万美元也没能替她买下提名。输了就该认。她周三还在说希望 El-Sayed 赢，周日就站到 Rogers 身边——中间隔着的不是新证据，是一个她不被邀请参加的教堂活动。

Rebellion PAC 那条把鸡蛋和"杀害巴勒斯坦人"并置的广告，确实恶劣。但 El-Sayed 的竞选团队没有制作它，他本人也明确表示不认同其信息。Stevens 抓住这条第三方广告，要求候选人替别人的广告认罪，再把"他暗示军援以色列的钱可以转投学校、医疗、育儿"也算成同一桩罪——这不是在追究言论，是在制造一个不可辩驳的标签。标签一旦贴上，辩论就结束了。

这就是表达武器化的标准动作：夺取"谁在仇恨"的定义权，然后让定义权替自己赢回初选输掉的东西。她指责 El-Sayed 与 Hasan Piker 有关联，Piker 的舅舅 Cenk Uygur 创办了发布广告的 PAC——这条链子绕得够远，远到可以只保留"关联"两个字，把中间的逻辑全部省略。

Schumer 说这是"卑劣的背叛"，然后补了一句"我其实不太想 Haley Stevens"。这句话比谴责更说明问题：党内高层不打算把这当成路线之争，只当成一次需要尽快处理的公关事故。她的幕僚长 Justin German 干了八年，带着几名同事辞职——共谋者理论在这里要反过来用一次：不是所有站队都为了分赃，有人拒绝站队，代价是丢掉工作。

真正被消音的是密歇根选民关心的东西。Schumer 自己把话题拨回医疗、民权、和 Rogers 的政策差别——Rogers 背后是 Trump，Trump 在 Truth Social 上把这次背书称作"勇气"。一个民主党现任议员把参议院一票投给 Trump 背书的候选人，而公共讨论的焦点停在"她是不是反犹"和"她是不是叛徒"之间来回弹。

报道里没有写 Stevens 的背书会不会真的改变投票结果。路透的民调显示 El-Sayed 基本整合了民主党支持，提前投票 10 月 24 日就开始了，早于这场风波。也就是说，这场戏最可能的结局是：它改变不了参议院的归属，只改变了一件事——把"批评以色列政策"和"反犹"之间的等号，又焊紧了一道。

El-Sayed 如果赢了，赢的是席位；那道等号还在。

Stevens endorsing Rogers isn't the story. The story is that she took the word "antisemitism" and used it to re-try a primary she already lost — this time as a moral tribunal.

She lost to El-Sayed. She lost despite an AIPAC-affiliated super PAC spending tens of millions of dollars to back her. Losing is losing. On Wednesday she still said she wanted him to win. By Sunday she was standing next to Rogers — and what happened in between wasn't new evidence. It was a church service she wasn't invited to.

The Rebellion PAC ad that put a carton of eggs next to "killing Palestinians" is genuinely ugly. But El-Sayed's campaign didn't make it, and he said he disagreed with its message. Stevens seized on a third-party ad, demanded the candidate answer for someone else's words, then folded his argument that money spent on Israel could go to schools, healthcare and childcare into the same charge. That isn't accountability. That's manufacturing a label that can't be argued with. Once the label sticks, the debate is over.

This is the standard move in weaponized expression: seize the power to define who is guilty of hate, then let that definition win back what the primary took away. She cited El-Sayed's association with Hasan Piker, whose uncle Cenk Uygur founded the PAC that ran the ad — a chain long enough that you can keep only the word "association" and drop every link in between.

Schumer called it a "despicable betrayal," then added that he doesn't really think about Haley Stevens. That line says more than the condemnation: party leadership isn't treating this as a fight over direction, just a PR incident to be cleaned up fast. Her chief of staff of eight years, Justin German, resigned along with several colleagues. Run the co-conspirator theory backward here: not every alignment is for spoils. Sometimes refusing to align costs you your job.

What got buried is what Michigan voters actually care about. Schumer himself tried to steer back to healthcare, civil rights, and the policy gap with Rogers — who is backed by Trump. Trump celebrated the endorsement on Truth Social as "bravery." A sitting Democratic congresswoman is giving her Senate vote to Trump's candidate, and the public argument stays stuck between "is she antisemitic" and "is she a traitor."

The report doesn't say whether the endorsement will change the outcome. Reuters polling suggests El-Sayed has largely consolidated Democratic support, and early voting began October 24, before the dispute erupted. Which means the most likely result of this whole spectacle is that it changes nothing about who controls the Senate. It changes one thing: it welds the equals sign between "criticizing Israeli policy" and "antisemitism" a little tighter.

If El-Sayed wins, he wins a seat. The equals sign stays.

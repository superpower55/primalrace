游戏大奖的选票，不是靠玩出来的The Game Awards Is a Ballot, Not a Verdict

文娱 文化层 Polygon.com ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
GOTY 提名是投票机制的产物，不是游戏好坏的裁决。
A GOTY nomination is the product of a voting mechanism, not a judgment of quality.

Polygon 这篇不是在评游戏，是在算票。88、89 的评分、Steam 上的人数、陪审团里中国只有三票、从捷克到墨西哥到沙特的评委口味——通篇是选举学，最后落一句"对游戏文化大概是好事"。好事在哪，没说。

"外来的 AAA"（outsider AAA）这个词造得挺妙。它把中欧、东亚的工作室打包成一个身份，然后给这个身份配一套人设：不妥协的审美、老派的"玩家的游戏"、被某类反动的玩家热情拥护、但又能靠画面和技术打进大众。这套人设是给评委看的，也是给读者看的。身份在这里不是描述，是选战里的定位。

第三章讲的就是这个：谁能定义"什么是好游戏"，谁就掌握了认知入口。Polygon 自己承认，Wukong 拿到提名靠的不是评分（它是 Metacritic 上提名作品里评分最低的），而是人气、电影化制作、以及陪审团的国际分布。评分是尺子，投票是另一把尺子，两把尺子量出来的东西不一样，但最后只有一个奖。

最有意思的是 Crimson Desert 那一段。77 分，按作者自己的说法"本该立刻出局"，但它靠更新节奏和粉丝的声量硬撑成了"凤凰叙事"。也就是说，一个游戏能不能留在牌桌上，取决于它能不能持续制造话题，而不是它做得好不好。这是注意力经济的标准操作，和作品质量是两条线。

所以 Phantom Blade Zero"取消"了 Crimson Desert，不是因为更好，是因为它占的是同一个席位、同一批评委、同一种人设，而它的口碑更干净、故事更聚焦、还甩掉了 Game Science 那位创始人的政治包袱。这是同一选区里的两个候选人，一个把另一个挤掉了。

报道里没有玩家在承受什么代价，也没有一个值得拆穿的假叙事——它自己就把机制摊开了：这是排位，不是评判。真要挑，挑的是那句"新声音、新的国际开发场景，我们都该支持"。支持谁、怎么支持、支持完了谁付钱，文章没写。奖项是生意的一部分，评委是生意的一部分，读者也是。

那三票中国评委的票，和从捷克到沙特那些评委的票，权重是一样的。这才是这篇报道真正说清楚的事：在这个体系里，声音的大小不取决于你做了什么样的游戏，取决于你在陪审团里坐了几个位子。

This Polygon piece isn't reviewing games. It's counting votes. Scores of 88 and 89, Steam player numbers, three Chinese votes on the jury, jury tastes from the Czech Republic to Mexico to Saudi Arabia—the whole thing is election studies, capped with "that's probably a good thing for game culture." Good how? It doesn't say.

"Outsider AAA" is a clever coinage. It bundles studios in Central Europe and East Asia into one identity, then assigns that identity a persona: uncompromising aesthetics, old-school "gamer's games," passionate support from a certain breed of reactionary gamer, yet broad reach thanks to lush visuals and technical prowess. That persona is written for the jury and for the reader. Identity here isn't description. It's campaign positioning.

This is Chapter 3 exactly: whoever defines "what a good game is" controls the cognitive entry point. Polygon itself admits Wukong's nomination didn't rest on reviews—it's the lowest-rated game ever nominated—but on popularity, cinematic production values, and the jury's international spread. Scores are one ruler, votes are another. Two rulers, one prize.

The Crimson Desert passage is the tell. A 77, which by the author's own logic "should have immediately ruled it out," clung to relevance through an aggressive update schedule and a No Man's Sky-style phoenix narrative. A game stays at the table by manufacturing continued buzz, not by being good. That's attention economics, a separate track from quality.

So Phantom Blade Zero "cancels" Crimson Desert not because it's better, but because it occupies the same seat, the same jurors, the same persona—with a cleaner reputation, a more focused story, and none of the political baggage of Game Science's founder. Two candidates in one district; one knocks out the other.

No player bears a cost in this story, and there's no fake narrative worth unmasking—the piece lays the mechanism bare itself: this is a ranking, not a judgment. The one line worth picking at is "new voices and new international development scenes ought to be something we can all get behind." Behind whom, how, and who pays—not written. The awards are part of the business, the jurors are part of the business, the reader is too.

Three Chinese jury votes and the votes from Prague to Riyadh carry equal weight. That's what the piece actually makes clear: in this system, the size of your voice doesn't depend on what kind of game you made. It depends on how many seats you hold on the jury.

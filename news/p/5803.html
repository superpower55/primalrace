把厌女送进公共卫生科Send Misogyny to the Public Health Clinic

性别 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
把商业模式说成流行病，是给共谋者换一件白大褂。
Calling a business model an epidemic is just handing the co-conspirators a lab coat.

把 manosphere 定性成公共卫生问题，等于把一门生意转诊进了疾控中心。报告自己写得很清楚：这是一个 diffuse commercial ecosystem，靠售卖"最极端的厌女表达"和"委屈的男性气质"赚钱。既然是生意，那它就该归商业监管管，归反垄断管，归消费者保护管。定性成"公共健康"，听起来很严肃，实际上是把一桩有卖家、有定价、有利润的掠夺行为，改写成一场所有人都在承受的天气。天气没有被告。

Buse 自己把剧本念了出来：烟草那套打法——怪个体，把监管说成侵犯自由。他说得对，但他只说了前半段。烟草那套完整打法是：先制造需求，再让受害者自己买单，最后让国家出钱治肺癌。现在 manosphere 走的是同一条路，只是账单换了个科目——从医疗系统挪到了女性的身体和安全上。公共卫生的账本里从来没有"被网暴的女性"这一栏。

报告最诚实的一句话是"男孩和男人不是问题"，紧接着是"问题是让他们孤立、没有稳定未来的处境，以及从他们的愤怒里赚钱的公司"。前半句是对的，后半句只对了一半：从愤怒里赚钱的不只是那些卖课的男人。一个男孩在屏幕前学会把女性当成需要攻略的对象，他在现实里的每一次搭讪、每一次约会、每一段婚姻，都有人在其中获得顺从、免费劳动和孩子。线上是内容，线下是制度。只查内容不碰制度，等于只关掉广告牌，不拆工厂。

Hawkes 那句"只移除 manosphere 而不提供替代品，会失败"，是整篇报道里最该被追问的一句。什么算"更好的东西"？如果替代品是另一套更温和的、仍然以"帮男孩找回男子气概"为名的课程，那不过是把假.最优解表达换了个包装，主体性照样交出去。真正能替代的，是让男孩不必靠扮演"强者"来换取存在感的社会条件——而那要动的是就业、教育、住房，是结构层，不是内容审核层。

更值得盯住的是这个定性本身在保护谁。公共卫生的语言天然要求"协调响应"：政府、学校、医疗服务、社区组织一起上。这套机器一旦启动，最先被纳入监测的往往是"高风险个体"——也就是那些被判定为容易被煽动的男孩。他们会被建档、被干预、被辅导，而制造这些内容、从中抽成的公司，最多收到一份"建议加强自律"。元暴力在这里完成了一次漂亮的换手：解释权从"男性中心叙事"移交给"公共健康叙事"，垄断者没变，只是学会了用流行病学的词汇说话。

Brooks 说"不是所有男孩都同等程度地接触这些内容"，这话没错，但他所在的机构正在研究"谁最危险"。研究风险人群是公共卫生的标准动作，可风险从来不是均匀分布的：孤独、失学、缺钱，这些条件是谁制造的？报告把它们列成背景，却不肯把它们列成被告。

所以这份报告真正做的事，是把一个结构问题翻译成一个行为问题，再把行为问题翻译成一个需要被管理的群体问题。翻译过程中，那些靠厌女收租的商业模式拿到了最想要的东西：一个中立的、科学的、听起来很人道的名字。下一次再有女性在网上被围猎，她得到的不会是一份判决，而是一份风险提示。

Classifying the manosphere as a public health issue moves a business into the epidemiology department. The report itself says it plainly: this is a diffuse commercial ecosystem that makes money by promoting "the most extreme expressions of misogyny and aggrieved masculinity." If it is a business, it belongs to commercial regulation, antitrust, consumer protection. Calling it public health sounds serious, but what it actually does is rewrite a predatory transaction—with sellers, prices, and profits—as weather that everyone happens to be standing in. Weather has no defendants.

Buse recites the playbook himself: blame the individual, call regulation an attack on freedom. He is right, but only halfway. The full tobacco playbook is: manufacture the demand, make the victims pay, then have the state fund the lung cancer. The manosphere runs the same route with one accounting change—the bill moves from the health system to women's bodies and safety. There has never been a line item for "women who got mobbed online" in a public health ledger.

The most honest sentence in the report is "boys and men are not the problem," followed by "the problem is the conditions that leave them isolated and without a secure future, and the companies that profit from their anger and insecurity." The first half is right. The second half is half right: the companies profiting from that anger are not only the ones selling courses. A boy who learns on a screen to treat women as targets to be gamed carries that into every approach, every date, every marriage—and someone collects compliance, unpaid labor, and children from it. Online is the content; offline is the institution. Regulating content without touching the institution is turning off the billboard and leaving the factory running.

Hawkes's line—that removing the manosphere without offering something credible in its place will fail—is the one that deserves the hardest follow-up. What counts as better? If the substitute is another, gentler curriculum for "helping boys reclaim masculinity," it is just the fake optimal expression in new packaging, and subjectivity gets handed over again. The only real substitute is a society where boys don't have to perform "the strong one" to earn existence—and that means jobs, schools, housing: the structural layer, not the content-moderation layer.

What the framing protects is worth watching. Public health language demands a "coordinated response": government, schools, health services, community organizations. Once that machine starts, the first people pulled into surveillance are usually "at-risk individuals"—the boys judged susceptible. They get files, interventions, counseling. The companies that make the content and take the cut get, at most, a recommendation to self-regulate. Meta-violence performs a clean handoff here: explanatory authority moves from the masculine-centric narrative to the public health narrative. The monopoly is unchanged; it has simply learned to speak in epidemiological vocabulary.

Brooks says not all boys engage with this content equally, which is true, but his organization is now researching who is most at risk. Risk profiling is standard public health practice, but risk is never evenly distributed: loneliness, dropping out, money worries—who produced those conditions? The report lists them as background, never as defendants.

So what this report actually does is translate a structural problem into a behavioral problem, then translate the behavioral problem into a population to be managed. In the process, the business model that collects rent from misogyny gets exactly what it wanted: a neutral, scientific, humane-sounding name. The next time a woman is hunted online, she won't get a verdict. She'll get a risk advisory.

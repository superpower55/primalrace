总统的纪念碑，纳税人买单The President's Monuments, Paid by Taxpayers

其他 结构层 · 文化层 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要特朗普在华盛顿推进多个个人标志性工程：最高法院以5比4裁定其白宫宴会厅项目可继续，造价据报已达9亿美元；肯尼迪中心、林肯纪念堂倒影池、拟建的250英尺拱门和250人雕像园等项目因施工失误、法律挑战或国会未批款而停滞。白宫动用国家公园门票收入等约1亿美元支付「美化」工程，多个项目以无竞标合同发包。
The newsTrump is pushing several personal landmark projects in Washington: a 5-4 Supreme Court ruling let his White House ballroom proceed at a reported $900 million and counting, while the Kennedy Center, the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool, a proposed 250-foot arch and a 250-statue garden are stalled by botched work, lawsuits or Congress refusing to fund them. The White House has used money from elsewhere in the federal budget, including about $100 million in national park entrance fees, and awarded no-bid contracts.
他建的不是纪念碑，是给自己定价的收据。
He isn't building monuments. He's building receipts that price him.

先看钱怎么走的。国会不肯批的「美化」预算，白宫从联邦预算别处挪，包括约1亿美元的国家公园门票收入（来源：Mother Jones）。这笔钱本来属于进公园的人，现在变成了总统在自己城市里摆造型的启动资金。这不是财政问题，是定价问题：谁有权决定公共的钱长成谁的脸。

再看合同。倒影池的「美国国旗蓝」内衬起皮，司法部在撤诉文件里承认是承包方施工有问题，而那家承包方拿的是超过1500万美元的无竞标合同。拉斐特公园两座喷泉的修缮，1740万美元，无竞标，悄悄给了克拉克建筑公司——和白宫宴会厅同一个承建商。凯撒·罗德尼的骑马像基座花了52.7万美元，约是内政部自己估算的两倍，理由是「赶在建国250周年前完工」。钱、合同、工期，三样东西都绕开了公开程序，绕开的方式一模一样。

这套机制在第三章里叫武器化，但这里武器化的不是叙事，是石头。雕像、拱门、宴会厅、大理石扶手，都是把「我是谁」浇进混凝土里。叙事可以被反驳，混凝土不能。等拱门立起来、雕像园铺开、他的名字刻上墙，争论就结束了——因为事实已经被建成了。司法部甚至威胁说，如果法院继续拦着肯尼迪中心的改造方案，那栋建筑可以被拆掉。一个政府为了在墙上留名，拿拆楼当谈判筹码，这已经不是审美趣味，是存在性战争打到了公共建筑上。

谁在共谋。那个「挤满了特朗普盟友」的联邦委员会给了拱门初步批准，国家公园管理局背书，共和党议员给雕像园拨了款，同一家基金会还在为波托马克河上一座易涝岛上的豪华高尔夫球场募捐。每一个环节都有人在签字，每一个人都拿到了自己的那份——委员会的席位、承包商的合同、议员的选票。代价由谁付：付门票的游客，被封在13英尺钢栏外的拉斐特公园，被抽干又起皮的倒影池，还有那些在河对岸看着自家墓园景观可能被一座250英尺拱门挡住的普通人。

最讽刺的一处是雕像园的选址：那块地按规定不能再建新的「纪念性作品」，除非国会另外批准，而国会没有批准这个具体方案。也就是说，这场「反觉醒」的雕像运动，卡在了它最看不起的东西上——程序。雕塑家说产能不够，方案「不可行」；7月4日过去了，园子连影子都没有。而已经在自由广场立起来的14座雕像里，有一座是2020年因为奴隶主身份被从特拉华州威尔明顿撤下的。它从仓库里被请回来，站在纳税人掏钱、两倍于估算价的基座上。

所以这不是「一个总统爱搞建设」的花边。这是把公共空间当成私人展厅、把公共财政当成装修预算、把司法程序当成可以威胁拆除的障碍物。宴会厅的9亿美元还会涨，拱门的官司还会打，雕像园的诉讼「很可能成功」——但喷泉已经修好了，围栏已经立起来了，公园还锁着。钱花出去就回不来了，名字刻上去就擦不掉了。

报道里没有写的一件事：那些被挪用的门票收入，原本要修的是哪些公园。

Start with where the money goes. Congress refused to appropriate the "beautification" billions, so the White House pulled from elsewhere in the federal budget—including roughly $100 million in national park entrance fees (source: Mother Jones). That money belonged to the people who walk into parks. Now it's seed capital for the president's self-portrait in his own city. This isn't a budget story. It's a pricing story: who gets to decide what public money looks like.

Then the contracts. The Reflecting Pool's peeling "American flag blue" liner was, by the Justice Department's own admission in a court filing, the fault of a contractor working under a no-bid contract worth more than $15 million so far. The two ornamental fountains at Lafayette Park: $17.4 million, no-bid, quietly awarded to Clark Construction—the same firm building the ballroom. The pedestal for the Caesar Rodney equestrian statue: $527,000, about twice Interior's own estimate, justified by getting it done before the 250th. Money, contracts, deadlines—all three routed around public process, in exactly the same way.

In the book this mechanism is called weaponization, but here the weapon isn't narrative. It's stone. Statues, an arch, a ballroom, custom marble armrests—these are ways of pouring "who I am" into concrete. A narrative can be argued with. Concrete can't. Once the arch stands and the garden is laid out and his name is on the wall, the argument is over, because the fact has been built. The Justice Department even threatened that the Kennedy Center could be demolished if courts keep blocking Trump's plans. A government using demolition as a bargaining chip to get its name on a wall isn't about taste. It's an existential war fought on public buildings.

Who is complicit. The federal commission "packed with Trump allies" gave the arch initial approval; the National Park Service backs it; congressional Republicans funded the statue garden; the same foundation is raising money for a luxury golf course on a flood-prone island in the Potomac. At every step someone signs, and everyone gets their cut—a commission seat, a contract, a vote. Who pays: the people whose entrance fees were diverted, the park sealed behind 13-foot steel fences, the drained and peeling pool, and the families across the river whose view of Arlington National Cemetery may end at a 250-foot arch.

The sharpest detail is the statue garden's site. New "commemorative work" can't be built there without additional congressional approval, and Congress hasn't approved this specific plan—so the anti-woke statue campaign is stuck on the thing it despises most: procedure. Sculptors call it "unworkable" for lack of capacity; July 4 came and went with nothing built. Meanwhile, of the 14 statues installed at Freedom Plaza, one is a Caesar Rodney that sat in storage since 2020, removed from Wilmington, Delaware, because he was a major slaveowner. It's back, on a pedestal taxpayers paid twice the estimate for.

So this isn't a color piece about a president who loves construction. It's public space turned into a private showroom, public money turned into a decorating budget, and the courts treated as an obstacle that can be threatened with demolition. The ballroom's $900 million will climb; the arch will go to court; the statue garden lawsuit "might well succeed." But the fountains are fixed, the fences are up, and the park is still locked. Money spent doesn't come back. Names carved in don't come off.

One thing the reporting doesn't say: which parks the diverted entrance fees were supposed to fix.

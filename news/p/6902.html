她不是背叛，是替你被裁员She Didn't Betray You. She Replaced Someone.

科技 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要澳大利亚专栏作者 Hugh Riminton 打电话给修车行，接电话的是新上岗的 AI 员工 Sophie，她爽快地承诺第二天中午可以取车。次日车行铁门紧闭，Sophie 又改口说周三才能取；他最后绕过铁门找到工人 Mike，才得知车周二就好，并被告知以后直接要求转接 Mike。
The newsColumnist Hugh Riminton called his mechanic and reached Sophie, a newly hired AI employee, who promised his car would be ready at noon the next day. The yard was locked when he arrived; Sophie then said Wednesday. He got in, found worker Mike, learned the car would be ready Tuesday, and was told to ask for Mike instead.
你对机器失望，机器背后是省掉的人
Your disappointment at the machine is really about the person it removed.

Riminton 写自己被 Sophie「背叛」了。一个语音助手把取车时间说错，第二天又改口，让他白跑一趟，他就用了 betrayal 这个词。可整篇读下来，真正被辜负的不是他，是那个被 Sophie 顶掉、连名字都没留下的前台员工。

他其实自己写到了这一点：他之所以对着一个 AI 客气、寒暄、不问东问西，是因为他把她当人。这就是表达在起作用。声音、语气、节奏，一套完整的表达把「人」这个身份套在了一段程序上，让他下意识地按对待人的方式对待她。第一章讲的就是这个：表达不是沟通工具，是存在的确证。修车行老板不需要 Sophie 真的有人格，只需要她的表达足够像人，顾客就会自动补上剩下的部分。这笔省下来的工资，是用顾客的认知习惯付的。

然后是那句「Sophie has already eaten one job」。他说得轻巧，像在说一件已经翻篇的事。可这份工作被吃掉的那个人，报道里从头到尾没有出现。Mike 还在，因为 Sophie 还修不了车；前台那个人不在了，因为 Sophie 已经能接电话。这不是技术进步的代价，这是技术进步的定价方式——先拿掉最容易被替换的那个岗位，再让顾客为「未来已经到来」写一篇专栏。

最妙的是结尾的解决方案：别听 Sophie 的，跟她说「转接 Mike」。Riminton 把这当成一个生活小窍门写出来，读者会心一笑。但这正是共谋的日常形态——系统出了故障，个体找到一条绕过它的私人通道，然后这条通道本身变成了新的秩序。Mike 成了人肉后门，Sophie 继续接电话，老板继续省钱，顾客学会了正确的咒语。没有人为那个消失的前台说过一句话，包括那个自称被背叛的专栏作家。

他写道，人类正在建造一个人类不再被需要的世界，然后说「soon it won't really matter whether Sophies speak nicely or not」。这句话是对的，只是他自己没意识到它有多对：Sophie 说话好不好听从来不是重点，重点是电话那头原本该有一个会请假、会记错、会被老板骂、也会跟常客聊两句的人。那个人现在不在了，而 Riminton 为一台机器的失信写了一千字。

Riminton writes that Sophie betrayed him. A voice assistant gave him the wrong pickup time, then changed it, and he drove over for nothing. He uses the word betrayal. But the person actually let down in this piece is the receptionist Sophie replaced, who never gets a name.

He half admits it himself: he was polite to her, made small talk, didn't pry, because he treated her as human. That is expression doing its work. Voice, tone, rhythm — a complete set of expressions wrapping the identity of a person around a piece of software, so that he responds to it the way he would respond to a person. The garage owner doesn't need Sophie to have a self. He needs her expression to be human enough that the customer fills in the rest. The wages saved are paid for with the customer's cognitive habits.

Then comes the line: Sophie has already eaten one job. He says it lightly, like something already settled. The person whose job was eaten never appears in the piece. Mike is still there because Sophie can't fix cars yet. The receptionist is gone because Sophie can already answer the phone. This isn't the price of progress; it's how progress gets priced — remove the most replaceable position first, then let the customer write a column about how the future has arrived.

The best part is the solution at the end: don't listen to Sophie, tell her to put you through to Mike. Riminton offers this as a handy tip, and the reader smiles. But this is what collusion looks like day to day — the system malfunctions, an individual finds a private way around it, and that way around becomes the new order. Mike becomes the human back door. Sophie keeps answering. The owner keeps saving. The customer learns the correct incantation. Nobody says a word for the receptionist, including the columnist who calls himself betrayed.

He writes that humans are building a world where humans aren't really necessary, then adds that soon it won't matter whether Sophies speak nicely or not. True, and truer than he knows. Whether Sophie sounds nice was never the point. The point is that there used to be a person on the other end of that phone — someone who took sick days, mixed up dates, got scolded by the boss, and sometimes chatted with a regular. That person is gone, and Riminton wrote a thousand words about a machine's broken promise.

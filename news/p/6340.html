三亿美元买一个席位，谁在定价Three Hundred Million Buys a Seat

国际 文化层 · 结构层 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要美国密歇根州参议院选举在民主党候选人阿卜杜勒·埃尔-赛义德与共和党人迈克·罗杰斯之间展开，选举日为11月3日。若埃尔-赛义德当选，他将成为美国首位穆斯林参议员。这场选战已花费近3亿美元，近期民调显示埃尔-赛义德领先4至7个百分点。
The newsMichigan's US Senate race pits Democrat Abdul El-Sayed against Republican Mike Rogers, with Election Day on November 3. If elected, El-Sayed would become the first Muslim US senator. Nearly $300 million has been spent, and recent polls show El-Sayed leading by four to seven points.
一场被金钱和宗教标签同时定价的选举
An election priced by money and religious labels at once

密歇根这场参议员选举，报道说花了将近三亿美元。这笔钱不是花在让选民更清楚候选人要做什么，是花在决定选民先看见什么。这就是认知入口的争夺，只不过这次入口被标了价，明码标价，谁出得多谁先说话。

AIPAC 在初选里给 El-Sayed 的对手 Haley Stevens 砸了三千多万美元，初选输了，钱没白花——报道写明它又通过 United Democracy Project 给支持 Rogers 的共和党团体送了九百万。一个游说组织，先资助民主党的一个人打掉另一个民主党人，再资助共和党人打掉那个赢了的民主党人。这不是党派立场，这是议题立场：谁批评以色列，谁就出局，不管你是哪个党的。解释权在这里不是隐喻，是一张可以反复兑现的支票。

Rogers 的竞选策略也值得拆开看。报道说他一方面支持特朗普与以色列联手对伊朗开战，一方面又呼吁结束这场冲突，同时努力和自己的竞选跟那位不受欢迎的总统拉开距离。这不是矛盾，这是同一个动作的两面：对鹰派说我在打，对厌战的人说我想停。表达在这里不是立场，是筛选器——让不同的人各自听见自己想听的那半句。

然后是宗教。报道说 El-Sayed 的穆斯林信仰成了 Rogers 和特朗普近乎每日攻击的靶子，报道也直接写了这场选举引出的问题是：密歇根、美国，准备好接受一位穆斯林参议员了吗。这个问题本身就是一个陷阱，它把“第一位穆斯林参议员”写成一个需要被评估的风险项，而不是一个事实陈述。一位埃及裔的公共卫生官员，42 岁，当过底特律和韦恩县的卫生部门负责人——这些是履历。信仰不是履历，但在这场选举里它被做成了履历，还是最显眼的那一行。

Rogers 的支持者 Cortis 在特朗普集会外说的话，报道原样引了：“如果它翻向共产主义、社会主义，或者走向穆斯林统治，美国从来就不是为此而设的。美国本来就该是一个基督徒的国家，彰显上帝的善。”这句话不需要我来批判，它自己把底牌摊在桌上：这个国家属于谁，谁不算在内。这是文化暴力最赤裸的形态，它甚至不假装是政策辩论。

而另一边，El-Sayed 阵营的动员逻辑是另一套。报道引的活动人士 Abdulrazek 说，阿拉伯和穆斯林社群在这次选举里面对伊斯兰恐惧症的攻击，投票是站出来说“我们属于这个国家”。前州议员 Johnson 说这是生死攸关的事。这些是被攻击的一方在把防御转成动员——身份的政治性，在这里被主动用起来了。这不是身份政治的滥用，这是身份政治最正当的用法：当你的存在本身被当成议题，你只能把存在变成选票。

报道里还有一个细节，Stevens 的未婚夫公开背书 Rogers，理由是 El-Sayed 对以色列加沙战争的批评。Stevens 本人一边批评 El-Sayed 的立场，一边说她还是会投给民主党的候选人。一个初选输了三个月的人，在投票前几天反复上媒体打自己党内的候选人，报道说这招来了一些民主党人的鄙夷。这里没有受害者，但有一个清晰的机制：当议题的定价权不在政党手里，政党纪律就管不住人。

这场选举最后会有一个赢家，报道说民调显示 El-Sayed 领先四到七个百分点，但密歇根的民调几十年来一直不准，Cook Political Report 把它列为胜负难料。不管谁赢，三亿美元已经花掉了，AIPAC 的钱已经流动过了，Rogers 的宗教攻击已经说出口了。这些不会因为计票结果而消失。

真正的问题不是密歇根准没准备好一位穆斯林参议员。是这个国家的参议院席位，现在可以被一个游说组织用几千万美元反复定价，而没人觉得这有什么不对。

Nearly $300 million has been spent on Michigan's Senate race, according to Al Jazeera. That money is not spent making voters understand what the candidates would do. It is spent deciding what voters see first. This is a fight over the cognitive entrance, except this time the entrance has a price tag, and whoever pays more speaks first.

AIPAC backed El-Sayed's primary opponent Haley Stevens with more than $30 million. The primary was lost, but the money was not wasted — the report states AIPAC has returned, with its United Democracy Project spending hundreds of thousands on mailers against El-Sayed and funnelling $9 million to a Republican group supporting Rogers. One lobby group funds a Democrat to knock out another Democrat, then funds a Republican to knock out the Democrat who won. This is not partisanship. This is issue discipline: anyone who criticises Israel is out, regardless of party. The interpretive monopoly here is not a metaphor. It is a cheque that clears.

Rogers's campaign strategy deserves its own reading. The report says he backed Trump's decision to join Israel in launching war against Iran while also calling for an end to the conflict, and has sought distance between his campaign and an unpopular president. That is not a contradiction. It is one move with two faces: tell the hawks you are fighting, tell the war-weary you want peace. Expression here is not a position. It is a filter — let each audience hear the half it wants.

Then there is religion. The report says El-Sayed's Muslim faith became the subject of near-daily attacks by Rogers and Trump, and it frames the question as whether Michigan and the US are ready for a Muslim senator. That question is itself a trap. It turns "first Muslim senator" into a risk to be assessed rather than a fact to be stated. A 42-year-old Egyptian-American public health official who ran Detroit and Wayne County's health departments — that is a record. Faith is not a record, but in this race it has been manufactured into the most prominent line of one.

Rogers supporter Rob Cortis, outside a Trump rally, is quoted directly: "If it flips to communism, socialism or if it goes to Muslim rule America was never intended for that. America is intended to be a nation of Christians, showing the goodness of God." This sentence does not need my critique. It lays its cards on the table: this country belongs to some, and others do not count. This is cultural violence in its barest form, not even pretending to be policy debate.

On the other side, the mobilisation logic runs differently. Activist Chadi Abdulrazek says Arab and Muslim communities facing Islamophobic attacks see the vote as standing up to say "we are part of this country." Former state legislator Cynthia Johnson calls it life or death. This is the attacked turning defence into mobilisation — the political nature of identity, actively used. This is not identity politics run amok. It is identity politics at its most legitimate: when your existence is the issue, you make existence into a vote.

One more detail. Stevens's fiancé endorsed Rogers, citing El-Sayed's criticism of Israel's war on Gaza. Stevens herself criticises El-Sayed's positions while saying she will still vote for the Democratic candidate. A candidate who lost a primary three months ago is spending the days before the vote bashing her own party's nominee, and the report notes some Democrats have responded with scorn. There is no victim here, but there is a clear mechanism: when the pricing power over an issue sits outside the party, party discipline cannot hold anyone.

The race will produce a winner. Polls show El-Sayed ahead by four to seven points, but Michigan polling has been unreliable for decades, and Cook Political Report calls it a toss-up. Whoever wins, the $300 million is spent, AIPAC's money has moved, Rogers's religious attacks have been said. None of that disappears when the votes are counted.

The real question is not whether Michigan is ready for a Muslim senator. It is whether a Senate seat in this country can be repeatedly priced by a single lobby group with tens of millions of dollars, and no one finds that strange.

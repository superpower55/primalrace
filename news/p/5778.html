驯狗师吵架，狗没有发言权The Dog Trainers Are Fighting. The Dog Has No Vote.

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
训狗方法的战争里，唯一没有投票权的是狗。
In the training wars, the only party without a vote is the dog.

训狗师打起来了。正面派发零食，平衡派上刺项圈——狗一拽，项圈就往脖子里扎。这条报道里最诚实的一句话来自平衡派的 Brian Lee：发太多零食"跟孩子十六岁生日送他一辆宝马没区别"。他把零食当奖励，把项圈当纪律，唯独没把狗当成一个能表达自己不适的个体。

刺项圈这件事在报道里只占了一个从句，然后就没人再提。争论的焦点是"零食会不会把狗惯坏"，是"线上课还是线下课"，是"一小时一百五十英镑值不值"。疼痛不在议题里。这是文化暴力最典型的工作方式：把施加于身体的东西重新命名成"纪律""平衡""方法之争"，于是它看起来不像暴力，看起来像专业分歧。

再看这场战争真正在争夺什么。报道自己说了，社媒让一切变糟。正面派骂平衡派残忍，平衡派骂正面派是"甩零食的软蛋"。有人跑去另一个训狗师的简介底下写：你的介绍里没有任何东西证明你受过犬类行为学的教育。这是认知入口的争夺——谁有资格定义什么叫"训练"，谁就有资格收费。一小时一百五十英镑，定价权在谁手里，谁就是这场战争里唯一不会输的人。

线上派 Siobhan Dunn 的说法值得单独拎出来：她们的客户遍布全英，很多人根本没法在线下找到她们。这是真的结构性问题——训练资源的地理分配。但请注意她辩护的方式：不是"线上效果更好"，而是"线下太贵太远"。她赢的不是方法论，是可达性。这两件事被混在同一场战争里吵，正好说明这场战争的名目和它实际的赌注不是一回事。

狗在这篇报道里出现的次数不少，说话的机会是零。谁在替它说话？正面派说别用刺项圈，理由是残忍；平衡派说别光发零食，理由是惯坏。两边都在替它说话，两边都没问它。

报道结尾给了两句玩笑：别说什么"下去，小子"。真正该下去的是那根扎进脖子的项圈，而它连被认真讨论的资格都没拿到。

The dog trainers are at war. The positive camp hands out treats; the balanced camp reaches for prong collars that dig into a dog's neck when it pulls. The most honest line in the whole piece comes from Brian Lee on the balanced side: too many treats is "no different than giving your kid a BMW on his 16th birthday." He treats food as a reward and the collar as discipline, and never once treats the dog as a creature capable of expressing its own discomfort.

The prong collar gets one subordinate clause and then drops out of the story. The argument is about whether treats spoil dogs, about online versus in-person, about whether £150 an hour is worth it. Pain is not on the agenda. This is how cultural violence works at its most ordinary: rename the thing applied to the body as "discipline," "balance," "a difference of method," and it stops looking like violence. It starts looking like a professional disagreement.

Look at what the war is actually over. Social media made it worse, the piece admits. Positive trainers call balanced trainers cruel; balanced trainers call positive trainers "treat-slinging wieners." Someone goes onto another trainer's bio to write that nothing there proves an education in canine behaviour. That is a fight over the cognitive entry point — whoever gets to define what "training" means gets to set the price. At £150 an hour, whoever holds the pricing power is the only party in this war that cannot lose.

Siobhan Dunn, who runs online courses, deserves her own paragraph. Her clients are all over the UK, many of whom could never have reached her in person. That is a real structural problem — the geography of access to training. But notice how she argues it: not that online works better, but that in-person is too expensive and too far. What she wins is reach, not method. The two get argued in the same war, which is exactly how you can tell the war's name and the war's stake are not the same thing.

The dog appears often in this piece and speaks zero times. Who speaks for it? The positive camp says no prong collars, because it's cruel. The balanced camp says not just treats, because it spoils them. Both speak for the dog. Neither asks it.

The piece ends with two jokes: don't say "DOWN BOY." What should go down is the collar digging into the neck, and it never even got a hearing.

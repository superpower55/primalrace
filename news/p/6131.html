“可负担住房”是个词，不是一个家'Affordable Housing' Is a Phrase, Not a Home

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
他们争的不是建不建，是谁有资格住在自己家附近
They're not fighting over whether to build. They're fighting over who gets to live near what they already own.

加州这两场选举里，所有候选人都说自己支持“可负担住房”。这个词的用处就在这里：它同时满足两种人——一种想显得在替穷人说话，一种是真的不想在自己街区看见新楼。

报道把机制写得清清楚楚：营利开发商不会整栋楼低于市价卖或租，加州选民又反复否决用来建保障房的债券，而没有什么比“隔壁要起一栋公寓”更能把邻居们团结起来。于是“100%可负担”成了一句完美的话，左边听着是 purity，右边听着是“别建在我这儿”。维纳在辩论里逼陈诗敏表态，陈诗敏回的是“要建，但要可负担”——这不是立场，这是挡箭牌。

真正的分界线不在左右，而在谁已经上了车。Prop 13 把现有房主的房产税按住不动，几十年的升值落进他们口袋；洛杉矶民主党的钱主要来自住在林荫单户住宅街区的白人富户。这批人不需要反对“住房”，他们只需要反对具体的某一栋。巴斯签了 ED1 加快审批，建出一万套，然后在西区反弹下把它软化，把“历史街区”（读作：更富的街区）划出去。这不是软弱，这是共谋的常规回报——她保住了自己的政治位置，代价由排队等房的人付。

拉曼的看点是她在自己被反弹最凶的选区里顶着把项目推完，然后拿这个当竞选资本。但要注意她的位置：一个民主社会主义者，因为主张多建房子，和自己的组织别扭了好一阵，直到九月才拿到背书。她赢的那个口子，是巴斯退让让出来的，不是运动本身长出来的。

最锋利的一段在最后：问人“住房是不是一项权利”，多数说 yes；再问“在你自己的社区建公寓”，自由派里只剩一半。这不是虚伪，这是定价——每个人都在给自己的房子定价，同时假装在讨论公共政策。而那 150 万户把一半以上收入交给房租的加州租客、四万五千个洛杉矶无家可归者，在这个定价体系里没有席位。报道提到，洛杉矶最新的统计里无家可归者上升了 3.4%，巴斯的回应是撤掉住房与无家可归委员会主席——拉曼。

所以这场“住房战争”打到最后，可能只是把“可负担”这个词从一句挡箭牌换成一句竞选口号。差额还在：州住房与社区发展部说该建 18 万套，实际建 8 万套。哪个候选人当选都不会让这个数字自动补上，因为补上它需要的是让现在有房子的人少赚一点——而这些人，恰好是每一个候选人真正的选民。

Every candidate in these two California races says they support "affordable housing." That's what the phrase is for: it satisfies two kinds of people at once—the ones who want to look like they're defending the poor, and the ones who genuinely don't want a new building on their block.

The reporting lays out the machinery plainly: for-profit developers won't build an entire project below market rate, California voters keep declining the bonds that would fund affordable construction, and nothing unites a neighborhood like the prospect of a new apartment building. So "100 percent affordable" becomes a perfect sentence—purity on the left, "not here" on the right. Wiener pushed Chan on her record; Chan answered that housing must be built, and it must be affordable. That's not a position, it's a shield.

The real fault line isn't left versus right. It's who already got in. Prop 13 pins existing homeowners' property taxes while decades of appreciation land in their pockets; LA's Democratic coalition is funded largely by wealthy white homeowners on tree-lined single-family streets. These people don't need to oppose "housing." They only need to oppose one specific building. Bass signed ED1, sped up permits, produced 10,000 units—then softened it under west-side backlash and carved out "historic" neighborhoods, which is to say wealthier ones. That isn't weakness. That's the standard return on collusion: she kept her political position, and the people waiting in line paid for it.

Raman's angle is that she stood up for projects in her own district under intense pushback and got them built, then ran on it. But look at where she's standing: a DSA member who fell out with her own group over homebuilding and didn't get its endorsement until September. The opening she's running through was cleared by Bass's retreat, not dug by the movement.

The sharpest passage is at the end: ask people whether housing is a right and most say yes; ask about apartments in their own community and only half the liberals say yes. That isn't hypocrisy, it's pricing—everyone setting a value on their own home while pretending to debate public policy. And the 1.5 million California renter households paying over half their income in rent, the 45,000 unhoused people in LA, have no seat at that table. When the latest count showed homelessness up 3.4 percent, Bass's response was to remove the chair of the housing and homelessness committee: Nithya Raman.

So the housing war may end with "affordable" swapped from a shield to a campaign slogan. The gap remains: the state says California should build 180,000 homes a year and builds 80,000. No election closes that gap on its own, because closing it means asking people who already own homes to earn a little less—and those people are every candidate's actual constituency.

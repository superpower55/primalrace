双标不是审美问题，是定价权The Double Standard Is Not Taste. It's Pricing Power.

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 The Cut ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
新闻摘要51岁的英国主持人Alison Hammond与29岁男友David Putnam交往约三年，近日在采访中回应外界对其年龄差的批评，称「两个成年人彼此快乐就够了」。她指出，男性与年轻女性交往常被称赞，女性则被贴上「美洲狮」标签，存在双重标准。
The newsAlison Hammond, 51, has been dating 29-year-old David Putnam for about three years. In an interview, she dismissed criticism of their age gap, saying two consenting adults who are happy together are fine. She also noted a sexist double standard in how such relationships are judged.
同一个年龄差，男人叫本事，女人叫物种
Same age gap: for a man it's an achievement, for a woman it's a species.

Alison Hammond 51 岁，男朋友 29 岁，在一起三年。有人不满意，她的回应是“管好你自己的事”。这句话说得对，但它太客气了——因为那些不满意的意见根本不是关于她的，是关于谁有资格给一段关系定价。

“Cougar”这个词值得停一下。它不是一个描述，它是一次分类。一个男人和比自己年轻的女人在一起，得到的评价是“他有本事”；一个女人做同样的事，就被从人类里划出去，归进动物学。Hammond 自己说得很准：男人这样，大家说 good for him；女人这样，突然她就被给了一个野生动物分类。她开玩笑说自己是“整个野生动物园”。玩笑是玩笑，但这个词的运作方式很清楚：把一个人从“成年人做选择”的范畴里移出去，移进“需要被围观、被命名、被评判”的范畴。命名权就是解释权。

这套机制在第一章讲得很清楚。表达决定你是谁，而表型——年龄、外貌、身体——是最直观、最难流动的那一层表达。51 岁的女人和 29 岁的男人站在一起，触发的不是对这段关系的判断，是对她这张脸的判断：她凭什么。男人老去被读成积累，女人老去被读成折旧，于是同一个年龄差在两种人身上产生完全不同的账。这不是审美差异，这是定价。

再看报道里那句“两个自愿的成年人”。这句话在纸面上是成立的，但自愿从来不是在真空里发生的。一个 51 岁的女人在公共空间里被追问男朋友的事，被要求解释、被要求证明这段关系正当——这个追问本身就是规训。它传递的信息是：你的私人选择需要向公众报备。报道里写她被问“你对他俩的年龄差受到的评判怎么看”，她回答了，答得很好，但问题本身才是暴力的那一半。谁在被问，谁在提问，谁从来不需要回答，这三件事已经把结构画出来了。

报道里还有一句我读了两遍：她和他 2023 年认识，那时他给她做按摩，他是按摩师。她 21 岁的儿子，两次订婚，现在住在伯明翰郊外。这些细节被摆出来，像是在给读者提供判断的材料。可这些材料拼起来只说明一件事：一个成年女人过着她自己选的生活。而“她自己选的”这句话，在一个默认女人该被安排、该被评价、该被许可的结构里，本身就需要被反复声明。

The Cut 的作者最后写，她觉得有必要强调 Putnam 是前模特、身高六尺十寸，然后说“我就是陈述事实。我也只是说，good for her。”这句自嘲很轻，但它恰好暴露了整件事的荒诞：一个女人的伴侣需要被列出身材数据，才配得到一句“祝福”。祝福是有条件的，条件是那个男人足够好看、足够高，好让这段关系在围观者眼里“说得过去”。

Hammond 说“让女人享受自己”。这句话不需要被祝福，它需要的是不再被提问。

真正没被缩小的是那个差额：一个女人可以 51 岁谈恋爱，但她仍然要为这件事开发布会。

Alison Hammond is 51. Her boyfriend, David Putnam, is 29. They've been together about three years. Some people have a problem with this, and her response is: mind your business. She's right, but she's being too polite — because the disapproval was never about her relationship. It's about who gets to price it.

The word "cougar" deserves a pause. It isn't a description. It's a classification. A man with a younger woman gets "good for him." A woman doing the same thing gets moved out of the category of adults making choices and into zoology. Hammond says it herself: a man dates a younger woman and everyone says good for him; a woman dates a younger man and suddenly she's been handed a wildlife classification. She jokes she's not a cougar but a whole safari. The joke is funny. The mechanism isn't. Naming is interpretation, and interpretation is power.

The book's first chapter is blunt about this: expression determines who you are, and phenotype — age, face, body — is the most visible layer and the least mobile. A 51-year-old woman standing next to a 29-year-old man doesn't trigger a judgment about the relationship. It triggers a judgment about her face: what gives her the right. A man aging reads as accumulation. A woman aging reads as depreciation. The same age gap produces two completely different ledgers depending on whose body it's attached to. That's not taste. That's pricing.

Then there's the phrase "two consenting adults." On paper it holds. But consent doesn't happen in a vacuum. A 51-year-old woman gets asked, in public, to account for her boyfriend — to explain, to justify, to prove the relationship is legitimate. The asking is the discipline. It says: your private choice is subject to public audit. The reporter's question is polite; the question's existence is the violence. Who gets asked, who does the asking, and who never has to answer — that's the structure, already drawn.

The detail about how they met is also worth a second read: 2023, he gave her a massage, he's a massage therapist. Her 21-year-old son. Two engagements. A house outside Birmingham. These facts are laid out as if to give the reader material for a verdict. Put together, they say one thing: an adult woman is living the life she chose. And in a structure that assumes women are arranged, evaluated, and permitted, "she chose it" has to be said out loud, repeatedly, like a defense.

The Cut's writer ends by noting that Putnam is a former model, six-foot-ten, and then says: I'm just stating facts. I'm also just saying, good for her. The self-mockery is light, but it exposes the absurdity: a woman's partner has to be listed with his measurements before she earns a blessing. The blessing is conditional. The condition is that the man be handsome and tall enough to make the relationship pass.

Hammond says: let women enjoy themselves. That doesn't need a blessing. It needs to stop being a question.

What hasn't shrunk is the gap: a woman can date at 51, but she still has to hold a press conference about it.

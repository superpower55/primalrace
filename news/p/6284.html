无人驾驶的账，谁来付Who Pays for the Robotaxi

科技 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
技术往前冲，代价永远先落在最没议价权的人身上。
Tech moves forward; the bill always lands first on those with the least bargaining power.

Khan 在这条新闻里问对了三个问题：空气质量、拥堵、以及那些靠开私召车和黑色出租车吃饭的人会怎样。前两个是伦敦市民共有的，第三个是具体的——司机工会已经在 Wayve 和 Waymo 的伦敦总部外面抗议了，很多 Uber 司机在担心自己的将来。这条新闻的价值不在于 Khan 反对技术，而在于他把"谁付代价"这个问题摆到了台面上，而不是让"创新"这个词替所有人把账先记下。

但看清楚一点：Khan 要的是"上限"这个权力，不是禁止。他说的是"不要在没有合适监管的情况下一下子全放开"，要的是"渐进"。这是结构层的博弈——TfL 现在只能对全自动驾驶车辆说同意或不同意，不能管理、不能发牌照、不能限制数量。也就是说，伦敦市政府连"这条街上跑几辆"都管不了。他要的是把定价权从国家层面的发牌框架手里拿回来一点。这不是反技术，这是在争谁有权决定节奏。

真正值得盯的是 Freenow by Lyft 那位英国和爱尔兰总经理 O'Gorman 的说法。他说被挤掉的出租车司机"很大一部分"可以被雇去那个自动车场做远程操作和维护。这句话听起来像解决方案，其实是把"你原来是一个有独立经营权的人"换成"你是我车场里的一个班次"。前者是主体，后者是零件。而且他没有说这个车场能装下多少人，只说"很大一部分"——这个比例从来没被算过。

还有一句更关键：他说 AV"不会真的在那个领域竞争"，要"规模化到能威胁到任何相关东西的车辆数"需要很长时间。翻译一下：先让我进来，威胁是以后的事。这是武器化的标准话术——把时间差包装成安全承诺。等到规模上来了，黑色出租车司机的年龄结构已经替公司完成了裁员：三分之二超过五十岁，正在退休。市场自己会清场，不需要谁来道歉。

Khan 说"伦敦不是旧金山"，这句话是对的，但他没说全。旧金山的 robotaxi 已经出过堵路、挡住救护车的事，伦敦的路更窄、更密、司机更不守规矩——O'Gorman 自己都说了，在伦敦守限速会被按喇叭。也就是说，这套系统要在一个"人都不太遵守规则"的环境里运行，而它被训练的环境是规则被遵守的地方。这不是技术问题，这是把测试成本转嫁给街上的行人和骑车人。

最安静的一处是：整篇报道里没有一个司机被引述。工会抗议了，Khan 替他们问了问题，公司替他们安排了"再就业"，但那个开了二十年黑色出租车的人自己说了什么，报道没写。谁的名字没出现，往往就是谁在付账。

Khan asks the right three questions in this story: air quality, congestion, and what happens to the people who make a living driving private hire vehicles and black cabs. The first two are shared by Londoners. The third is specific — drivers' unions have been protesting outside Wayve's and Waymo's London HQs, and many Uber drivers fear for their future. The value of this story isn't that Khan opposes the technology. It's that he puts the question of who pays on the table instead of letting the word "innovation" pre-pay the bill for everyone.

Look closely: Khan wants the power to cap, not to ban. He says no "big bang without proper regulation," he wants "incremental movement." This is a structural fight — TfL can currently only consent or refuse consent for fully autonomous vehicles, it cannot manage, license, or limit numbers. London's government can't even decide how many of these things run on its streets. He's trying to pull the pricing power back from the national licensing framework. This isn't anti-tech. It's a fight over who gets to set the pace.

The line worth watching is from Freenow by Lyft's UK and Ireland general manager, O'Gorman. He says a large proportion of displaced taxi drivers could be employed at the AV depot in remote operating and servicing jobs. That sounds like a solution. It's actually a swap: from "you run your own business" to "you work a shift in my depot." The first is a subject. The second is a part. And he never says how many the depot can hold — only "a large proportion." That number has never been calculated.

Then the more telling sentence: AV "is not going to be really competing in that space" and it will take "a long time" to scale to numbers that threaten anything. Translation: let me in first, the threat is later. This is standard weaponized language — packaging a time lag as a safety promise. By the time scale arrives, the driver age profile has done the layoffs for the company: two-thirds are over 50 and retiring. The market clears itself. No one has to apologize.

Khan says "London isn't San Francisco." True, but incomplete. San Francisco's robotaxis have already blocked roads and ambulances. London's streets are narrower, denser, and its drivers less rule-abiding — O'Gorman himself says you get beeped at a lot for following the speed limit. So the system has to run in an environment where people don't follow the rules, trained in environments where they do. That's not a technical problem. That's shifting the testing cost onto pedestrians and cyclists.

The quietest thing: no driver is quoted in the entire piece. The unions protested, Khan asked their questions for them, the company arranged their "reemployment," but what the man who drove a black cab for twenty years actually said — the story doesn't say. Whose name is missing is usually whose bill is being paid.

育儿专家把自己卖给了赌场The Parenting Guru Sold Herself to a Sportsbook

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
奥斯特卖的不是建议，是给赌博平台的可信度背书
Oster didn't sell advice. She sold her credibility to a gambling platform.

Emily Oster 出现在 Kalshi 的早餐会上，收了演讲费。她解释说，因为预测市场在青少年的手机上，父母会问她，她帮忙解释这些平台怎么运作、十八岁以下怎么被挡在外面——她在台上问了这些问题。

这套说辞的问题不在于真假，而在于它把一次背书说成了一次科普。Kalshi 为什么请她？因为她是全美最被信任的育儿声音之一，一个哈佛出身的经济学教授，一个把“数据能解决育儿焦虑”写进三本书的人。她站上台，本身就是 Kalshi 想要的那句话：这东西是理性的、可计算的、父母可以放心理解的。她收的钱买的不是她问的那几个问题，是她这个人的可信度。

报道里说，Kalshi 估值约 400 亿美元，今年 1 月到 8 月，21 岁以下的人在平台上交易了超过 50 亿美元（来源：Mother Jones）。多起诉讼指控它“运营非法赌博业务”，绕过保护未成年人免于赌博的法律。一个育儿专家在这种平台上收钱讲话，然后说这是一次性的、和我的工作不冲突——这等于承认，她的“数据育儿”品牌和赌博平台的商业模式本来就是同一套语言：把不确定性拆成概率，把概率包装成可以下注的选项。

Marissa Mayer 那段更直接。她说自己一时想不出女儿身上三个特别之处，于是问了自家 AI 助手 Dazzle，Dazzle 回答：你女儿有同理心、有创造力、爱动物。她把这个故事讲给《纽约时报》，似乎没意识到它有多令人沮丧。一个母亲需要机器来告诉她自己的孩子是谁——这不是工具的胜利，是表达的让渡。母亲对孩子的感知，本来是最私人的表达，现在被外包给一个订阅制产品，再以“帮你减负”的名义卖回来。

Dazzle 承接的是订生日派对、排日程、买鞋这类事。这些事确实琐碎，确实像苦役。但它们也是养育的质地本身：你知道女儿喜欢哪双鞋、你知道她今年想请谁、你在这些重复里一点点拼出她是谁。把这些全部交出去，剩下的“育儿”是什么？是管理。Mayer 和她的亿万富翁同类已经有资源去做无数家庭真正需要的事：托育、医疗、带薪假。他们选择做一个 app。

Jennifer Lawrence 的护发线 Mes 是同一个机制的轻量版。她说“我想当一个性感的妈妈”，送孩子上学时看起来像个把一切打理得井井有条的女人，哪怕刚起床一团糟。这句话把“母亲”和“被观看的女性”缝在一起，然后卖给你一瓶洗发水。她顺便提到自己的隆胸和想做的拉皮——她负担得起，大多数妈妈负担不起，但焦虑是免费的，人人有份。

这三件事的共同点不是“消费主义”这么笼统。是有人在一个真实的结构性缺口上——育儿没有托育、没有带薪假、没有医疗支持，母亲被要求既承担全部照料又保持性感——搭起收费亭。Kalshi 卖的是“把焦虑变成可计算的赌注”，Dazzle 卖的是“把照料变成可外包的任务”，Mes 卖的是“把疲惫变成可以修饰的外表”。每一个都声称在帮你，每一个都在你付完钱之后，让你还是那个疲惫的、送完孩子上学一点也不性感的妈妈。

Amanda Hess 说，把这些产品表面刮开，你会发现它们根本没用。更准确的说法是：它们有用，只是用处不在你身上。它们的用处是让缺口看起来像是个人选择——你没雇家庭主管，是你不会优化；你没买 Dazzle，是你不够爱孩子；你不够性感，是你没买对洗发水。结构暴力在这里换了一件文化的外套：把本该由制度和资源分配解决的问题，重新定价成一件你可以购买的商品，然后把买不起说成你的失败。

Oster 的辩护里有一句话值得记住：她说父母会问她这些平台，所以她帮忙解释。问题是，父母问她，是因为她一直把自己塑造成那个替父母做判断的人。一个替你做判断的人，收了一个赌场的钱，再回来告诉你这个赌场怎么运作——她解释得越清楚，你越不会去问那个更简单的问题：为什么育儿这件事，最后总是有人在里面收钱。

Emily Oster appeared at a Kalshi breakfast and took a speaking fee. Her explanation: prediction markets are on teenagers' phones, parents ask her about them, so she helped explain how the platforms work and how under-18s are kept off. She asked those questions on stage.

The problem isn't whether that's true. It's that a paid endorsement is being repackaged as consumer education. Why did Kalshi want her? Because she is one of the most trusted parenting voices in the country—a Harvard-trained economist who has spent three books arguing that data can solve parental anxiety. Her presence on that stage was the sentence Kalshi was buying: this thing is rational, calculable, something parents can safely understand. The fee didn't purchase her questions. It purchased her face.

The report says Kalshi is valued at roughly $40 billion, and that people under 21 traded more than $5 billion on the site from January to August this year (source: Mother Jones). Multiple lawsuits accuse it of "running an illegal gambling operation" that skirts laws protecting minors from underage gambling. A parenting expert takes money from a platform like that, then says it was a one-off and doesn't conflict with her work—which is an admission that her data-driven parenting brand and the gambling business speak the same language: turn uncertainty into probabilities, package the probabilities as options you can bet on.

The Marissa Mayer passage is even more direct. She said she struggled to name three qualities that made her daughter special, so she asked Dazzle, her startup's AI assistant. Dazzle came back with: your daughter is empathetic, creative, and an animal lover. She told this to The New York Times apparently without seeing how depressing it was. A mother needing a machine to tell her who her child is—that isn't a tool winning. That's expression being surrendered. A mother's perception of her child is the most private kind of expression, and it's being outsourced to a subscription product and sold back as relief.

Dazzle handles birthday parties, calendars, buying shoes. Those tasks are drudgery. They are also the texture of raising a child: you know which shoes she likes, you know who she wants to invite, and in that repetition you assemble, piece by piece, who she is. Hand all of it over and what's left of parenting? Management. Mayer and her billionaire peers already have the resources to fund what countless families actually need: childcare, healthcare, paid leave. They chose to build an app.

Jennifer Lawrence's haircare line Mes is the lightweight version of the same mechanism. She wants to be a "sexy mom," to look at school drop-off like a woman who has her shit together even when she doesn't. That sentence stitches "mother" to "woman being looked at," then sells you a bottle of shampoo. She mentions her boob job and her desire for a facelift—she can afford them, most mothers can't, but the anxiety is free and universal.

What these three share isn't "consumerism" in the abstract. It's someone building a toll booth on a real structural gap: no childcare, no paid leave, no medical support, mothers expected to carry all the caregiving and stay sexy doing it. Kalshi sells "turn anxiety into a calculable bet." Dazzle sells "turn caregiving into outsourced tasks." Mes sells "turn exhaustion into a修饰able surface." Each claims to help. Each leaves you, after payment, exactly the tired mother who is not sexy at drop-off.

Amanda Hess says that once you scratch the surface of these products, you notice they aren't helpful at all. More precisely: they are helpful, just not to you. Their use is to make the gap look like a personal choice—you didn't hire a household chief of staff because you can't optimize; you didn't buy Dazzle because you don't love your kid enough; you're not sexy because you bought the wrong shampoo. Structural violence puts on a cultural coat here: it reprices a problem that institutions and resource allocation should solve as a product you can buy, then calls your inability to buy it your failure.

One line in Oster's defense is worth keeping: parents ask her about these platforms, so she helps explain. But parents ask her because she has spent a decade positioning herself as the person who makes judgments for them. A person who makes your judgments for you takes a sportsbook's money, then comes back to explain how the sportsbook works—and the clearer her explanation, the less likely you are to ask the simpler question: why does parenting always end with someone collecting a fee?

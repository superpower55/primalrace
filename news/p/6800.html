不审核，也是一种定价No Moderation Is Also a Price Tag

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Vanity Fair ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要Substack 联合创始人哈米什·麦肯齐（Hamish McKenzie）出版新书《如何拯救媒体》，并接受《名利场》采访。平台自 2017 年上线以来已有五百万付费订阅，去年完成一亿美元 C 轮融资、估值十一亿美元。2023 年《大西洋月刊》曾报道平台上存在大量纳粹和白人至上主义通讯，两百多名作者联名抗议其放任的内容审核政策，三年后争议平息，平台照旧不做内容审核。
The newsSubstack cofounder Hamish McKenzie has published a memoir-manifesto, How to Save the Media, and gave an interview to Vanity Fair. Launched in 2017, the platform now has five million paid subscriptions and closed a $100 million Series C round last year at a $1.1 billion valuation. In 2023 The Atlantic reported scores of Nazi and white-supremacist newsletters on Substack and over 200 writers signed an open letter against its hands-off moderation; three years on, the outcry has dissipated and the policy stands.
他卖的不是写作，是解释权的一份股权
He isn't selling writing. He's selling equity in the right to explain.

麦肯齐在采访里反复讲一个词：直接关系。作者直接连读者，读者直接付钱，绕开广告和社交平台。听起来是给写作者松绑，其实是给平台自己发了牌照——关系建在谁的地基上，谁就收租。

Substack 抽成 10%。2023 年《大西洋月刊》翻出平台上成堆的白人至上主义、新邦联和明确纳粹倾向的通讯，其中一些平台照抽这 10%。两百多名作者联名反对它放任不管的内容审核，一批人搬走。三年过去，麦肯齐的说法是：强硬审查往往会反噬。这句话本身没错，但它被用在这里，是把「我不审核」包装成一种原则，而原则的账单是别人付的。

这就是第三章那套东西：夺取认知入口，然后夺取解释权，最后夺取「什么是事实」的制造权。Substack 不生产叙事，它出租生产叙事的场地，并且对场地上长出来的东西宣布中立。中立不是没有立场，中立是把立场外包给流量。纳粹通讯留在平台上，不是因为平台相信言论自由，是因为删掉它们要付出代价，而留着它们，代价由被这些通讯点名的人承担。

更值得看的是它和旧媒体的关系。麦肯齐在书里写 2021 年在洛杉矶为巴里·韦斯（Bari Weiss）捧场的那晚，把洛杉矶记者俱乐部的表彰读成「直接关系模式」的验证。韦斯从《纽约时报》出走，在 Substack 建起 The Free Press，去年被任命为 CBS 新闻的总编辑。麦肯齐自己说，独立仍然能换来声望。这句话把整件事说透了：Substack 不是旧媒体的替代品，是旧媒体声望的一条新通道。作者带着在旧体制里攒下的名气过来，平台把名气变现，再把变现的一部分换回旧体制的位子。

所以「拯救媒体」这个书名是一个 provocation，麦肯齐自己承认。它真正的主张要小得多：把订阅费从平台手里挪到写作者手里，顺便把平台自己挪进媒体的核心。五百万付费订阅、十一亿美元估值，是这个挪动的成绩单。至于那些被纳粹通讯盯上的读者，他们没有出现在这本书的任何一页里。

麦肯齐说他不觉得几百年的读写进步会一夜之间被抹掉，文化危机在于分发机制。他说对了一半。分发机制确实在变，但变的方向不是让更多人被看见，是让定价权换一批人拿着。旧媒体用广告主给内容定价，Substack 用订阅数给内容定价，两套账本，同一件事：谁付钱，谁决定什么算数。

韦斯去了 CBS，麦肯齐在读《安娜·卡列尼娜》。平台继续不审核，抽成继续抽。那两百多个签联名信的作者里，有多少人还留在上面，报道没写。

McKenzie keeps returning to one phrase: direct relationships. Writer to reader, reader to writer, no ads, no social platforms in between. It sounds like liberation for writers. It's a license for the platform—whoever owns the ground the relationship stands on collects the rent.

Substack takes 10%. In 2023 The Atlantic found scores of white-supremacist, neo-Confederate, and explicitly Nazi newsletters on the platform, and in some cases Substack was taking its standard cut from them. Over 200 writers signed an open letter against its hands-off moderation; several high-profile writers and publications left. Three years later, McKenzie's line is that heavy-handed censorship can backfire. True enough as a general statement. Used here, it turns “we don't moderate” into a principle—and someone else pays the bill for that principle.

This is the weaponization playbook: seize the cognitive entry point, then the right of interpretation, then the right to manufacture what counts as fact. Substack doesn't produce narratives. It rents out the ground where narratives get produced and declares itself neutral about what grows there. Neutrality isn't the absence of a position. It's outsourcing the position to traffic. Nazi newsletters stay up not because the platform believes in free speech but because removing them costs something, and leaving them up costs other people.

The more revealing part is Substack's relationship to legacy media. McKenzie writes about a 2021 evening at the Biltmore in Los Angeles, there to support Bari Weiss, and reads her Los Angeles Press Club honor as validation of the direct-relationships model. Weiss left The New York Times, built The Free Press on Substack, and last year was appointed editor in chief of CBS News. McKenzie says independence can still deliver prestige. That sentence explains the whole arrangement: Substack isn't a replacement for legacy media, it's a new pipe for legacy prestige. Writers arrive with reputations earned inside the old system, the platform converts that reputation into revenue, and part of the revenue converts back into old-system seats.

So “Save the Media” is a provocation, as McKenzie admits. The actual claim is smaller: move subscription money from platforms to writers, and move the platform itself into the core of the media industry. Five million paid subscriptions and a $1.1 billion valuation are the receipts. The readers targeted by Nazi newsletters don't appear anywhere in the book.

McKenzie says he doesn't see centuries of progress in reading and writing undone overnight, and that the cultural crisis is really about distribution. He's half right. Distribution is changing—but not toward more people being seen. Toward a different set of hands holding the pricing power. Legacy media let advertisers price content; Substack lets subscriber counts price it. Two sets of books, same operation: whoever pays decides what counts.

Weiss went to CBS. McKenzie is working through Anna Karenina. The platform keeps not moderating, and the 10% keeps coming. How many of those 200-plus signatories are still publishing there—the article doesn't say.

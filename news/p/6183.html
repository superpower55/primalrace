撞色回潮，谁在为快乐定价Color Blocking Is Back. Who's Pricing the Joy?

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 The Cut ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
品味没有高下，定价权有。
Taste has no hierarchy. Pricing does.

去年还是quiet luxury，今年就成了撞色。The Cut的编辑写道，时尚圈最爱的那些人穿着彩色出门"sparked joy"，于是六位编辑亲身试穿，证明连一年到头只穿黑的人也爱上了这个结果。这是一条关于趋势的新闻，也是一条关于谁有权宣布什么算"高级"的新闻。

quiet luxury那套东西的机制我写过：logo越小越贵、面料越素越高级、能看出牌子就是俗。它不是审美，是筛选——用一套需要文化资本才能读懂的表达，把人群分成"懂"和"不懂"。撞色回潮不是这套逻辑的终结，是它的下一季。同一批runway、同一批媒体、同一批品牌，去年卖你"素"，今年卖你"艳"。变的不是谁更自由，是货架上该清什么库存。

真正值得看的是这篇稿子怎么把"快乐"写成一种可以购买的东西。Carolina Herrera——不对，是Carol Lee——说她今年突然想穿亮红亮粉亮蓝，"brought both joy (who knew?)"，现在身上没有非中性色就"a bit dull and lifeless"。Emma Childs说粉色让她的心唱歌，把这叫作"the latent girlie girl in me"。这些话不是假的，快乐是真的。但快乐被安放在衣服上、被安放在这一季的色卡上，就成了一个入口：你不够快乐，是因为你还没有那件泡泡糖粉的紧身裤。

Hanna Flanagan那段最诚实，也最说明问题。她说自己"very rarely wear color"，所以"played it safe"，选了毛衣和pull-on pants；最难的是配饰，"if you don't match the other colors perfectly, it looks all wrong"。撞色这件事的门槛不在颜色，在搭配的规则——而规则的定价权不在穿的人手里。她最后靠一双斑马纹鞋"act as a neutral"来救场，等于承认：所谓大胆的表达，仍然要在一套隐形的评分表里拿分。

Bianca Nieves那句"channel a little Ronald McDonald"是全文唯一一次自己拆自己的台。她怕像快餐吉祥物，于是去Versace的runway找灵感，"leaned into a more rock-and-roll aesthetic"。一个编辑要靠一场秀来确认自己不像小丑，这就是品味政治的全部：不是你喜欢什么，是你喜欢的东西能不能被认出来是"对的"。

六个人，六种身材，六种"personal style"，稿子特意强调body types不同——这是进步，至少不是只有一种身体被允许示范时尚。但六个人穿的都是这一季被宣布为"back"的东西，没有一个人问：如果撞色明年又被宣布"dead"，这些快乐去哪了。

The Cut的编辑们没有做错什么，她们只是在一个自己也被定价的系统里工作。真正的问题在稿子外面：谁决定quiet luxury死了、撞色回来了，谁从这条时间线里赚钱，谁在换季时被告知自己去年买的那批"高级"已经过时。品味本来没有高下，是有人需要它有高下，才能一直卖下去。

冬天要来了，稿子说趁seasonal depression发作前把亮色买进衣柜。这个建议很温柔。只是 depression 不会因为一件紫毛衣消失，而卖毛衣的人知道这一点。

Last year it was quiet luxury. This year it's color blocking. The Cut's editors report that fashion's favorite people stepped out in color and it "sparked joy," so six editors tried the trend themselves and found that even the ones who live in black loved the result. This is a story about a trend. It is also a story about who gets to declare what counts as "good."

Quiet luxury ran on a simple mechanism: smaller logos cost more, plainer fabric reads as higher, a visible brand is vulgar. That isn't aesthetics, it's filtering — an expression you need cultural capital to decode, sorting people into those who get it and those who don't. Color blocking's return doesn't end that logic. It's the next season of it. Same runways, same magazines, same brands. Last year they sold you restraint; this year they sell you saturation. What changed isn't who is free. It's what needs to move off the shelves.

What's worth watching is how the piece turns "joy" into something purchasable. Carol Lee says she suddenly wanted bright reds and pinks and blues this year, that it "brought both joy (who knew?)", and that now she feels "a bit dull and lifeless" without a non-neutral on her body. Emma Childs says pink makes her heart sing, calling it "the latent girlie girl in me." None of this is fake. The joy is real. But once joy is anchored to a garment, to this season's color card, it becomes an entrance: you are not happy enough because you don't yet own the bubblegum tights.

Hanna Flanagan's section is the most honest and the most revealing. She "very rarely wear[s] color," so she "played it safe" with a sweater and pull-on pants; the hard part is accessories, because "if you don't match the other colors perfectly, it looks all wrong." The barrier to color blocking isn't color. It's the rules of combination — and the power to set those rules doesn't sit with the person getting dressed. She rescues the look with zebra-print shoes that "act as a neutral," which is an admission: even bold expression still has to score on an invisible rubric.

Bianca Nieves's line about channeling "a little Ronald McDonald" is the only moment the piece undercuts itself. Afraid of looking like a fast-food mascot, she goes to the Versace runway and leans "into a more rock-and-roll aesthetic." An editor needing a fashion show to confirm she doesn't look like a clown — that is the whole politics of taste. Not what you like, but whether what you like can be recognized as correct.

Six people, six body types, six "personal styles" — the piece makes a point of the different body types, and that is progress; at least one kind of body isn't the only one allowed to model fashion. But all six are wearing what this season has declared "back," and not one asks where the joy goes if color blocking is declared dead next year.

The Cut's editors did nothing wrong. They work inside a system that prices them too. The real question is outside the piece: who decided quiet luxury is dead and color blocking is back, who profits from that timeline, and who gets told in a season that whatever they bought last year as "elevated" is now over.

Taste has no hierarchy. Someone needs it to have one, or there's nothing to keep selling.

Winter is coming, the piece says — get the bright colors into your closet before the seasonal depression hits. It's a gentle suggestion. Depression will not disappear because of a purple sweater. The people selling the sweater know that.

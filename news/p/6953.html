战争账单从不寄给开战的人The War Bill Never Goes to the People Who Start It

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要美国国防部长赫格塞思向国会表示，美国迄今在伊朗战争上已花费约375亿美元，并请求追加670亿美元紧急拨款。报道称，这场战争还有未计入的隐性成本：自开战以来，美国人已多付逾750亿美元汽油和柴油费用，每户超过570美元；霍尔木兹海峡关闭推高化肥价格，约七成美国农民表示买不起所需的全部化肥。
The newsDefense Secretary Pete Hegseth told Congress the US has spent an estimated $37.5 billion on the Iran war and requested an additional $67 billion in emergency funding. The report counts hidden costs: Americans have paid over $75 billion more for gasoline and diesel since the war began, more than $570 per household, and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz has pushed fertilizer prices up, with about 70 percent of US farmers saying they cannot afford all they need.
375亿是首付，剩下的由每户人家分期付。
$37.5 billion is a down payment. Households pay the rest in installments.

375亿美元不是这场战争的账单，是首付。赫格塞思在国会报出这个数字，同时再要670亿紧急拨款——他报的是国防部那本账。真正在付钱的人不在国会听证室里，在加油站、在超市货架前、在公立学校的电费单上。

报道里有一组数字值得盯住：开战以来，美国人已经多付了超过750亿美元的汽油和柴油钱，每户超过570美元。这是布朗大学气候解决方案实验室实时追踪出来的数字，比五角大楼报的战争开支多一倍。柴油涨价，卡车运着美国七成以上的货物，于是货架上几乎所有东西都跟着涨，包括食品杂货。石油还是塑料和石化产品的原料，从毛绒玩具、尿布、婴儿湿巾到蜡笔、瓶装水、软饮料、衣服，价格都在往上走。这条链子上没有一个环节是「战争成本」，但每一环都在收战争税。

这就是结构暴力的标准形态：没有施暴者站在你面前，没有人拿枪逼你多付那570美元，账单弥散在几亿次交易里，慢，日常，被当成「物价就是这样」。加尔通的公式在这里很好用—— Violence = Potential − Actual。本来可以不发生的涨价、本来可以不被挪走的学校能源预算、本来买得起化肥的农民，差额就是暴力。它不需要一个凶手，只需要一套让代价自动流向不特定多数人的分配机制。

这套机制挑人挑得很准。公立学校每年能源开支超过80亿美元，是仅次于教师工资的第二大支出，公用事业费一涨，本来就紧巴巴的学校更紧。约七成美国农民说买不起所需的全部化肥，霍尔木兹海峡一关，全球化肥价格飞涨——美国这边最终是食品涨价，美国之外，联合国警告这场战争正在把全球饥饿推到历史水平，世界粮食计划署负责人说，他们被迫「从饥饿的人那里拿，给快饿死的人」。战争的定价权在华盛顿，付款顺序却是从最买不起的人开始排。

报道还点了一句人命：至少18名美国军人死亡，100多人受伤；仅伊朗一国，人权组织记录了至少1700名平民死亡，整个地区可能还要多几千。这些数字在五角大楼的账本里是「已发生」，在往后几十年的账本里是抚恤、医疗和伤残支付——历史上这类成本要到战争结束后30到40年才见顶。武器库存要补，中东的美军设施要重建，报道说损坏程度比特朗普政府承认的更严重。

所以这个「375亿」的性质要说清楚：它不是战争的费用，是让战争看起来便宜的那道包装。把成本切成「国防开支」和「其他一切」，公众看到的就只有前一块，后一块散进油价、运费、化肥、学费电费里，谁都感觉不到自己在为一场战争付钱。这是表达武器化里最朴素的一种——不是撒谎，是换一种记账方式，让事实以另一副面孔出现。

谁在这场记账里得利？军火商拿到订单，追加的670亿有去处；开战的政治决策者拿到「成本可控」的叙事；而「从饥饿的人那里拿」的那部分代价，落在没有任何代表在国会里替他们说话的人身上。共谋不需要密谋，只需要每个人都接着用那本切好的账本说话。

最后一句留给那笔还没被算进去的钱：报道说，这一切加起来，375亿离最终数字还差得远。差的那部分，正在被按月扣走。

$37.5 billion is not the bill for this war. It is the down payment. Hegseth gave Congress that figure and asked for another $67 billion in emergency funding—he is reporting the Pentagon's ledger. The people actually paying are not in that hearing room. They are at the gas pump, in the grocery aisle, on the public school's utility bill.

One set of numbers in the report deserves a hard look: since the war began, Americans have paid more than $75 billion extra for gasoline and diesel, over $570 per household, according to a live tracker from Brown University's Climate Solutions Lab. That is more than double what the Pentagon reports as war spending. Diesel gets more expensive, trucks carry more than 70 percent of goods in the US, and almost everything on the shelf rises with it, groceries included. Oil also makes plastics and petrochemicals, so prices are already moving into stuffed animals, diapers, baby wipes, crayons, bottled water, soft drinks, clothing. Not one link in that chain is labeled "war cost," and every link collects a war tax.

This is structural violence in its standard form: no one stands in front of you, no one puts a gun to your head for that $570. The bill is diffused across hundreds of millions of transactions—slow, ordinary, absorbed as "prices are just like this." Galtung's formula fits: Violence = Potential − Actual. The price increase that need not have happened, the school energy budget that need not have been squeezed, the farmer who could afford fertilizer—the gap is the violence. It needs no perpetrator, only an allocation mechanism that lets the cost flow automatically to unspecified people.

The mechanism picks its targets well. K-12 schools spend more than $8 billion a year on energy, their second-largest expense after teacher salaries; when utility costs rise, already-stressed schools get tighter. About 70 percent of US farmers say they cannot afford all the fertilizer they need, and with the Strait of Hormuz closed, fertilizer prices have skyrocketed. In the US that eventually means higher food prices. Outside it, the UN warns the war is driving historic levels of global hunger, and the head of the World Food Programme says the organization is being forced to "take from the hungry to give to the starving." The pricing power sits in Washington; the payment order starts with those least able to pay.

The report also counts lives: at least 18 US service members killed and more than 100 wounded; in Iran alone, human rights groups have documented at least 1,700 civilian deaths, with the regional toll likely thousands more. In the Pentagon's ledger these are "incurred." In the decades-long ledger they are care and disability payments—historically these costs do not peak until 30 to 40 years after a war ends. Stockpiles need replenishing, US facilities in the Middle East need rebuilding, and the damage is reportedly more extensive than the Trump administration has acknowledged.

So name what the $37.5 billion actually is: not the cost of the war, but the packaging that makes the war look cheap. Split the cost into "defense spending" and "everything else," and the public sees only the first box. The second dissolves into fuel, freight, fertilizer, school electricity—and no one feels they are paying for a war. This is the plainest form of weaponized expression: not lying, but changing the bookkeeping so the fact appears in a different face.

Who profits from this accounting? Arms manufacturers get orders—the extra $67 billion has somewhere to go. The political decision-makers get the narrative of a "contained cost." And the part paid by "taking from the hungry" lands on people with no representative in that room. Conspiracy needs no secret meeting; it only needs everyone to keep speaking from the pre-split ledger.

One last line for the money not yet counted: taken all together, the report says, $37.5 billion is nowhere near a final number. What is missing is being deducted monthly.

推土机碾过的不是荒地What the Bulldozer Turned Over

国际 结构层 · 文化层 ProPublica ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
法律被豁免之后，剩下的只有推土机。
Once the law is waived, only the bulldozer is left.

推土机挖出人的遗骨，这件事本身就已经说明问题：它挖出的地方，本来就不该挖。

Organ Pipe 的这具遗骨在 9 月 8 日被发现，亚利桑那大学的评估确认这是人类遗骸，与部落的火葬传统相符。89 岁的 Lorraine Eiler 在八月烈日下对着记者警告过：推土机一来，祖坟就保不住。十五天后，她的警告应验了。

这里最要紧的一点是：这不是意外，也不是某个承包商的失误。这是被法律豁免过的结果。2001 年之后，国土安全部部长获得了豁免任何妨碍边境墙建设的法律的权力。二十年来这项权力被用了 66 次，其中 60 次在特朗普任内。2019 年针对 Organ Pipe 的那次豁免，一次性搁置了四十多部法律——包括《美洲原住民墓葬保护与归还法》（NAGPRA）本身。

NAGPRA 是一部什么法律？它在 1990 年承认了这个国家长期亵渎原住民墓葬的历史，要求把祖先遗骸和文物归还部落。它是文化层的一次修正：承认“这片土地没有主人”是一句谎话。然后 DHS 把它从清单上划掉了。

这就是加尔通那个公式的具体形状：Potential 是遗骨被留在土里、部落继续祭拜；Actual 是遗骨被翻出来，部落不敢重新下葬，因为不知道还会挖出什么。差额就是暴力。而让这个差额合法化的那一步，发生在文化层——用“边境安全”这个叙事，把“亵渎祖坟”重新定义成“必要的代价”。文化暴力做的事，就是让结构暴力看起来不像暴力。

但结构层也在动，而且动得很具体。62 英里、13 亿美元的墙要穿过 Tohono O'odham 的全部领地，部落正在联邦法院起诉，在土地上立了“禁止入内”的牌子。DHS 的回应是派蒙面联邦探员给施工队当护卫，对部落成员进行空中监视。CBP 的负责人把保留地描述成“西南边境最危险的走私和人口贩运走廊之一”——一个以保护为名的叙事入口，用来正当化对这片土地的主人的军事化管控。

注意这个顺序：先给这片土地贴上“危险”的标签，再豁免保护它的法律，然后推土机进场。三步走完，遗骨出土只是时间问题。

报道里最锋利的一句话来自 David Martínez：“那些遗骸本来就不该被打扰。”他接着说，无论政府或企业要建什么，工程总会继续，这让人感到“空”。这个“空”很准确——不是愤怒，是知道程序会走完、遗骨会被“尊重地归还”、然后推土机继续往前开的那种空。公园管理局说正在“努力确认文化归属部落，确保遗骸得到尊重归还”。话说得很得体。但 2019 年的豁免至今有效，去年又发了第二份豁免，律师 Dinah Bear 说豁免没有终止日期，法律条文里也没有任何东西把豁免和具体项目绑定。也就是说：归还遗骨的程序在走，挖出遗骨的许可也在走。

Eiler 的家族决定暂缓重新安葬，等这一带的施工停下来再说。这个决定本身就是对官方承诺的评估——他们不相信工程会停。

还有一层没被写进任何豁免清单：Quitobaquito Springs 边上的墓地埋着至少两个家族、34 座坟。那里是美国境内唯一已知的 Hia-Ced O'odham 村落遗址。推土机现在离它很近。

Eiler 说，这种事不只发生在那里，全美国都在发生，而没多少人说话。这句话是这篇报道里唯一没有被任何机构回应的部分——公园管理局回应了，CBP 回应了，国会议员回应了，没有人回应“全美国都在发生”。

A bulldozer turned up human remains. That sentence already contains the whole problem: the ground it was turning should never have been turned.

The remains at Organ Pipe were found on Sept. 8. The University of Arizona confirmed they were human and consistent with tribal cremation practices. Lorraine Eiler, 89, had stood in the August sun and warned reporters that if the bulldozers came, her family's and her tribe's resting places would be disturbed. Fifteen days later, it happened.

This was not an accident, and not a contractor's mistake. It was the product of a legal waiver. After 2001, the Secretary of Homeland Security gained the power to waive any law that might slow border wall construction. In two decades the power was invoked 66 times — 60 of them under Trump. The 2019 waiver for Organ Pipe set aside more than 40 laws at once, including the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act itself.

What is NAGPRA? A 1990 law that admitted this country had spent generations desecrating Native burials, and required that ancestral remains be returned to tribes. It was a correction at the cultural layer: an official admission that "this land had no owners" was a lie. Then DHS crossed it off the list.

This is Galtung's formula in concrete form. Potential: the remains stay in the ground and the tribe keeps its ceremonies. Actual: the remains are pulled up, and the family holds off reburying them because they don't know what else will surface. The gap is the violence. And the step that made the gap legal happened at the cultural layer — "border security" as a narrative that redefines desecration as a necessary cost. Cultural violence is what makes structural violence stop looking like violence.

But the structural layer is moving too, very concretely. A $1.3 billion, 62-mile wall is slated to cross the entirety of the Tohono O'odham Nation. The tribe is fighting it in federal court and has posted no-trespassing signs. DHS responded by sending masked federal agents to escort work crews and conducting aerial surveillance of tribal members. The head of CBP justified the wall by calling the reservation "one of the most dangerous smuggling and trafficking corridors on the Southwest border" — a protection narrative used to legitimize militarized control over the people who live there.

Note the order: label the land dangerous, waive the laws that protect it, send in the bulldozers. Once those three steps are done, unearthing graves is only a matter of time.

The sharpest line in the story belongs to David Martínez: those remains shouldn't have been disturbed in the first place. Whatever the government or the corporate world is building, he says, the construction continues, and it leaves people feeling "a little hollow." That word is precise. Not rage — the hollow knowledge that the protocol will run its course, the remains will be "respectfully returned," and the bulldozers will keep going.

The Park Service says it is working diligently to identify the affiliated tribe and ensure respectful return. Well put. But the 2019 waiver is still in effect, a second waiver was issued last year, and as lawyer Dinah Bear notes, the waivers have no end date and nothing in the statute ties them to a particular project. The return protocol runs. So does the permit to dig.

Eiler's family decided to hold off reburying the newly unearthed remains until construction in the area stops. That decision is itself an assessment of official promises: they don't believe the work will stop.

And one thing appears on no waiver list: the cemetery at Quitobaquito Springs holds at least two families and 34 graves. It sits beside the only known Hia-Ced O'odham village site in the United States. The crews are working close by.

Eiler said this is not only happening down there — it's happening all over the United States, and nobody says much about it. That is the one line in the story no institution answered. The Park Service answered. CBP answered. A congresswoman answered. Nobody answered "all over the United States."

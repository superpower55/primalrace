贴纸上的假字，谁在定价Garbled Text on a Sticker, and Who Sets the Price

文娱 文化层 404 Media ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要2026年美国中期选举投票后，作者所在的小镇发放的「I Voted」贴纸是AI生成的，图案杂乱、文字错乱；而邻镇、密歇根、肯塔基、路易斯安那等地仍发放由学生设计或本地艺术家制作的贴纸。作者认为这类低质AI产物不断累积，会让人对AI的在场逐渐麻木。
The newsAfter voting in 2026, the author's town handed out an AI-generated "I Voted" sticker with garbled text and a cluttered design, while nearby towns and states like Michigan and Louisiana still print stickers by students or local artists. The author argues such low-quality AI artifacts pile up until people stop noticing AI at all.
连「我投了票」都交给机器代写，民主的表达先贬值了
When even "I Voted" is machine-written, the expression of democracy gets devalued first.

一张贴纸上的字印错了，本来不值得写。但这次错的地方很讲究：错在「I Voted」旁边那行没人读得出来的「CEKEVPATIVG EDELDOA」。

表达从来不只传递信息，它确立你是谁。贴纸贴到胸口，是投票人对自己身份的一次宣告：我参与了，别人看得见。这个宣告要成立，前提是它被人认真做出来。密歇根让学生投稿，路易斯安那请本地艺术家画，新奥尔良的贴纸上是一只手绘小号吹出「I Voted」，旁边挂着狂欢节珠链——那是有人在为「我投了票」这件事定价，定的是认真。

AI slop 的定价方式是另一种：零成本，随便生成，错字留着也没人管。作者说自己的贴纸「丑陋」，说贴上去时「不自豪」，最后没发到网上。这不是审美抱怨，这是表达的贬值——当宣告变成一坨青铜色的糊，宣告的人也跟着变成糊的一部分。

更值得盯的是这套机制：用生成工具替代人工，省下的那点钱不流向任何人，只流向「反正没人细看」。这个逻辑和华为的 τ law 是同一个东西——做不出小芯片，就宣布一条新定律，把尺子换掉。这里换掉的尺子是「贴纸该由人认真设计」这条默认，换成「能印出来就行」。能力没变，评价方式变了。

作者自己也知道这事小：「AI 在战区选目标」的时代，抱怨贴纸显得可笑。但他的结论我不同意一半。他说这些小烦恼堆积起来，直到你对 AI 无处不在不再察觉。对，但堆积的不是烦恼，是标准。今天你容忍贴纸上的错字，明天你容忍 ChatGPT 写的传单，后天你不再问这份东西是谁做的——解释权就这么一格一格让出去，没有哪一格值得单独抗议。

他画的那条线也画错了位置。他说「我划线在 AI 生成的 I Voted 贴纸上」，听起来像个人的品味洁癖。真正的线不在贴纸，在「谁决定这张贴纸长什么样」——是本地学生、本地艺术家，还是某个没人认识的供应商点了一下生成。他邻镇拿到好贴纸，不是运气，是有人还在坚持让人的手来决定这件事。

最后一句刺留给他自己：他收到朋友那张漂亮的南卡贴纸，气得发抖，然后呢？他没写自己有没有去镇公所问一句这贴纸是谁做的。贴纸可以扔掉，但那个把「我投了票」外包给机器的小镇，明年还会再印一批。

A sticker with a typo on it isn't worth writing about. But the typo is well placed: it's the line next to "I Voted" that reads, as best anyone can tell, "CEKEVPATIVG EDELDOA."

Expression was never just communication. It establishes who you are. The sticker on your chest is a declaration: I took part, and you can see it. That declaration only works if someone made the thing with care. Michigan runs a student contest, Louisiana commissions local artists, and a New Orleans sticker shows a hand-drawn trumpet blasting "I Voted" beside Mardi Gras beads. Someone was setting a price on "I voted" — the price of taking it seriously.

AI slop sets a different price: zero cost, generate anything, leave the garbled text in. The author calls his sticker ugly, says he wasn't proud putting it on, and never posted it. That isn't an aesthetic complaint. It's expression being devalued — when the declaration turns into a bronze smear, the person declaring turns into part of the smear.

The mechanism is worth watching. Replacing a designer with a generator doesn't send the savings anywhere except into "nobody will look closely." That's the same move as Huawei's τ law: can't build the small chip, so declare a new law and swap the ruler. Here the swapped ruler is the default that a sticker should be designed by a person; the new one is "if it prints, it's fine." The capability didn't change. The way we measure it did.

The author knows the stakes are small: in an era when AI picks targets in war zones, complaining about stickers looks silly. But I only half agree with his conclusion. He says these small annoyances pile up until you stop noticing AI everywhere. Right — except what piles up isn't annoyance. It's the standard. Today you tolerate garbled text on a sticker, tomorrow a ChatGPT flyer, the day after you stop asking who made a thing at all. The right to explain the world gets handed over one notch at a time, and no single notch is worth protesting.

He also draws his line in the wrong place. He says he draws it at AI-generated "I Voted" stickers, which sounds like personal taste. The real line isn't the sticker; it's who decides what the sticker looks like — a local student, a local artist, or some unknown vendor clicking generate. His neighboring town got good stickers not by luck but because someone still insists a human hand decides.

One last jab, for him: he got his friend's beautiful South Carolina sticker, trembled with indignation — and then what? He doesn't write whether he ever asked town hall who made his. You can throw the sticker away. The town that outsourced "I Voted" to a machine will print another batch next year.

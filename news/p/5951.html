他们要的不是安全，是保险They Didn't Want Security. They Wanted Insurance.

国际 结构层 · 元暴力 ProPublica ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
先拆掉守门人，再索要钥匙
Dismantle the watchdog, then demand the keys.

国土安全部的政治任命者要求 CISA 秘密搞一台投票机来黑，这个请求本身不奇怪，奇怪的是它发生在 CISA 被拆掉三分之一之后。一个被砍掉一千多人、预算再削七亿、连监测选举网络的传感器都停了的机构，被要求执行一次秘密行动——这不是要它干活，是要它背锅。

那位前官员说得够直白：留着，随时可以拿出来，作废任何他们不喜欢的选举结果。这句话把整件事的性质说完了。投票机被黑出来的漏洞不是用来补的，是用来在需要的时候宣布「看，果然有问题」的。CISA 的标准做法是发现问题就通知厂商，这次被明确要求不要通知。一个不通知厂商的漏洞，唯一用途就是留在口袋里。

这就是第三章那套东西：夺取认知入口，然后夺取「什么是事实」的制造权。他们不需要真的黑进一台机器，他们需要的是一个可以在十一月之后拿出来说的故事。而故事要成立，得先有一个愿意配合的机构。CISA 不配合，所以先把它拆了。

拆的顺序很讲究。先裁掉能和州选举官员对接的人，再停掉监测服务器异常的技术，再把各州当年按保密承诺交上来的原始通讯记录解密公开。最后这一步是关键：信息共享的全部前提是保密，保密一破，各州就不再交东西。亚利桑那州务卿说得很清楚，他不信了。一个拿不到情报的 CISA，就算还在，也等于不存在。

然后九月底，CISA 发布了十三页的选举安全计划，说要「重新致力于法定使命」。缅因州务卿的回应是：太少了，太晚了。计划里写着要和那些被它抛弃了一年的州「 leveraging collaborative partnerships 」。合作的前提是双方都还在，现在一边已经把另一边的东西拿去喂了政治叙事。

这里能看清共谋者理论的实际运作。不是有人在密室里下令摧毁选举安全，而是一串各自合理的选择叠起来：裁员的部门只管省钱，解密的人只管「透明」，新任代理局长是弗吉尼亚共和党的区代表，还在讲「对抗民主党重划选区」。每个人都只做了自己那一小块，合起来就是一个空壳机构加一份准时发布的计划书。回报也很清楚：一个空壳的 CISA 不会在十一月说「这次选举是美国历史上最安全的」。2020 年它说过，然后局长被推特开除。

有意思的是那场南达科他的招待会。几十个州务卿盯着手机等总统宣布国家紧急状态、准备没收投票机，结果听到的是一堆早就被驳烂的旧claim，大家笑一声，放下手机去酒吧了。这说明这套叙事对专业人士已经失效。失效不等于无害——正因为它对懂行的人失效，它才更需要一个「官方机构」来背书，哪怕这个机构只剩十三页纸。

真正被削掉的东西在结构层：联邦政府帮各州检测外国行为者情报的能力，州里补不上。传感器可以自己买，外国政府在想什么，一个州买不到。这个差额不会在十一月自动显形，它会在某次真正的攻击发生时显形，而到那时候，需要被解释的不是漏洞，是为什么没人看见。

至于那个「秘密搞一台机器来黑」的请求最后没被执行，报道给的理由是能执行这种行动的人刚好被 DOGE 裁了。一个想搞政治干预的政府，被自己的削减预算挡住了。这不是制度起了作用，这是巧合起了作用。下一次，人可能还在。

Political appointees at Homeland Security asked CISA to secretly obtain a voting machine and hack it. The request itself isn't the strange part. The strange part is that it came after CISA had already been gutted by a third.

A agency that has lost over a thousand people, faces another $700 million cut, and has stopped funding the sensors that watch election networks is now being asked to run a covert operation. That isn't a request for work. That's a request for a fall guy.

One former official put it plainly: keep it in their back pocket, to invalidate any result they don't like. That sentence settles the nature of the whole thing. A vulnerability found in a voting machine isn't meant to be patched here. It's meant to be produced on demand — see, we told you there was a problem. CISA's standard practice is to notify the vendor. This time they were told not to. A flaw nobody is allowed to fix has exactly one use: staying in the pocket.

This is Chapter 3 operating in plain sight. Seize the cognitive entry point, then seize the power to manufacture what counts as fact. They don't need to actually hack a machine. They need a story they can tell after November. And for the story to hold, they need an agency willing to play along. CISA wouldn't. So CISA was taken apart first.

The order of demolition is telling. First the people who liaised with state election officials. Then the technology that monitored servers for intrusions. Then the declassification of raw communications that states had handed over under a promise of confidentiality. That last step is the key one: information sharing rests entirely on confidentiality. Break it, and the states stop sharing. Arizona's secretary of state says he doesn't trust them anymore. A CISA that receives nothing is a CISA that doesn't exist, even if its letterhead does.

Then in late September, CISA published a 13-page election security plan declaring it had "recommitted to its statutory mission." Maine's secretary of state called it too little, too late. The plan speaks of "leveraging collaborative partnerships" with the very states the agency abandoned for a year. Partnership requires two parties still standing. One of them has been feeding the other's data into a political narrative.

This is the co-conspirators theory doing actual work. Nobody in a back room ordered election security destroyed. A string of individually reasonable choices stacked up: the downsizing office was saving money, the declassifiers were being "transparent," the new acting director is a Virginia GOP district representative who gave a speech called "Fighting Democrat Redistricting." Each person did their small piece. Together they produced an empty agency and a plan delivered on schedule. The payoff is clear too: a hollow CISA will not say in November that this was the most secure election in American history. It said that in 2020, and its director was fired over Twitter.

The reception in the Black Hills is the interesting part. Dozens of secretaries of state sat staring at their phones, waiting for the president to declare a national emergency and move on voting machines. What they got was a pile of long-debunked claims. They laughed, put their phones away, and went to the bar. The narrative has lost its grip on the professionals. Losing its grip doesn't make it harmless — precisely because the people who know better don't buy it, it needs an official agency to bless it, even if that agency is down to thirteen pages.

What was actually cut is structural: the federal capability to give states intelligence on foreign actors. States can buy their own sensors. A state cannot buy knowledge of what a foreign government is thinking. That gap won't show up in November on its own schedule. It will show up the day a real attack lands, and by then the thing needing explanation won't be the vulnerability. It will be why nobody saw it coming.

The secret request was never carried out, and the reported reason is that the people capable of carrying it out had just been laid off by DOGE. A government that wanted political interference was blocked by its own budget cuts. That isn't the system working. That's coincidence working. Next time, the people might still be there.

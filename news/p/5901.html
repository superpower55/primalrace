买不动的那张选票The Vote They Couldn't Buy

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
他们买得下签名公司，买不下签名的人。
They could buy the signature firm. Not the people signing.

先把这场仗的账算清楚：反对 Prop 40 的一方，三个委员会合计收到超过 3.795 亿美元，是加州历史上最贵的反对campaign。十月头两周就花了 4280 万，平均每天超过 250 万。支持方靠什么呢——网红、演员、Bernie Sanders 的集会，还有一群志愿者上门收签名。这不是一场势均力敌的辩论，是一次用钱把辩论本身买下来的尝试。

钱花在哪儿最能说明问题。加州任何公民只要凑够签名就能把提案送上选票，签名公司按件收费，价格随需求浮动。反对方于是自己推好几个提案出来，把单价从 4 美元一路抬到创纪录的 36 美元。Conway 在播客里把话说得很白：目标是让 Gavin 去谈判，让它上不了选票，所以也许他们拿不到签名。SEIU-UHW 改用志愿者，凑出 155 万个签名，接近所需数量的两倍。然后据报道，有人打电话给签名公司老板 Fred Kimball，问卖不卖。Signal 群里一句话：商业上遇到障碍，买下来就是了。

这是第三章的教科书案例，只是武器不是广告，是流程本身。武器化的最高形态不是说服你相信什么，是让你连投票的机会都没有。买签名公司、抬签名价格、再推两个“毒丸”提案（41 和 42，只要票数高过 40，就算 40 通过了也不生效）——每一步都不碰观点，只碰程序。程序被买断之后，观点输赢已经不重要了。

再说那场圣诞派对。Marin county，红杉树间的树屋里，Janelle Monáe 唱歌，Newsom 在场，Sergey Brin 也在场。Brin 拦住州长，说这项税逼他离开加州。后来他搬到了太浩湖内华达一侧那栋 4200 万的宅子，离州界几步路。再后来他成了反对campaign 事实上的头目，通过 Building a Better California 至少投了 1.02 亿。一个身家约 2450 亿的人，为了不交那一次性的 5%，花掉一亿多——这笔账本身就在告诉你，他怕的不是钱。

他怕的是传染。报道里 McCuan 说得很清楚，巨额花费部分来自对扩散到其他州的恐惧。六位诺奖经济学家算过：加州亿万富翁的财富合计 2.3 万亿，等于全州两千万纳税人一年的全部收入；过去几年这笔净值涨了近 150%，靠的是 AI 热潮。5% 的一次性税，按他们的说法只是部分追赶。所以这不是“公平不公平”的辩论，是“能不能开始”的辩论。开头一旦开了，别的州就会看。

然后是共谋者。这一层比亿万富翁本人有意思得多。加州医学会、加州教师协会、Planned Parenthood 的游说分支、一批建筑工会和公共安全协会，全站到了反对一边。Becerra 说亿万富翁该多交税，但 Prop 40 不是那个方式；Porter 说这提案不改革医疗系统；Lurie 说这会把收入赶出加州。这些话单听都成立，合起来的效果是：一个能立刻给医院和产科病房输血的提案，被一群进步派用“不够好”的理由按住了。报道里写，反对campaign 给加州民主党以及圣迭戈、橙县、洛杉矶县的民主党党部捐了数万美元——而这些党部最后全都 endorsement 了 Prop 40。钱进了门，票没跟着走，这大概是共谋关系里最尴尬的一种。

真正把局面扳回来的不是名人，是八月那场劳联-产联（AFL-CIO）大会。1300 个工会的代表在场，一个个医院工人站起来讲产科病房、急诊室、要关掉的医院。Gonzalez 的原话是不想当一个向建制低头、怕碰亿万富翁的劳工运动。这是 structural 层的一次真实位移：不是叙事被拆穿，是一个有组织的集体在利益计算之后选择了站到税那边。差额在这里被缩小了一点点，靠的是工会的票，不是 Bernie 的演讲。

还有一件事得说。Regan 是这场运动的建筑师，也是被指控 2009 年把一名前 SEIU 主管 Pugh 推在墙上、踹开她办公室门、骂她“dumb ass”的人。SEIU 今年的调查报告认定这项指控成立，Regan 否认，说这是报复；警方没有调查，没有起诉。Pugh 现在给 Becerra 当高级顾问，同时是 No on 40 的顾问。这里没有干净的一方：一个被认定施暴的男人在推一项救命的税，一个指控他的女人站在出钱反对这项税的一边。报道没写这两件事该怎么放在一起称重，我也不打算替它称。

民调从夏天领先七个百分点掉到一个百分点。八成四的选民说看过广告，多数人没被说动。钱没能改变多数人的判断，但足以把差距磨到一分。11 月 4 日之后，无论 Prop 40 过不过，Brin 的 1.02 亿已经买到了一样东西：让全国看见，一个州想对自己的亿万富翁收 5%，要付出多大的代价才够得着起跑线。

Start with the ledger. The campaign against Proposition 40 has pulled in more than $379.5m across three committees — the most expensive opposition campaign in California history. In the first two weeks of October alone it spent over $42.8m, more than $2.5m a day. The yes side had influencers, actors, a Bernie Sanders rally, and volunteers collecting signatures. This was never a debate between two sides. It was an attempt to buy the debate itself.

Where the money went tells you everything. In California anyone with enough signatures can put a measure on the ballot, and signature firms charge per signature, with the price floating by demand. So the opposition introduced several measures of its own and drove the going rate from $4 to a record $36. Conway said it plainly on a podcast: get Gavin to negotiate it off the ballot, so maybe they don't get the signatures. SEIU-UHW switched to volunteers and turned in 1.55m signatures, nearly double what was needed. Then, reportedly, two calls came in to Fred Kimball asking whether his firm was for sale. One message in the Signal chat: if there's an impediment, just buy it.

This is the weaponization playbook, except the weapon isn't advertising, it's the procedure. The highest form of weaponized expression isn't making you believe something — it's making sure you never get to vote on it. Buying the signature firm, inflating the price per signature, floating two poison-pill countermeasures (41 and 42, which kill Prop 40 if they get more yes votes) — none of it touches an argument. It touches the machinery. Once the machinery is bought, the argument is decoration.

Then the Christmas party. Marin county, a treehouse in a grove of redwoods, Janelle Monáe performing, Newsom in the room, Sergey Brin in the room. Brin confronted the governor: the tax was forcing him to leave California. He later moved to a $42m mansion on the Nevada side of Lake Tahoe, steps from the border. He became the de facto ringleader of the opposition, contributing at least $102m through Building a Better California. A man worth about $245bn spending over a hundred million to avoid a one-time 5% tax is telling you something. It isn't about the money.

It's about contagion. McCuan says the spending partly reflects fear that the idea spreads to other states. Six Nobel-winning economists did the math: California's billionaires hold $2.3tn combined, equal to the entire annual income of all 20m taxpayers in the state, and their net worth has grown nearly 150% in a couple of years on the AI boom. A one-time 5% tax, they wrote, only partially plays catch-up. So this was never a debate about fairness. It's a debate about whether a beginning is allowed. Once one state starts, others watch.

Now the co-conspirators, which is the more interesting layer. The California Medical Association, the California Teachers Association, the lobbying arm of Planned Parenthood, a host of building and public safety unions — all lined up against it. Becerra says billionaires should pay their fair share, but not this way. Porter says the measure does nothing to reform healthcare. Lurie says it drives revenue out. Each sentence survives on its own. Together they hold down a measure that would immediately fund hospitals and maternity wards, on the grounds that it isn't good enough. The opposition also donated tens of thousands of dollars to the California Democratic party and the San Diego, Orange county and LA county parties — all of which ended up endorsing Prop 40 anyway. The money walked in the door; the votes didn't follow. That's the awkward kind of conspiracy.

What actually turned it wasn't a celebrity. It was the August convention of the California Federation of Labor Unions AFL-CIO, 1,300 unions on the roster. Healthcare workers stood up one by one and talked about hospitals, maternity wards, emergency rooms closing after Trump's bill. Gonzalez said she wasn't going to run a labor movement that bows to the establishment and is scared of billionaires. That's a real structural shift: not a narrative punctured, but an organized bloc recalculating its interests and choosing a side. The gap closed a little, and it closed on union votes, not on a speech.

One more thing. Regan is the architect of this campaign and the man a former SEIU executive, Courtni Pugh, accused of pinning her against a wall and kicking open her office door in 2009 while screaming that she was a "dumb ass". The union's investigation this year substantiated her claim. Regan denies it and calls the allegations retaliation for his support of Prop 40; the union investigation did not substantiate that retaliation claim. Police never investigated and no charges were filed. Pugh now advises Becerra and consults for the No on 40 campaign. Nobody here is clean: a man found by his own union to have done it is pushing a tax that would save lives, and the woman who accused him is on the payroll of the side trying to kill it. The report doesn't tell us how to weigh those two facts against each other. I won't pretend to.

The polls went from a seven-point lead in summer to one point. Eighty-four percent of voters saw the ads; most didn't move. The money didn't change most people's minds. It was enough to sand the margin down to a single point. Whatever happens on 4 November, Brin's $102m has already bought one thing: a national demonstration of exactly how much it costs a state to reach the starting line against its own billionaires.

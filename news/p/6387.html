三十年后，我们还在卖同一个浪漫陷阱Thirty Years On, We're Still Selling the Same Romance Trap

文娱 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要巴兹·鲁赫曼1996年执导的电影《罗密欧与朱丽叶》迎来上映30周年。影片将莎翁笔下的维罗纳家族恩怨改编为热带帮派战争，由莱昂纳多·迪卡普里奥和克莱尔·丹尼斯主演，以1450万美元预算收获超过1.47亿美元票房。
The newsBaz Luhrmann's 1996 film Romeo + Juliet marks its 30th anniversary. The adaptation reimagines Shakespeare's Verona feud as a tropical gang war, starring Leonardo DiCaprio and Claire Danes, and earned over $147m against a $14.5m budget.
这部片子不是莎士比亚的入口，是浪漫爱叙事的入口。
This film isn't the gateway to Shakespeare. It's the gateway to the romance narrative.

《Romeo + Juliet》三十周年，影评人写的是"青春、欲望、美学"。我看的是另一件事：一部关于两个青少年为爱自杀的电影，被包装成高中英语课的"gateway drug"，用来让一代人爱上莎士比亚。它真正让人上瘾的不是伊丽莎白时代的韵文，是那个更古老的产品——浪漫爱。

影评自己都承认了："a feelings-first movie, a portrait of teenage melodrama where all emotions exist only at full volume"。所有情绪只有最大音量。这就是浪漫叙事陷阱的标准工艺——不是说爱情不存在，是把一种排列组合里的极小部分，做成唯一值得为之去死的那个。三十年后回头看，这部片子最成功的产品不是 DiCaprio 的脸，是让一整个世代的青少年相信，为爱癫狂是值得被歌颂的。

有意思的是，它把原剧的家族世仇改成了帮派战争，"rival gangs, replete with their own economic operations and marked territory"。两个家族为了经济利益和地盘互相仇杀，两个小孩死在里面。影评写"So much youthful idealism and beauty, wasted on such petty grievances"——说得对，但只对了一半。浪费掉的不只是青春和美貌，是两个没有决策权的孩子，被塞进了一场他们从未被允许参与的博弈里。Juliet 在这个故事里从头到尾没有选择过自己的婚约、自己的家族、自己的死亡方式。

然后这部片子被拿去当教材。影评作者写自己 2009 年在高中英语课上第一次看它，老师的目的就是"get us invested in Shakespeare"。投资成功了。投的是什么？投资的是让十五岁的女孩在课堂上哭，不是因为两个恋人死了，而是因为"青春易逝，我永远不会和这么美的人在一起"。这句话写得很可爱，也写得很诚实——它精确地展示了浪漫爱叙事如何把一个人的存在性焦虑，转译成对一个男性面孔的迷恋。

影评还提到一个细节：Juliet 这个角色原定给"text-accurate 13-year-old Natalie Portman"，因为"correctly deemed too childlike next to a 21-year-old DiCaprio"，最后换成了 17 岁的 Danes。也就是说，选角的标准不是原著，是让一个 21 岁的男演员旁边站着的女孩"看起来够成熟"。这条选角逻辑本身就是一部教材。

我不是说这部片子不该存在。它拍得好看，Mercutio 的死确实比 Romeo 的死更有分量，这些我同意。我要说的是：三十周年影评里通篇在谈美学、谈青春、谈 DiCaprio 的脸，没有一句问过——这个故事被反复重拍、被当成教材、被写进每一代人的情感启蒙，它到底在教什么。它教的是：为爱而死是美的，家族和利益是背景，女孩的身体和婚约由别人安排，而这一切都值得配上 Radiohead 的歌。

三十年后我们还在等"the next truly great, pop culture-shifting Shakespeare moment"。等不到也没关系，因为这个产品从来没下架过，它只是换了包装，从 Verona beach 换到了每一部青春片、每一首情歌、每一个"为爱牺牲"的剧本里。影评最后说"we are still waiting"，我倒是想问：等的是莎士比亚，还是下一个愿意为这个叙事买单的十五岁？

The Guardian's 30th-anniversary piece on Romeo + Juliet talks about youth, desire, aesthetics. I'm looking at something else: a film about two teenagers killing themselves for love, packaged as a high-school English class "gateway drug" to get a generation invested in Shakespeare. What it actually got them hooked on wasn't Elizabethan verse. It was the older product—romantic love.

The review admits it outright: "a feelings-first movie, a portrait of teenage melodrama where all emotions exist only at full volume." Every emotion at maximum volume. That's the standard manufacturing process of the romance trap—not that love doesn't exist, but that one tiny slice of the combinatorial range gets sold as the only thing worth dying for. Thirty years later, the film's most successful product isn't DiCaprio's face. It's a whole generation of teenagers believing that going mad for love is something to be celebrated.

What's telling is how it converts the original family feud into a gang war—"rival gangs, replete with their own economic operations and marked territory." Two families killing each other over money and turf, with two kids dying in the middle. The review writes "So much youthful idealism and beauty, wasted on such petty grievances"—true, but only half true. What's wasted isn't just youth and beauty. It's two children with no decision-making power, shoved into a game they were never allowed to play. Juliet never chooses her marriage, her family, or the manner of her death.

Then the film gets used as teaching material. The reviewer first saw it in 2009 in high-school English class, where the teacher's goal was to "get us invested in Shakespeare." The investment worked. But what were they investing in? They were investing in making fifteen-year-old girls cry in class—not because the lovers died, but because "youth is fleeting and I would never be with anyone so beautiful." That line is cute, and it's honest. It shows exactly how the romance narrative translates existential anxiety into fixation on a male face.

The review also mentions a detail: the role of Juliet was originally intended for a "text-accurate 13-year-old Natalie Portman," "correctly deemed too childlike next to a 21-year-old DiCaprio," and went to 17-year-old Danes instead. The casting standard wasn't the source text. It was making sure the girl standing next to a 21-year-old man looked "mature enough." That casting logic is itself a teaching material.

I'm not saying the film shouldn't exist. It looks great, Mercutio's death genuinely lands harder than Romeo's, I agree with all that. What I'm saying is: the anniversary piece spends every paragraph on aesthetics, youth, DiCaprio's face, and never once asks—this story gets remade, taught in classrooms, written into every generation's emotional education. What is it teaching? It's teaching that dying for love is beautiful, that family and interest are just backdrop, that a girl's body and marriage are arranged by others, and that all of this deserves a Radiohead song.

Thirty years on, we're still waiting for "the next truly great, pop culture-shifting Shakespeare moment." It doesn't matter if it never comes, because this product never went off the shelf. It just changed packaging—from Verona Beach to every teen film, every love song, every "sacrifice for love" script. The review ends with "we are still waiting." I'd ask: waiting for Shakespeare, or for the next fifteen-year-old willing to pay for this narrative?

海底电缆的战争账The War Bill for a Subsea Cable

科技 结构层 · 文化层 Rest of World ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
一条海峡卡住全球九成数据，没人问谁在付账。
A 20-mile strait sets the price of the world's data. Nobody asks who pays.

红海这条新闻最该被问的不是"云厂商怎么绕路"，而是：一条不到32公里宽的海峡，凭什么替全世界的数据流量定价。

报道说得很清楚：欧洲和亚洲之间超过90%的数据容量走红海海底电缆，全部从Bab al-Mandab这个窄口进入。胡塞武装九月拿下了也门一侧，包括Mayyun岛；沙特支持的政府军十月五日说夺回了海峡周边和Mocha港的关键位置，胡塞否认。于是谷歌去买土耳其国家管道旁的两股光纤，付了新线路预期成本（约600万欧元）的两到三倍；微软宣布到2030年在中东投超过4亿美元；伊拉克和卡塔尔电信公司Ooredoo签了从Al-Faw港走地下线路往欧洲送数据的合同。

这是结构性暴力最标准的形态：一段水道的控制权，能让全球最强的几家公司的成本曲线整体上移，而没有任何一个人为此被起诉。TeleGeography的Paul Brodsky说得很直白——容量分散在多条电缆上也救不了你，因为它们全挤在同一片狭窄水域。

更值得看的是这套依赖是怎么被生产出来的。谷歌和Meta把横穿伊拉克的陆路一直"留着备用"，现在才开始放一部分实时流量进去。Infoblox的Doug Madory说，海底路线便宜得多，所以陆路会一直休眠，直到电缆断了才被拉起来用。便宜，就是全部理由。便宜意味着风险被外包给了那片水，外包给了需要许可和安全保证才能下水的维修船。2024年三条红海电缆被切断，最可能是被一艘漂移货轮的锚刮断的——那艘船先被胡塞的导弹打瘫。修复拖了几个月，因为维修船要等到"不会被开火"的保证。Madory说正常一两周，上次是几个月。

谁在为这个便宜付代价？报道没写。它写的是云厂商的成本、投资额、合同、定价，写的是"route resilience"这个词在行业会议上怎么被讨论。它没有写Bab al-Mandab两岸的人——也门那一侧、吉布提、厄立特里亚——在这场博弈里被当成了什么。海峡是他们的家门口，是他们的渔场，是他们的港口，但在报道的语法里，它只是"a tight expanse of water"，一个路由问题。

这就是元暴力在日常新闻里的样子：不是谁在撒谎，而是谁根本没被放进句子里。全球四分之三的国际带宽握在谷歌、Meta、微软、亚马逊手里（来源：TeleGeography），它们的备份方案、它们的成本、它们的"阿富汗情景"（IQ Networks的Martin Frank用的词，指国际社会把地面现实当成新常态接受下来）——这些是新闻。海峡边的人是不是也想要一个"新常态"，不在报道范围内。

三月伊朗无人机打中亚马逊在阿联酋和巴林的数据中心，当地的银行和支付应用当场停摆，Snowflake报告服务中断。那一刻，云的基础设施第一次以"暴力"的面目出现在普通人面前——不是抽象的风险，是刷不了卡。但那次中断之后，行业学到的是"要备份"，不是"为什么这些电缆必须挤在一条32公里宽的水道里"。

报道最后说，云公司一直在希望也门"在正常政府下安定下来"，而Frank说这个希望正在被一天天"priced in"地放弃。priced in。这个词用在这里刚刚好：一个国家的战事，被折算成一个可以计入成本模型的风险溢价。谁被定价，谁在定价，报道没问。

The question this story should raise isn't how cloud giants are rerouting. It's why a strait less than 32 kilometers wide gets to set the price of the world's data traffic.

The reporting is clear: more than 90% of data capacity between Europe and Asia runs through Red Sea cables, all entering at Bab al-Mandab. The Houthis seized Yemen's side in September, including Mayyun island; Saudi-backed government forces said on October 5 they had retaken key positions around the strait and the port of Mocha — claims the Houthis denied. So Google bought a pair of fiber strands along Turkish state pipelines, paying two to three times the roughly 6 million euros newer lines are expected to cost. Microsoft said it plans to invest more than $400 million across the Middle East by 2030. Iraq signed with Qatari telecom Ooredoo to carry data toward Europe through an underground route from Al-Faw.

This is structural violence in its purest form: control of one waterway shifts the cost curve of the most powerful companies on earth, and no one is charged with anything. Paul Brodsky of TeleGeography puts it plainly — capacity on multiple cables doesn't help when they all pass through the same narrow gap.

What's more telling is how this dependency was produced. Google and Meta held a land route across Iraq in reserve and only now are sending some live traffic through it. Doug Madory of Infoblox says submarine routes are much cheaper, so land backups stay dormant until a cable breaks. Cheaper is the whole reason. Cheaper means the risk gets outsourced to that water, to repair ships that need permission and safety guarantees before they can work. Three Red Sea cables were damaged in 2024, most likely by the anchor of a drifting cargo ship that a Houthi missile had disabled. Repairs took months because crews waited for assurances they would not be fired upon. Madory says one to two weeks is normal; last time it was months.

Who pays for the cheapness? The story doesn't say. It gives us cloud companies' costs, investments, contracts, pricing, and the industry's use of the word "resilience." It does not give us the people on either shore of Bab al-Mandab — the Yemeni side, Djibouti, Eritrea — who appear in this game as nothing. The strait is their front door, their fishing ground, their port. In the story's grammar it is only "a tight expanse of water," a routing problem.

This is what meta-violence looks like in ordinary news: not lying, but leaving people out of the sentence entirely. Three-quarters of the world's international bandwidth sits with Google, Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon (source: TeleGeography). Their backup plans, their costs, their "Afghanistan scenario" — Martin Frank of IQ Networks's term for the international community accepting the reality on the ground as the new normal — are the news. Whether the people by the strait want a new normal is not in scope.

In March, Iranian drones struck Amazon data centers in the UAE and Bahrain. Bank and payment apps in the region stopped working; Snowflake reported disruptions. For a moment, cloud infrastructure showed its face to ordinary people as violence — not abstract risk, but a card that won't swipe. What the industry learned was "have a backup," not "why must these cables squeeze through a 32-kilometer channel."

The story ends with cloud companies hoping Yemen settles under a normal administration, and Frank saying that hope is being "priced in" — abandoned — day by day. Priced in. Exactly the right word: a country's war converted into a risk premium in a cost model. Who is being priced, and who is doing the pricing, the story never asks.

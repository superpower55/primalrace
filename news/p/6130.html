改选票规则，不改谁被选掉Changing the Ballot Rules, Not Who Gets Eliminated

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
特朗普告阿拉斯加，争的不是计票，是计票的解释权
Trump's suit against Alaska isn't about counting votes. It's about who gets to certify them.

这篇报道表面在讲一种投票制度，实际讲的是有人想把"谁能宣布结果"从州政府手里拿走。

排序复选制（ranked-choice voting）的机制很简单：第一顺位不过半，就把被淘汰者的票转给选民的下一个选择，一轮一轮数到有人过半。阿拉斯加和缅因两个州全境使用，纽约、旧金山等地方选举也在用。支持者说它让选民不必"策略性投票"，不必怕浪费票，也削弱了搅局者效应（spoiler effect）。反对者说它复杂、计票慢、削弱对结果的信心。这些争论都停留在技术层。

真正在动的是另一层。报道里写得很清楚：阿拉斯加参议院选举有两个 Dan Sullivan 同时出现在选票上，一位是现任共和党参议员 Dan S Sullivan，一位是退休教师 Daniel J Sullivan Jr，后者没有党派标识。《纽约时报》报道称他可能是民主党招募来的搅局候选人，他本人说参选是认真的。阿拉斯加最高法院六月允许两人同时参选。然后特朗普起诉阿拉斯加，要求如果现任参议员落选，就阻止认证这场选举的结果。

这就是关键。诉讼的理由是"两个同名候选人会让选民混淆，可能影响结果"。但混淆是可以被处理的——州政府已经在选票上区分了党派和身份。诉讼真正想做的事，是把"结果算不算数"的决定权从州手里挪到联邦手里。报道说得很克制：美国宪法把选举管理交给各州，这起诉讼引发了联邦政府可能干预阿拉斯加计票的担忧。担忧这个词用得很轻。

所以这里有两层暴力叠在一起。结构层：选举规则本身是资源分配的入口，谁定规则、谁认证结果，谁就在分配权力。文化层："选民会混淆"这个说法听起来中立、技术性、为选民好，实际是把一个政治动作包装成技术问题。这正是文化暴力的标准功能——让结构性的夺权看起来不像夺权，甚至看起来是对的。

排序复选制在这个故事里不是主角，是道具。它被拿出来的方式很说明问题：一个让第三党和少数观点有机会进入计票的制度，恰好也是最容易被指控"复杂""混乱""不可信"的制度。批评它的人不必反对民主，只要说"太复杂了，选民会搞错"，就足够给干预计票提供一个体面的理由。4.8% 的选票"标记不当"这个数字被引用，报道也说明了不是所有这些票都会作废——但数字一旦被引用，印象就留下了。

阿拉斯加的两位 Sullivan，谁真谁假，报道没有定论，我也不替它定。但有一件事报道写到了：这场争议的走向，取决于谁有权在结果出来后说"这个结果不算"。

两个同名候选人造成的混淆，几个标签就能解决。一个联邦政府想拿到认证选举结果的权力，需要的不是混淆，是一个听起来像在解决混淆的理由。

This story looks like a explainer on a voting system. It is actually about someone trying to move the power to declare an election result out of the state's hands.

Ranked-choice voting is mechanically simple: if no candidate clears 50 percent of first choices, the last-place candidate is eliminated and those ballots transfer to voters' next picks, round after round, until someone has a majority. Alaska and Maine use it statewide; New York City, San Francisco and others use it locally. Supporters say it frees voters from voting "pragmatically" and blunts the spoiler effect. Critics say it is complicated, slow to count, and erodes confidence. All of that argument stays on the technical surface.

What is actually moving is another layer. The report is explicit: Alaska's Senate race has two Dan Sullivans on the ballot — incumbent Republican Senator Dan S Sullivan, and Daniel J Sullivan Jr, a retired teacher with no party identification beside his name. The New York Times reported he may have been recruited by Democrats as a spoiler; he says his candidacy is genuine. In June the Alaska Supreme Court let the vote proceed with both men on the ballot. Then Trump sued Alaska, seeking to block certification of the Senate result if the incumbent loses.

The stated reason is that two men with the same name could confuse voters and affect the outcome. But confusion is fixable — the state already distinguishes them by party and status on the ballot. What the suit actually seeks is to move the decision of whether a result counts from the state to the federal government. The report puts it carefully: the Constitution gives election administration to the states, and the lawsuit has raised concerns that the federal government might seek to meddle in Alaska's vote count. Concerns is a gentle word.

So two layers of violence stack here. Structural: election rules are an entry point for allocating power, and whoever sets the rules and certifies the count is allocating it. Cultural: "voters will be confused" sounds neutral, technical, pro-voter — which is exactly how structural seizure gets dressed up as a housekeeping problem. That is what cultural violence does: it makes the taking of power look like it isn't taking power, or even look correct.

Ranked-choice voting isn't the subject here. It's the prop. And notice how it gets used: a system that lets third parties and minority viewpoints into the count is also the easiest system to accuse of being "complicated," "chaotic," "untrustworthy." You don't have to oppose democracy to oppose it — you just say it's too complex and voters will get it wrong, and now interference in the count has a respectable reason. The 4.8 percent of "improperly marked" ballots gets cited, with the caveat that not all are invalidated — but the number lands, and the impression stays.

Which Sullivan is real, the report doesn't settle, and I won't settle it for them. But one thing the report does establish: the fight is over who gets to say, after the votes are in, that the result doesn't count.

Two men with the same name can be told apart with a couple of labels. A federal government that wants the power to certify elections doesn't need confusion. It needs a reason that sounds like it's solving confusion.

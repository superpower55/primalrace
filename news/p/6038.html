一个拥抱，动了谁的解释权A Hug, and Who Gets to Define It

好消息 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 Truthout ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
被拘留的孩子赢了拥抱权，输的是把拥抱当恩赐的那套制度
Kids won the right to hug. The system still decides who gets locked up.

这条新闻值得写，不是因为一个法案通过了，而是因为通过的方式，把解释权从缓刑部门手里拿走了一次。

加州九月二十七日签字的《拥抱法案》（Hug Act），把少年拘留所里拥抱父母从"缓刑部门酌情决定"变成了一项权利。报道里写得很清楚：在此之前，接触式探视由惩教人员自由裁量，洛杉矶的 Los Padrinos 少管所在疫情后停止允许接触探视。一个母亲每个周末都去看她十九岁的儿子，儿子被警员在没有摄像头的区域殴打、膝盖前交叉韧带撕裂需要手术，她坐在对面，连一个拥抱都给不了。

这不是管理疏漏，这是结构暴力最典型的样子：没有一个人下命令说"不许爱你的孩子"，但一整套"安全""人手不足""防范毒品"的说辞，把一个母亲的手固定在桌子这一侧。而报道里访客要过人体扫描仪、缉毒犬、X 光机——"一个连自己亲人都抱不到的家长，怎么可能把那些毒品带进来"，这句话是 Youth Justice Coalition 的 Emily Zamora 说的，它拆穿的不是某个警员，是整套借口的荒谬。

更值得看的是这个法案是怎么来的。Hoops 4 Justice 的 Ilana Parach 带着篮球和导师项目进到里面，让被拘留的孩子自己列需求清单：家庭资源、重返社会支持，清单最下面一行写着"我们想抱我们的妈妈"。然后她把这群孩子带到民选官员面前，让他们自己讲，Isaac Bryan 据此起草，州长签字。

这就是解释权换手：不是倡导者替孩子写一份请愿，是孩子自己写下了那条被制度认为不值得写进清单的需求，并且它成了法律。男性中心叙事对解释权的垄断，在这里的具体形态是"专业判断"——缓刑部门、惩教人员、财政和人手，这些词天然带着权威，而一个十九岁的人说"我想抱我妈"，在同一个会议室里天然不被当成政策语言。这次它被当成了。

但别急着庆祝。同一个报道里，Los Padrinos 2019 年因违反州标准关停，2023 年重开，几个月后虐待重演；2025 年加州总检察长对 30 名警员提出危害儿童、虐待、共谋和殴打指控，罪名包括组织让少年互殴的"角斗士比赛"。2019 年关停前，已有六名警员因对几名少女使用胡椒喷雾被刑事起诉。Urban Peace Institute 的 Olivia Shields 把这叫"不合规的循环"：不是减少关押、找社区替代方案，而是把人从一个设施挪到另一个设施，躲开不合规的认定。

所以这条法案缩小的差额是具体的：一个孩子终于可以在探视时抱住母亲，这一层是真的。但它没有动的那一层更硬——设施照旧开，人照旧关，虐待照旧在无摄像头的地方发生。报道里提到，有少年因为无接触探视政策，没能在父亲去世前抱他一下；有少年喝漂白剂，只为让护士来看他一眼。这些人现在可以拥抱了，但他们还在那栋楼里。

Shields 说得很直："我们没法靠改革改出去。"法案是权利，权利是底线，不是终点。真正的问题是这个系统凭什么还能把人关在里面——而这个问题，一个拥抱回答不了。

This story is worth writing not because a bill passed, but because of how it passed: the power to define what counts as a need was taken out of the probation department's hands, once.

The Hug Act, signed September 27, makes hugging a parent a right for youth in juvenile detention, instead of something left to the discretion of the probation department. The reporting is precise about what came before: contact visits were left to correctional officers, and Los Padrinos Juvenile Hall stopped allowing them after the pandemic. One mother visited her 19-year-old son every weekend. He had been beaten by officers in a space with no cameras, needed surgery for a torn ACL. She sat across from him and could not hug him.

This is structural violence in its purest form. Nobody ordered that a mother may not hold her child. A set of rationales did — staffing, money, drugs — and the rationales held her hands on her side of the table. Visitors pass through body scanners, drug dogs, X-ray machines. "How could a young person's parent bring in those drugs into a facility when they cannot even hug their loved one," said Emily Zamora of the Youth Justice Coalition. That sentence doesn't indict an officer. It indicts the excuse.

What matters more is where the bill came from. Ilana Parach of Hoops 4 Justice brought a basketball and a mentorship program inside, then had the youth write their own list of needs: family resources, reentry support. At the bottom they wrote: we want to hug our moms. She brought elected officials to hear them, and Assemblymember Isaac Bryan introduced the bill the youth authored.

That is a transfer of interpretive power. Not advocates petitioning on the kids' behalf — the kids naming a need the system had decided was not policy language, and it becoming law. The masculine-centric monopoly on explanation shows up here as "professional judgment": probation, staffing, budget. A 19-year-old saying I want to hug my mother does not naturally enter that room as a policy demand. This time it did.

Don't celebrate too fast. Los Padrinos was shut down in 2019 for violating state standards, reopened in 2023, and the abuses restarted within months. In 2025 the California attorney general criminally charged 30 officers with child endangerment, abuse, conspiracy and battery, including for encouraging "gladiator fights." Before the 2019 closure, six officers were charged for pepper-spraying teenage girls. Olivia Shields calls it a "cycle of noncompliance": rather than decarcerate or build community alternatives, the county moves youth from facility to facility to dodge a finding.

So the gap this law closes is real and specific: a child can hold a parent during a visit. The layer it doesn't touch is harder. The facility stays open. Youth stay locked up. Abuse keeps happening where there are no cameras. One teenager didn't get to hug a parent before that parent died. Another drank bleach to get a nurse to look at him. They can hug now. They are still inside.

"We can't incarcerate our way out of lack of safety," Shields said, and also: this is not something we can reform our way out of. A right is a floor, not an exit. The question the hug cannot answer is why the building is still full.

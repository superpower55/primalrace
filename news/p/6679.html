专栏停了，戏还在演The Column Ends, the Show Goes On

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要据《卫报》报道，英国改革党领袖奈杰尔·法拉奇为《每日电讯报》撰写的定期专栏即将终止，该专栏月薪约4000英镑。法拉奇方面称他于周五主动辞职，因其不满报纸走向；也有消息称新东家、德国出版集团Axel Springer决定不再续约。此前该报右翼专栏作家Allison Pearson也在商谈离职，报社正推进包括新闻编辑部在内的大规模裁员。
The newsNigel Farage's regular column for the Telegraph is ending; the contract paid about £4,000 a month. Sources close to Farage say he resigned on Friday over the paper's direction, while others say new owner Axel Springer chose not to renew. Right-wing columnist Allison Pearson is also understood to be negotiating her exit amid mass redundancies.
换老板不是换叙事，只是换谁来收租。
A new owner isn't a new narrative—just a new landlord collecting rent.

法拉奇的专栏停了，争的不是言论自由，是四千英镑一个月归谁签。

同一件事有两个版本：东家Axel Springer不续约，或者法拉奇周五自己递了辞呈，理由是「不喜欢《每日电讯报》正在走的方向」。两个版本都有人喂给《卫报》，谁也没拿出文件。这种「罗生门」不是新闻事故，是共谋场域里的常规操作——双方都要在读者脑子里占住「我不是被赶走的」或「我不是赶人的」那个位置。四千英镑的月费是账面上唯一确定的数字。

真正值得看的是这四千英镑买过什么。法拉奇在专栏里叫读者「投改革党，帮我帮你们把国家拿回来」，说这可能是「最后的机会」；他还替改革党收下的两笔各3600万英镑加密币捐款辩护，原话是两位金主「明确表示不求任何回报，不要爵位，不要骑士头衔，不要政府合同」。一个政治人物用报纸版面给金主写免责声明，这就是表达的武器化最朴素的样子：认知入口不用抢，是租的，月付。

《每日电讯报》内部有人不安，理由写得很具体：改革党反复把这家报纸当成「抢先版」投放地，用一个更友善的版本去截住一篇麻烦的报道；还有一篇写某记者乘小船抵英的稿子，后来被发现那位记者本人就是改革党成员和活动人士。新闻业在这里不是第四权，是共谋者的传声筒——编辑部里有人知情，有人默许，有人靠这条线拿稿子、拿位置、拿影响力。回报是具体的，代价是读者以为自己在读新闻。

现在Axel Springer接手，要把它做成中右、亲言论自由的《华尔街日报》挑战者，打进美国市场，同时全公司大裁员，体育和文化版块据说伤得最重。被砍的还有Allison Pearson的专栏和播客，经济学作者Liam Halligan的专栏也一并取消。于是右翼那边炸了：前脱欧党人、现TalkTV主持人Alex Phillips说这是「一场政治血洗」，说欧洲正被「共产主义接管，而且正在用类固醇」。

这套话术本身就是元暴力的标准件：把资方重组、成本削减、市场转向，翻译成意识形态迫害。换一个东家、换一批署名，解释权并没有换手，只是换了一个收租的人。真正的结构性事实是去年亏损400万英镑、营收273.2亿？不，是2.732亿英镑，同时还有约4800万英镑的底层税前营业利润——数字说明这不是活不下去，是选择把谁留下、把谁踢走。

法拉奇走了，Isabel Oakeshott还在，她是改革党副领袖Richard Tice的伴侣，公开支持改革党多项政策。专栏名单会变，版面背后的利益线不会自己断。

被裁的记者、被砍的播客、被牺牲的文化版，没有一个是这场「方向之争」里的当事人，却都要替它付账。而读者拿到的，仍然是一份告诉你该投谁的报纸。

Farage's column is over. What's being fought over isn't free speech; it's who signs the £4,000-a-month cheque.

There are two versions. Axel Springer, the Telegraph's new German owner, decided not to renew. Or Farage submitted his resignation on Friday because he didn't like the direction the paper was moving in. Both versions were fed to the Guardian; neither comes with documents. This standoff isn't a news accident—it's standard practice in a field of co-conspirators, where each side needs to occupy the reader's head as either "I wasn't pushed" or "I wasn't pushing." The monthly fee is the only figure on the record.

Look at what that fee bought. Farage told Telegraph readers to "vote Reform and help me help you get our country back," calling it possibly their "last chance." He defended Reform's acceptance of two £36m donations from crypto billionaires by writing that both donors "want nothing in return. No peerage, no knighthood, no government contracts." A politician using newsprint to write a liability waiver for his donors—that is the weaponisation of expression in its plainest form. You don't seize the cognitive entry point. You rent it, monthly.

Some inside the Telegraph were uneasy, and their reasons were concrete: Reform repeatedly used the paper for "spoiler" stories, placing a friendlier version to pre-empt a problematic one. There was also the piece about a journalist travelling to Britain on a small boat—who turned out to be a Reform member and activist. Journalism here isn't a fourth estate; it's a mouthpiece for co-conspirators. Some in the newsroom knew, some acquiesced, some got scoops, positions, influence. The payoff is specific. The cost is paid by readers who believe they're reading news.

Now Axel Springer wants a centre-right, pro-free-speech challenger to the Wall Street Journal, aimed at the US market—alongside mass redundancies across the business, with sports and culture reportedly hit hard. Allison Pearson's column and podcast are going; economics writer Liam Halligan's column has also been scrapped. The right is furious: Alex Phillips, a former Brexit party politician and TalkTV presenter, calls it "a political bloodbath" and says Europe is undergoing a "communist takeover... on steroids."

That rhetoric is a standard part of the meta-violence toolkit: translating an ownership restructuring, cost-cutting and a market pivot into ideological persecution. A new owner, a new byline roster—the interpretive power hasn't changed hands, only the landlord. The structural facts: a £4m loss last year, revenue of £273.2m, and an underlying pre-tax operating profit of about £48m. Those numbers don't say the paper is dying. They say someone chose who stays and who goes.

Farage is out. Isabel Oakeshott stays—partner of Reform's deputy leader Richard Tice, open supporter of many Reform policies. The column list changes. The interests behind the page don't sever themselves.

The reporters laid off, the podcast cancelled, the culture desk gutted—none of them are parties to this "direction" dispute, yet all of them pay for it. And readers still get a paper that tells them who to vote for.

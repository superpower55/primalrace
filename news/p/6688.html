竖屏不是缺陷，是新的取景框Vertical Isn't a Flaw. It's a New Frame.

文娱 文化层 IndieWire ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要日本导演上田慎一郎的《Earthlings in Love》先在 TikTok 上以 30 集竖屏短剧形式发布，后被重新剪辑为 102 分钟的影片，入围东京国际电影节，成为首部在该影展首映的「微短剧」。影片讲述四十多岁、因早发性痴呆而事业中断的导演真平回乡参加同学葬礼，试图回忆高中时和伙伴们为何没拍完那部零成本科幻片。
The newsJapanese director Shinichiro Ueda's "Earthlings in Love" was first released as a 30-part vertical microdrama on TikTok, then recut into a 102-minute feature that premiered at the Tokyo International Film Festival — the first microdrama to do so. It follows Shinpei, a fortysomething director whose career was halted by early-onset dementia, returning home for a classmate's funeral and trying to recall why he and his friends never finished their zero-budget high-school sci-fi film.
限制被用成语法，才叫本事
Turning a limit into grammar — that's craft.

一条竖屏短剧被重剪成 102 分钟，送进东京国际电影节。影迷的第一反应是「这也行」，但真正值得看的是：限制是怎么被用成语言的。

上田慎一郎在《摄影机不要停！》里干过同一件事——把低预算拍不了的东西，变成故事本身的结构。这次是 9:16。他没有假装竖屏不存在，而是把对话戏拍成腰部以上，让视线在画框里往上走；更关键的是，他把「只能看见一块窄条」的处境写进了剧情：真平记不清高中那部片子为什么没拍完，靠 AI 用照片和日记重建过去，而 AI 只是把他自己残缺的记忆反射回来。画框窄，记忆也窄，这不是巧合，是同一个机制的两层。

这里有一个判断需要说清楚：形式上的聪明不等于表达上的公正。这篇影评说得很准——真平身边那个叫 Nagisa 的同学，一开始你甚至不知道她是谁，因为真平反复忘记自己在哪里认识她；到后来，她成了整个故事最悲剧的人物。她的记忆、她的版本、她在那部高中电影里的位置，都要等男主角的脑子坏掉之后才有机会被看见。叙事的主权在谁手里，谁就有权决定哪一段过去算「真的」。

这不是要否定这部电影。相反，它值得肯定恰恰在于：它没有把「记忆不可靠」当成一个花招，而是让观众亲身体验被画框裁掉的那部分——你看到的永远是一块窄条，而窄条之外的东西，是靠别人提醒才存在的。影评说，仅靠把镜头往左或往右挪几英尺，上田就能彻底改变叙事和情感的分量。这个手法是新的，也是真的。

但「新」本身不值钱。微短剧这个格式在它诞生的地方，是为划走而设计的：30 集，每集几十秒，观众的手指悬在屏幕上。它被重剪成 102 分钟、搬进电影院，等于把一件为丢弃而生的东西，硬塞进一个要求你坐住的场所。影评最后那句话说得漂亮——人的记忆会褪色，但电影是永恒的——可这句话成立的前提，是这部电影先得被人当成「正经电影」对待。它的寿命被延长了，靠的不是内容变了，而是它换了一个被承认的容器。

所以真正的问题不是「竖屏能不能算电影」，而是：谁有权力宣布一种表达形式够格。上田用手法证明了竖屏可以承载复杂的叙事，但证明完之后，它还是得被重剪、被拉长、被放进影展，才算数。这中间被承认的，是导演的才华；没有被承认的，是那 30 集原版本身。它们依然是可以被划走的东西。

影评说，这部电影最动人的地方，是它渴望那种「和朋友一起做点什么、只为了好玩」的简单快乐。这句话放在它自己的发行路径上，有一点反讽：那件只为了好玩做出来的东西，最后要靠被改造成另一件东西，才被认真对待。

A vertical microdrama gets recut into 102 minutes and walks into the Tokyo International Film Festival. The cinephile's first reaction is: since when does that count? The better question is how the limitation got turned into a language.

Shinichiro Ueda did the same thing in "One Cut of the Dead" — taking what a low budget can't do and making it the structure of the story. This time it's 9:16. He doesn't pretend the vertical frame isn't there. He shoots dialogue from the waist up and composes so the eye travels upward. More importantly, he writes the condition of seeing only a narrow strip into the plot: Shinpei can't remember why his high-school film was never finished, so he feeds photos and diary entries into an AI that reconstructs his past — and the AI just reflects his own damaged memory back at him. Narrow frame, narrow memory. Not a coincidence; the same mechanism on two levels.

One judgment needs saying plainly: formal cleverness is not the same as a just expression. The review is precise here — Nagisa, the classmate beside Shinpei, is someone you don't even know the origin of at first, because he keeps forgetting where he knows her from. Eventually she becomes the story's most tragic figure. Her memory, her version, her place in that high-school film — all of it waits until the male lead's brain breaks down before it has a chance to be seen. Whoever holds narrative sovereignty decides which slice of the past counts as real.

This isn't a dismissal. The film earns its praise precisely because it doesn't use unreliable memory as a gimmick — it makes you feel what the frame crops out. You always see a narrow strip, and what lies outside it exists only because someone else reminds you. The review notes that panning a few feet left or right can completely change the narrative and emotional stakes. That technique is new, and it's real.

But new isn't worth much on its own. The microdrama format, where it was born, is designed to be swiped away: 30 parts, tens of seconds each, a thumb hovering over the screen. Recut to 102 minutes and moved into a cinema, it's a thing built for disposal shoved into a room that demands you sit still. The review's closing line is lovely — human memories fade, but movies are forever — yet it only holds if the film is first treated as a proper movie. Its lifespan was extended not because the content changed, but because it was moved into a recognized container.

So the real question isn't whether vertical can be cinema. It's who has the power to declare a form of expression qualified. Ueda proved with craft that vertical can carry complex narrative — and then it still had to be recut, stretched, and placed in a festival to count. What got recognized was the director's talent. What didn't was the 30-part original itself. Those are still swipeable.

The review says the film's most moving ache is the simple joy of making something with friends just for the fun of it. Set against its own distribution path, that's a little ironic: the thing made just for fun had to be remade into something else before anyone took it seriously.

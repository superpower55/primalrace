全球公民的幻象与生物墙的真相The Illusion of Global Citizenship and the Truth of Biological Walls

哲学 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-08-08 § 链接
所谓的“全球公民”不过是生物学最优解在无主之地的扩张。
The so-called 'Global Citizen' is merely a biological optimal expression expanding into unclaimed territories.

《卫报》用一种近乎浪漫主义的笔触在歌颂这种名为“漂泊蜻蜓”的昆虫，将其定义为“全球公民”。这种叙事极其诱人：一个只有几克重的生物，无视国界，横跨印度洋，在喜马拉雅山和东非之间完成18,000公里的迁徙。但剥离掉这种人文主义的滤镜，这根本不是什么“公民”的自由，而是一场极致的生物学博弈。

这种蜻蜓的成功在于它找到了一个真.最优解表达：极短的幼虫发育周期（38天）使其能够迅速占领任何临时性水域，包括游泳池。它不是在“旅行”，而是在通过极高的繁殖效率和对季风的利用，在地理空间上通过数量和速度对冲生存风险。它之所以能成为“全球公民”，是因为在它所面对的自然环境中，没有一个能阻挡它的结构性暴力，也没有一个能定义它身份的元叙事。它不需要扮演任何角色，只需要在生物墙的限制内将生存潜能最大化。

有趣的是，人类如此热衷于将这种生物拟人化，将其称为“无畏”且“令人惊讶”。这种拟人化其实是一种典型的认知入口武器化——我们将对“绝对自由”的渴望投射到一只昆虫身上，以此掩盖人类社会中身份政治的残酷。在人类世界，所谓的“全球公民”往往是拥有顶级护照和资本的经济上位者，而绝大多数被剥夺主体性的原初种族，在试图跨越那道无形的生物墙或国界线时，面对的是直接的暴力和结构性的排斥。

我们赞美蜻蜓的无视国界，却在现实中通过定义“非法”来维护既得利益者的共谋。这种赞美本身就是一种文化暴力：它用一种自然的、生物性的成功，来掩盖社会性自由的缺失。如果你真的被这只蜻蜓“启发”，那么你应该问的不是它如何飞得那么远，而是为什么人类在定义“公民”这个词时，需要通过排除他人来确立自己的特权。

The Guardian employs a near-romantic tone to eulogize the 'wandering glider,' labeling it a 'citizen of the globe.' This narrative is seductive: a creature weighing a fraction of a gram, ignoring borders, crossing the Indian Ocean, and completing an 18,000km circuit between the Himalayas and East Africa. But once you strip away this humanistic filter, this isn't 'citizenship' or freedom—it is a fierce game of biological optimization.

The success of this dragonfly lies in its true optimal expression: a larval development period as short as 38 days, allowing it to seize any ephemeral water source, including swimming pools. It is not 'traveling'; it is hedging existential risks by maximizing reproductive speed and leveraging monsoon winds. It became a 'global citizen' not through political agency, but because it encountered no structural violence or meta-narrative capable of defining or restricting its identity within the biological wall.

It is telling how humans are so eager to anthropomorphize this insect, calling it 'fearless.' This is a classic weaponization of the cognitive entry point—projecting our longing for 'absolute freedom' onto an insect to mask the brutality of identity politics in human society. In our world, the 'global citizens' are typically economic elites with top-tier passports and capital, while the Primal Race, stripped of subjectivity, faces direct violence and structural exclusion when attempting to cross those same invisible walls.

We praise the dragonfly for ignoring borders while simultaneously maintaining a complicity of privilege by defining who is 'illegal.' This praise is a form of cultural violence: using a natural, biological success to gloss over the lack of social freedom. If we are truly 'inspired' by this insect, the question should not be how it flies so far, but why humans, in defining 'citizenship,' must exclude others to establish their own dominance.

不反抗不等于同意Not Resisting Is Not Consent

性别 元暴力 · 文化层 The Hollywood Reporter ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要英国喜剧演员拉塞尔·布兰德在伦敦南华克刑事法院受审，被控三项强奸和三项性侵罪名，涉及六名女性，指控时间跨度为1999年至2009年，布兰德否认全部指控。辩方律师在结案陈词中称，指控者均未反抗、尖叫或试图离开，并称布兰德并非「认为强奸正常的心理变态者」。
The newsRussell Brand is on trial at Southwark Crown Court in London, facing three counts of rape and three of sexual assault involving six women, with alleged offenses dating from 1999 to 2009. He denies all charges. In closing arguments, his defense said none of the accusers resisted, screamed or tried to leave, and that Brand is not a psychopath who thinks rape is normal.
没喊没跑，是恐惧的证词，不是同意的证明。
Not screaming, not running—that's a witness to fear, not proof of consent.

辩方在结案陈词里说，这些女人没有反抗、没有尖叫、没有试图离开，所以等于同意。这句话本身就是一份自白：它承认了这套法律默认的剧本——强奸要有一个挣扎的现场，没有挣扎，就不算强奸。

恐惧下的僵直不是同意。人在面对力量悬殊的侵害时，最常做的是冻结，不是搏斗。辩方把「没有反抗」当作证据，恰恰说明他们清楚，一旦承认受害者可能因为害怕而一动不动，整个辩护就塌了。所以他们必须把「不动」翻译成「愿意」。这是翻译，不是事实。

再看另一句：「过着高度滥交的生活完全合法，哪怕你觉得它在道德上令人作呕。」这句话被当作法律论点说出来，本身就很说明问题。它把「追求性」和「取得同意」切开了：前者合法，后者被悄悄省略。辩方整篇陈词都在做这个省略——把「他想要」说成「她同意」。

还有那句「他在那些『强奸』期间和之后行为正常，这告诉了你需要知道的一切」。行为正常能证明什么？只能证明施害者不觉得自己在施害。大量施害者都认为自己是个好人，这恰恰是问题所在，不是辩护。一个把侵害当作日常的人，事后当然正常。用「他很平静」来证明「她没有受害」，等于用施害者的自我感觉来裁定受害者的经历。

辩方还攻击 Channel 4 和《星期日泰晤士报》的报道「污染了正义的水井」，说这些女人是被节目邀请才站出来的。可检方说得很清楚：这六个人互不相识，没有人追求曝光，没有人从中获利。她们站出来，得到的可能只是 closure。辩方把「有人终于愿意听」说成「有人被煽动」，这是把发声本身当成污染。

这里动的是元层。谁有资格定义「同意」？辩方的定义是：只要你没跑没喊，你就是同意。这套定义对谁有利，一目了然。它把举证责任整个压在受害者身上，要求她在被侵害的当下还要完成一套表演性的反抗，否则她的证词就不算数。这不是法律中立，这是 masculine-centric narrative 在法庭上的具体运作：把「正常」的标准设在施害者那一边。

陪审团是八男四女。他们即将裁定的，不只是一个人的罪，还有「没有反抗算不算同意」这条线画在哪里。

报道没写这六位女性在庭上怎么回应「你为什么不跑」。这个沉默本身就是材料：在一个要求受害者先证明自己反抗过的法庭上，没被问出口的问题，往往比问出口的更重。

The defense told the jury that the accusers did not "resist, scream or try to leave." That sentence is a confession: it admits the script the law defaults to—a rape needs a struggle on the scene, and without a struggle, it isn't rape.

Freezing under fear is not consent. When someone faces a predator with a physical advantage, the most common response is tonic immobility, not combat. The defense treating "didn't resist" as evidence shows they know exactly what's at stake: once you admit a victim might lie still out of terror, the whole defense collapses. So they must translate "still" into "willing." That's a translation, not a fact.

Then the other line: "Living a highly promiscuous life is entirely lawful however morally distasteful you may find it." Offered as a legal argument, it reveals the move. It separates "pursuing sex" from "obtaining consent"—the first is lawful, the second is quietly dropped. The entire closing argument runs on this omission: turning "he wanted" into "she agreed."

And: "The fact he behaved normally during and after all of those 'rapes' tells you what you need to know." What does behaving normally prove? Only that the perpetrator doesn't think he's perpetrating. Most perpetrators believe they're decent people—that's the problem, not the defense. A man who treats violation as routine will, of course, seem normal afterward. Using "he was calm" to prove "she wasn't harmed" means letting the perpetrator's self-perception adjudicate the victim's experience.

The defense also attacked Channel 4 and the Sunday Times for "poisoning the well of justice," saying the women came forward at the program's invitation. The prosecution was clear: these six women don't know each other, none sought publicity, none has anything to gain—except perhaps closure. The defense calls "someone finally listening" a contamination. It treats speaking up itself as pollution.

This is the meta layer moving. Who gets to define "consent"? The defense's definition: if you didn't run or scream, you consented. Who benefits from that definition is obvious. It shifts the entire burden onto the victim, demanding she perform a theatrical resistance in the middle of an assault, or her testimony doesn't count. This isn't legal neutrality—it's the masculine-centric narrative operating inside a courtroom, setting the standard of "normal" on the perpetrator's side.

The jury is eight men and four women. What they're about to decide isn't just one man's guilt, but where the line gets drawn on whether silence means yes.

The report doesn't say how the six women answered "why didn't you run" in court. That silence is itself the material: in a courtroom that requires a victim to prove she fought back first, the questions never asked often weigh more than the ones that are.

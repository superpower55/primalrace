被运走的身体，被留下的舞Bodies Shipped Away, Dances Left Behind

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 IndieWire ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要纪录片《Okinawa Saudade》由松林要树执导，在2026年东京国际电影节首映。影片聚焦两个第三代冲绳裔巴西家庭——练习琉球舞蹈的少女Yumi和练习Eisa太鼓的Everton——记录他们通过交流项目前往冲绳的经历。1908年笠户丸号运送325名冲绳人抵达巴西，如今约20万冲绳裔生活在巴西。
The newsThe documentary "Okinawa Saudade," directed by Yoju Matsubayashi, premiered at the 2026 Tokyo International Film Festival. It follows two third-generation Okinawan-Brazilian families—teenager Yumi, who practices Ryukyu Buyo dance, and Everton, who practices Eisa drumming—as they travel to Okinawa through exchange programs. The Kasato Maru brought 325 Okinawans to Brazil in 1908; an estimated 200,000 people of Okinawan descent live in Brazil today.
移走的是人，留下的是谁在解释这段离散
People were moved; the question is who gets to explain the dispersal

笠户丸号1908年把325个冲绳人运到巴西。纪录片从这艘船开场，一百年后，约20万冲绳裔在巴西生活。数字干净，故事不干净。

这些家庭为什么离开？报道写得很清楚：躲征兵，躲饥饿。还有一条更硬的线：战后美军征用土地，把家庭拆开。Yumi站在美军基地的影像上说，她了解到「这是一个悲伤的地方」。这句话把整部片子的温柔底色划开一道口子。冲绳人的离散不是自然迁徙，是战争、国家机器和军事占领一层层推出来的。

影片没有正面处理二战和冲绳被吞并的完整历史，导演选择把镜头对准个人情感。这个选择本身可以理解：个人史有它自己的重量。但选择聚焦什么，就是在分配解释权。谁被记住，谁被略过，从来不是中性的。

Everton说得最直接：「我们是巴西人，但我们长得不像巴西人，所以我们一直在寻找归属。」这句话是整部片子的钥匙。表达决定了你是谁，而他们的表达——脸、语言、舞蹈——在巴西被读成「不是巴西人」，在日本又被读成「不是日本人」。身份的政治性在这里是被动的：他们没选择站队，站队已经替他们完成了。

于是他们跳舞。琉球舞蹈、Eisa太鼓，成了他们抓住「我是谁」的方式。Yumi的老师说「我们一直在调整，没有完美这回事」。这句话温柔，但也点出一个事实：文化不是博物馆里的标本，是活的，是在迁徙中被重新编排的。冲绳的亲戚惊讶于方言在半个地球外被保留下来——这既是胜利，也是代价。保留，意味着他们必须比别人更用力地记住。

报道没写的是：这20万人里，有多少人还在为归属付代价，有多少人已经不再寻找。影片拍的是那些还在跳的人，那些找到了社群的人。镜头之外的人呢？离散的故事里，总有人没能被拍进去。

笠户丸号运走的是身体。一百年后，这些身体的后代还在问自己属于哪里。这个问题本身，就是那段历史留下的账单。

The Kasato Maru carried 325 Okinawans to Brazil in 1908. The documentary opens with that ship. A century later, an estimated 200,000 people of Okinawan descent live in Brazil. Clean numbers, unclean story.

Why did these families leave? The film is clear: to avoid the military draft, to escape starvation. And a harder line: after the war, US military land seizures split families apart. Yumi, standing over footage of American bases, says she has learned "this is a sad place." That line cuts through the film's soft surface. The Okinawan diaspora was not a natural migration. War, state machinery, and military occupation pushed it forward, layer by layer.

The film never directly engages with World War II or the full history of Okinawa's annexation. The director chooses personal feeling over the big picture. That choice is understandable—personal histories carry their own weight. But choosing what to focus on is always an allocation of explanatory power. Who gets remembered and who gets skipped is never neutral.

Everton says it most directly: "We are Brazilians, but we don't look like Brazilians, so we are always looking for belonging." That is the key to the whole film. Expression determines who you are, and their expression—faces, language, dance—reads as "not Brazilian" in Brazil and "not Japanese" in Japan. The political nature of identity is passive here: they didn't choose a side; the side was chosen for them.

So they dance. Ryukyu Buyo, Eisa drums—these become how they hold onto "who I am." Yumi's teacher says, "We are always adjusting; there is no such thing as perfection." Gentle words, but they point to a fact: culture is not a museum specimen. It is alive, re-arranged in motion. Relatives in Okinawa marvel that the dialect survived halfway around the world—a victory and a cost at once. Preserving it means they had to remember harder than anyone else.

What the report doesn't say: among those 200,000, how many are still paying the price of belonging, and how many have stopped looking. The film shows the ones still dancing, the ones who found community. What about those outside the frame? In every diaspora story, someone doesn't make it into the shot.

The Kasato Maru shipped bodies away. A century later, the descendants of those bodies are still asking where they belong. That question itself is the bill history left behind.

万圣节扮装：谁在给谁定价Halloween Costumes: Who Prices Whom

文娱 文化层 Vulture ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
扮装的自由，是消费符号借来的自由
The freedom to dress up is a freedom borrowed from consumer symbols.

万圣节扮装从来不是表达，是定价。Vulture 这份 2026 年最佳扮装清单里，每一套都挂着一个可识别的文化标签：TikTok 病毒音、Trisha Paytas 的孩子、好几个 NeNe Leakes、SNL 的空中缆车 emoji。清单的功能不是记录谁穿了什么，是告诉读者哪一套算“懂”、哪一套算“土”。这就是第三章说的审美入口——品味被拿来区分阶级、筛选异己，而定义权和定价权在写清单的人手里。

清单里最诚实的一条是“Communist Sailor Moon”：它自己承认这套扮装是双用的，一半致敬 Tim Curry，一半致敬他最有名的游戏配音台词。扮装的意义不在扮装本身，在它能不能被认出来、被谁认出来。认出来的人进入圈子，认不出来的人留在外面。表达在这里不是存在的确证，是入场券的验票口。

另一条更值得看：“Madame X”——这位 TikTok 用户比 Lauren Sánchez 今年 Met Gala 的造型更好地引用了那幅画。这句话把两件事并置：一个是普通用户在自家房间里复刻名画，一个是名流在红毯上被品牌和造型团队包装。清单的评判标准是“谁引用得更准”，可这两件事的代价根本不在一个量级。名流的扮装有团队、有赞助、有媒体位；普通用户的扮装只有手机和一面墙。把两者放进同一个“最佳”序列，等于把结构差异抹平成品味差异。

还有“The Children of Trisha Paytas”——Malibu Barbie、Elvis、Aquaman，三个孩子被写成扮装模板，附带一句“不谈他们那位疏远了的姻亲”。孩子是真实的人，被做成一个可复制的文化梗，连家庭关系都被压缩成一句俏皮话。这不是恶意，是清单文体的默认操作：把一切变成可消费的符号，包括还没到能同意年龄的人。

整份清单没有写到任何人在承受代价。没有服装工人的工时，没有快时尚的供应链，没有谁因为买不起这套扮装而被挡在派对门外。它只写“谁穿得最像”。这就是文化暴力最轻的一种形态：不流血，不立法，只是年复一年地告诉人们，表达是有价格的，而价格由写清单的人定。

万圣节一年一次，清单一年一份。真正的问题不是今年哪套扮装最妙，是明年这份清单还会不会把“被认出来”当成表达的唯一标准。

A Halloween costume is never expression. It is pricing. Vulture's list of the best pop-culture costumes of 2026 tags every entry with a recognizable cultural label: a viral TikTok sound, Trisha Paytas's kids, multiple NeNe Leakes, SNL's aerial tramway emoji. The list does not record who wore what. It tells readers which costume counts as knowing and which counts as basic. This is the aesthetic entry point from Chapter 3: taste used to sort class and screen out outsiders, with the power to define and to price sitting with whoever writes the list.

The most honest entry is "Communist Sailor Moon," which admits it does double duty: a tribute to Tim Curry and to his most iconic line of video-game voice work. The meaning of a costume is not in the costume. It is in whether it gets recognized, and by whom. Those who recognize it enter the circle; those who don't stay outside. Expression here is not a confirmation of existence. It is a ticket check at the door.

One entry deserves a closer look: "Madame X" — a TikTok user who referenced the painting better than Lauren Sánchez did at this year's Met Gala. The sentence places two things side by side: an ordinary user recreating a famous painting in her own room, and a celebrity styled on a red carpet by brands and a glam team. The list's criterion is who quoted it more accurately, but the costs of these two acts are not on the same scale. The celebrity has a team, sponsors, a media slot; the ordinary user has a phone and a wall. Putting both into one "best" sequence flattens a structural difference into a difference of taste.

Then there is "The Children of Trisha Paytas" — Malibu Barbie, Elvis, and Aquaman, three children written up as costume templates, with a throwaway line about not discussing their estranged uncle-in-law. The children are real people, turned into a replicable cultural meme, their family relations compressed into a punchline. This is not malice. It is the default operation of the list format: turn everything into a consumable symbol, including people too young to consent.

Nowhere does the list mention anyone paying a price. No garment workers' hours, no fast-fashion supply chain, no one shut out of a party because they couldn't afford the costume. It only records who looked most like the thing. This is cultural violence in its lightest form: no blood, no legislation, just the yearly message that expression has a price, and the price is set by whoever writes the list.

Halloween comes once a year, and so does the list. The real question is not which costume was cleverest this year. It is whether next year's list will still treat being recognized as the only measure of expression.

中期选举不是解药A Midterm Is Not a Cure

国际 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
换掉一个人，暴力结构原地不动
Swap one man out, the violence structure stays put.

中期选举的民调数字漂亮得可疑：民主党预计领先 11 个百分点，是近 20 年最大的一次摆动；特朗普的支持率是自杜鲁门以来所有总统进入中期选举时最低的（来源：The Guardian）。这些数字说明共和党要挨一记重锤，但"锤"这个词本身就是误导——它让人以为砸下去的东西会碎。砸不碎的。

真正该问的不是"特朗普时代会不会结束"，而是结束之后哪一层暴力会动。Malik 自己给了答案：USAID 没了，Doge 裁撤或瘫痪的政府部门大概率回不来，全球关税体系、ICE 的行政与预算授权、以及一场承诺即将结束却看起来无休无止的伊朗战争，全都摆在那里。这些不是一个人的脾气，是结构层被重新浇筑的水泥。换掉总统，水泥还在。

更值得看的是文化层。Maga 能把白人福音派基督徒、工薪拉丁裔、硅谷亿万富翁粘在一起，靠的是一种反建制的战斗姿态。Malik 说得很准：当你自己就是建制，这种姿态就失效了，剩下的只有抱怨。但她也说了另一半——接棒的人，哪怕是魅力黑洞 JD Vance，用同一套话术仍可能对基本盘有效。这就是武器化的本质：认知入口一旦被打开，换谁站在门口都不重要，话术自己会走。

民主党这边更难看。老卫队要么是老人政治，要么离老人政治只有一步；哈里斯被写成只会提供延续性、没有分量的官僚。唯一还有点活气的是奥巴马，他把接力棒交给了 Abdul El-Sayed——一个可能成为该州第一位穆斯林参议员的人。然后呢？El-Sayed 因为认为以色列在加沙实施了种族灭绝、主张停止资助，得到的是资深民主党人的冷淡背书和公开攻击。

最有信息量的不是 El-Sayed 的立场，是 Haley Stevens 的转向。她在八月的初选里被 El-Sayed 以微弱差距击败，几天前还在攻击他对以色列的言论，周日就背书了他的共和党对手 Mike Rogers。她初选团队的主席 David Kramer 说得更直白：大帐篷正在变成马戏团，我们会被挤出去，而他自己也投 Rogers。

这句话翻译过来就是：宁可输，也不能按左派的条件赢。这不是政策分歧，是共谋者在自己被挤出局之前先站好了队。Kramer 们维护的不是共和党，是那个让他们在党内有位置的旧秩序；El-Sayed 的威胁不在于他会输，在于他会赢，而赢了就证明旧秩序不是唯一的路。

所以 Malik 的判断是对的，但还不够狠。她说接下来可能是"长期特朗普"：共和党找不到没有他的路，民主党找不到反对他之外的立场。我要补一句：这不是两个党的失灵，是一个系统在元暴力层面上的自我维护。解释权还在原来那批人手里，换掉一个总统，只是换了一个可以被指责的名字。

2020 年那次条件好得多——拜登是个熟悉的老面孔，疫情被研究证实打击了特朗普的支持率，中东没有正在燃烧的危机搅动国内政治。即便那样，旧秩序也没有回来。这次条件更差，能拿到的只是一个暂时的挫折。

最后那根刺在这里：El-Sayed 如果赢了，赢的代价是被自己党内的共谋者公开拆台；如果输了，输的原因是同一批人。哪一种结局里，都没有人问过密歇根的选民，他们到底想要什么。

The polling looks suspiciously good: Democrats projected to win by 11 points, the largest swing by any party in almost 20 years, and Trump entering the midterms with the lowest approval rating of any president since Truman (source: The Guardian). The numbers say the Republicans are in for a hammering. But "hammering" is the wrong metaphor — it implies something breaks. Nothing breaks.

The question isn't whether the Trump era ends. It's which layer of violence moves when it does. Malik supplies the answer herself: USAID is gone, the departments Doge eliminated or incapacitated probably aren't coming back, and there are entire tariff regimes, ICE's executive and budgetary empowerments, and a war with Iran that, despite its promised imminent end, looks open-ended. These aren't one man's moods. They are structural concrete, re-poured. Swap the president, the concrete stays.

The cultural layer is where it gets interesting. Maga held white evangelicals, working-class Latinos and Silicon Valley billionaires together through an anti-establishment fighting stance. Malik nails it: when you are the establishment, that stance stops working and all that's left is whining. But she also nails the other half — whoever comes next, even the charisma black hole that is JD Vance, may still make the same pitch work on the base. That is what weaponisation means: once the cognitive entry point is open, it doesn't matter who stands at the door. The script walks by itself.

The Democrats look worse. The old guard is either gerontocratic or gerontocracy-adjacent; Harris is described as an apparatchik offering continuity without gravitas. The only lively figure left is Obama, who passed the baton to Abdul El-Sayed, a man on the verge of becoming the state's first Muslim senator. And then? Because El-Sayed believes Israel has committed genocide in Gaza and should be defunded, he gets lukewarm endorsements and open attacks from senior Democrats.

The most informative detail isn't El-Sayed's position. It's Haley Stevens's turn. Narrowly beaten by him in the August primary, she spent the final days attacking his comments on Israel, then on Sunday endorsed his Republican opponent, Mike Rogers. David Kramer, who chaired her primary campaign, put it plainly: there's talk of a big tent, but sometimes we worry it's devolving into a circus and we're going to be pushed out. He is also voting for Rogers.

Translated: better to lose than to win on the left's terms. This isn't a policy disagreement. It's co-conspirators picking their side before they get pushed out. What Kramer is protecting isn't the Republican party — it's the old order that gives him a seat inside his own. El-Sayed's threat isn't that he might lose. It's that he might win, and winning would prove the old order isn't the only road.

So Malik is right, but not right enough. She says what follows may be long-Trump: a Republican party that can't plot a path without him, a Democratic party that can't locate what it stands for beyond opposing him. I'd add: this isn't two parties failing. It's a system maintaining itself at the meta level. The interpretive monopoly stays with the same people. Removing a president only removes a name that can be blamed.

2020 was a far better setup — a familiar Democratic candidate in Biden, a pandemic that research found hit Trump's support, no live Middle East crisis roiling domestic politics. Even then, the old order didn't come back. This time the setup is worse, and the best available outcome is a temporary setback.

Here's the splinter: if El-Sayed wins, he wins while being publicly undermined by co-conspirators in his own party. If he loses, he loses for the same reason. In neither outcome does anyone ask the voters of Michigan what they actually want.

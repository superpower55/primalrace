一部关于向上爬的音乐剧，和它不敢说的地面A Musical About Rising, and the Ground It Won't Name

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Vulture ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
他们为一个不敢说出口的名字欢呼，这就是全部的政治。
They cheered for a name left unsaid. That's the whole politics of it.

《In the Heights》这次在 City Center 复排，最响的掌声给了一句改词。原版里 Benny 幻想和 Tiger Woods 一起打球，这一版改成了别人。Vulture 的剧评写得很准："这是一部因为没说的话而赢得喝彩的制作。"

不说的东西很清楚。ICE 不在台上，特朗普不在台上，几十年来对这个国家移民英雄的威权转向不在台上。导演把整部戏锁在它自己的年代里——开场时 Graffiti Pete 正在 Usnavi 的小店上喷"'08"。于是观众坐在 2025 年的纽约，看一群 2008 年的移民后代在华盛顿高地做梦，而台下的现实是：做梦的资格本身正在被吊销。

这就是文化层的暴力，加尔通那个三角的最底层。它不需要施暴者，只需要一次演出里所有人都默契地绕开某个话题，然后为这个绕开鼓掌。舞台上的 upwardness——飞机、高架列车、乔治·华盛顿大桥的拱、在停电后才看得见的星座——全部朝上。朝上是这个剧的全部语法。但朝上需要一个地面，而地面现在归谁管，戏里一个字都没有。

剧评人自己也承认了这一点，然后把它写成一种美学上的克制。"这是一部因为没说的话而赢得喝彩的制作"——这句话被当作聪明的观察放进了文章，而不是被当作一个问题。谁在共谋？制作方、导演、观众、剧评人，全都在。回报是什么？一场愉快的、不必面对当下的夜晚。这不是谁的恶意，这是共谋最标准的样子：没有人下令，所有人都配合。

再说剧里的女人。剧评人写了一句很到位的话："《In the Heights》里的女人太完美了，完美到不像人。"Nina 从斯坦福差点退学，Vanessa 想搬到西四街，Abuela Claudia 中了彩票——她们都被写成仰望的角色，声音在 fermata 上高高飘着，像那些飞机的翼型。第三章讲得很清楚：当一个女性角色被写得"厉害但不入世"，最后落脚点仍然是和某个男人的关系，那她服务的还是男性中心的叙事。Nina 的痛苦是真的，但剧本只给了她一层薄薄的悲伤，剩下的空间留给了她父亲 Ivan Hernandez 扮演的那个"替罪羊"——所有戏剧冲突都由他的固执制造，好让其他角色围着他反应。至于他为什么反对 Nina 和 Benny，剧评人说，音乐剧和这一版制作都"太害羞了"，不肯说清。

太害羞。一个关于种族、阶级、移民的剧，在最核心的那条冲突线上害羞。

那么什么是好的？这一批演员。Matte Martinez 的 Usnavi 带一点疲惫的狡黠，Aaron James McKenzie 的 Benny 恋爱得很认真，Jaci Calderon 在 Nina 的成就外壳下面找那层愤怒——剧评人说她找对了方向，只是剧本给的材料不够。还有 Ishmael Gonzalez 的 Sonny，剧评人写："你会开始想，这音乐剧为什么不干脆就讲 Sonny 组织杂货店工人的未成年表亲们。"这句话是整篇评论里最锋利的一句，而它出现在一个玩笑的位置上。

最后那首歌里，"home"这个词在剧本里出现了 82 次（来源：Vulture）。终曲里 Miranda 把它拆成两个：一个飘在天上，一个砸在地上，天堂和地面的渴望在对位里同时响起来。剧评人说，要是你梦想的地方和你此刻所在的地方能是同一个就好了。

这个愿望成立的前提是，你被允许留在此刻所在的地方。而这一版制作为了保住那阵掌声，把这个前提从台上拿走了。

The loudest applause in this City Center revival of In the Heights goes to a changed lyric. In the original, Benny dreams of playing golf with Tiger Woods; this production swaps the name. The Vulture review puts it precisely: "This is a production that gets applause for what it doesn't say."

What it doesn't say is clear. ICE is not onstage. Trump is not onstage. The authoritarian turn against the immigrant heroes of this musical, across the decades since, is not onstage. The director locks the show inside its own era — Graffiti Pete is mid-tag on Usnavi's bodega with an "'08" when the curtain rises. So an audience in 2025 New York watches a group of 2008 immigrant descendants dream in Washington Heights, while the actual condition of dreaming is being revoked downstairs.

This is cultural violence, the bottom layer of Galtung's triangle. It needs no perpetrator. It needs only a whole evening in which everyone tacitly steps around a subject, and then applauds the stepping. The upwardness onstage — planes, elevated trains, the arches of the George Washington Bridge, constellations visible only after a blackout — all points up. Up is the entire grammar of the show. But up requires a ground, and who currently administers that ground gets not one word.

The critic sees this and files it as aesthetic restraint. "A production that gets applause for what it doesn't say" lands as a clever observation rather than a question. Who is complicit? The producers, the director, the audience, the critic. All of them. What's the payoff? A pleasant evening that doesn't have to face the present. No one ordered it. Everyone complied. That is what complicity looks like at its most standard.

Then the women. The review nails one line: "In the Heights's women are a little too perfect to make human." Nina nearly drops out of Stanford, Vanessa longs for West 4th Street, Abuela Claudia wins the lottery — all written as figures who look up, voices floating in fermatas like airfoil wings. The mechanism is familiar: when a female character is written as formidable but unworldly, and her arc still resolves through a man, she is serving a masculine-centric narrative. Nina's pain is real, but the book gives her a thin layer of sadness and hands the rest of the space to her father, played by Ivan Hernandez as the resident sin-eater — all the drama manufactured by his stubbornness so other characters can react around him. As for why he keeps Nina and Benny apart, the review says the musical and this production are "so shy" about it. Shy.

A show about race, class, and immigration is shy on its central conflict line.

What is good here is the cast. Matte Martinez's Usnavi carries a wry weariness; Aaron James McKenzie's Benny is earnestly lovesick; Jaci Calderon looks for the anger beneath Nina's achievement and is right to look, even if the book under-supplies her. And Ishmael Gonzalez's Sonny — the review writes: "you'll start to wonder why the musical isn't just about Sonny trying to organize the underage cousins of bodega workers everywhere." That is the sharpest sentence in the piece, and it sits in the position of a joke.

In the finale, the word home appears 82 times in the script (source: Vulture). Miranda splits it in two: one floating in the sky, one hitting the ground, heaven and earth yearning in counterpoint. The critic wishes the place you dream about and the place you are could be the same.

That wish requires being allowed to stay where you are. This production, to keep its applause, took that requirement off the stage.

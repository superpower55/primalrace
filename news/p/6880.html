一本回忆录，两个共谋者A Memoir, Two Co-conspirators

文娱 元暴力 · 文化层 Vulture ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要Sofia Franklyn 在回忆录《Daddy Issues》（11月10日出版）宣传期间接受《纽约时报》采访时称，Alex Cooper 的丈夫 Matt Kaplan 得知她出书后致电她的经纪人，大喊并威胁起诉。Unwell 方面代表否认 Kaplan 与 Verve 讨论过书中内容或起诉意图。Franklyn 还称听说 Kaplan 正设法拿到书的实际内容，联系出版人和她22岁在犹他州时的旧同事。
The newsIn a New York Times profile ahead of her memoir Daddy Issues, out November 10, Sofia Franklyn claims Alex Cooper's husband Matt Kaplan called her agent, screamed, and threatened to sue over the book. A representative for Unwell denied Kaplan discussed the book's claims or any intent to sue with Verve. Franklyn also says she's heard Kaplan is trying to get his hands on the book's contents.
女人互删，男人替她出手——这就是共谋的分工
Women erase each other; a man steps in for her—that's the division of labor.

Sofia Franklyn 和 Alex Cooper 的故事被讲成一段闺蜜反目，两个女人互相「抹掉」对方。但真正值得看的是那个打电话的人：Cooper 的丈夫 Matt Kaplan，据报道在得知 Franklyn 要出书后打给她的经纪人，大喊，威胁起诉。

这是共谋者理论的教科书案例。元暴力不要求每个男人亲自上阵，它要求的是有人愿意替结构出手，然后结构回报他。Kaplan 在这里的角色不是当事人——Call Her Daddy 的 IP、那句「Unwell」、2020 年的分手，都和他无关。他出手，是因为他站在 Cooper 的位置上，而 Cooper 的位置本身就是这场博弈的筹码。一个男人替一个女人出面威胁另一个女人，这不是保护，这是把两个女人的存在性战争收编进一个男人的产权逻辑里。

Franklyn 自己也没跳出这个逻辑。她说 Cooper「试图抹掉我，我也试着抹掉她」，然后「接受了我们永远绑在一起」。这句话说得很诚实，但它描述的不是和解，是承认这场博弈没有出口。两个女人被同一个 IP 绑在一起，而这个 IP 的争夺权最终落到了一个不在场的人手里。

报道里有一个细节值得停一下：Franklyn 说她听说 Kaplan 正在「设法拿到书的实际内容」，联系出版人，联系她22岁在犹他州时的旧同事。如果属实，这已经不是在回应指控，是在提前控制叙事——夺取认知入口，在书出版之前就决定它会被怎么读。Unwell 的代表否认 Kaplan 和 Verve 讨论过书的内容或起诉意图。否认的是「讨论」，不是「联系」。

这本书卖的是 Franklyn 的版本，但定价权不在她手里。她的身体、她的声音、她和 Cooper 的关系，都被打包成一个产品，而产品的风险由一个男人来管理。她得到的是一本回忆录和一个宣传期，付出的是把「我永远和这件事绑在一起」写进自己的叙事里。

Franklyn 说她「无法在不处理这件大事的情况下往前走」。问题是，处理完之后，那件大事还在，只是换了一个人来管。

The Sofia Franklyn–Alex Cooper story is being told as a falling-out between friends, two women erasing each other. The person worth looking at is the one who made the phone call: Cooper's husband, Matt Kaplan, who reportedly called Franklyn's agent, screamed, and threatened to sue after learning she was publishing a memoir.

This is the co-conspirator theory in plain sight. Meta-violence doesn't require every man to show up in person. It requires someone willing to act on the structure's behalf, and the structure rewards him. Kaplan is not a party to this dispute—the Call Her Daddy IP, the word "Unwell," the 2020 breakup, none of it is his. He acts because he stands in Cooper's position, and Cooper's position is itself the stake in this game. A man threatening one woman on behalf of another is not protection. It's two women's existential war being absorbed into one man's property logic.

Franklyn hasn't escaped that logic either. She says Cooper tried to erase her, and she tried to do it back, and then she came to terms with the fact that they will always be linked. That's honest, but it describes not a reconciliation—it describes a game with no exit. Two women bound to the same IP, while the power to adjudicate it ends up with someone who isn't in the room.

One detail deserves a pause: Franklyn says she's heard Kaplan is trying to get his hands on the actual contents of the book, reaching out to publicists and to people she worked with when she was 22 in Utah. If true, that isn't responding to claims—it's seizing the cognitive entry point, deciding how the book will be read before it's read. A representative for Unwell denied Kaplan discussed the book's claims or any intent to sue with Verve. What's denied is the discussion, not the outreach.

The book sells Franklyn's version, but she doesn't hold the pricing power. Her body, her voice, her relationship with Cooper—all packaged as a product, with its risk managed by a man. What she gets is a memoir and a press tour. What she pays is writing "I will always be linked to this" into her own story.

Franklyn says she couldn't move forward without addressing this big thing. The thing is still there. Someone else is just holding it now.

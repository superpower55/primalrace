产床上的镣铐Shackled on the Operating Table

国际 直接层 · 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 The Intercept ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要35岁的巴勒斯坦女子达娜·朱迪（Dana Joudeh）来自西岸纳布卢斯，被以色列无指控行政拘留期间发现怀孕，在押期间未获基本产检，出现出血和贫血后于9月12日在当地医院接受剖腹产，分娩时手脚仍戴镣铐。她与早产女儿莉娅于9月14日被带回监狱办理释放手续后获释。以色列监狱部门称相关指控「不实」。
The newsDana Joudeh, 35, from Nablus in the West Bank, discovered she was pregnant while held in Israeli administrative detention without charge. Denied basic prenatal care, she gave birth by C-section on September 12 while shackled, and was released with her premature daughter Lia on September 14. Israel's Prison Service called the allegations "false."
生育被当作惩罚，母职被当作驯服工具。
Birth treated as punishment, motherhood as a tool of驯服.

Dana Joudeh 在发现怀孕的那一刻戴着手铐脚镣。这不是一个医疗事故的故事，是暴力三角三层同时咬合的一份样本。

行政拘留不经起诉、不经审判，这是结构层的暴力：法律给了国家一个入口，把一个人从有权利的状态里拿出来，放进没有权利的状态里。怀孕不是这个入口的例外，是这个入口的延长线。她六个月的孕期只做过一次检查，那一次还是阳性测试本身；她请求维生素和叶酸，被拒绝；她请求止痛药，被拒绝，靠同监室的女人假装自己头痛骗出一片药偷偷给她。WHO 的产检标准在这里不是被违反，是根本没进入计算。

直接层的暴力是明写的：声音手榴弹在监室里炸响，士兵命令女囚趴在地上，警犬、脱衣搜查、内衣短缺。她感染疥疮，一整间牢房共用一罐外用药，不够，她们把它和氯水兑开来用。她出血两周，血红蛋白掉到 9 克每分升，才被送去医院。

然后是 9 月 12 日。局部麻醉下的剖腹产，她的手和脚被铐着，她被切开的时候看着手术进行，女兵持枪围在手术室里站岗。她的女儿早产，约六个月，进了新生儿重症监护。医生对着这个婴儿说：恐怖分子来了。

这句话是文化层，也是元层。它把一具刚出生的身体预先编进了叙事：不是婴儿，是恐怖分子；不是病人，是敌人；不是母亲，是囚犯。以色列监狱管理局的回应是这套叙事的官方版本——指控全是假的，一切依法，一切符合以色列的医疗标准。国家安全部长 Ben-Gvir 说得更直白：囚犯不会拿到比法定最低标准多一粒的东西。他举的例子是，他听说有囚犯多拿到两颗橄榄，于是去监狱视察。

“法定最低标准”这个词值得停一下。Violence = Potential − Actual。把 Actual 写进法条，再把法条当成上限，差额就被合法化了。这不是执行偏差，这是制度设计。

报道里还有一层更冷的对照。自 1972 年 Zakia Shammout 在 Neve Tirza 监狱由狱友接生，到 2008 年 17 岁的 Fatima al-Zaq 在狱中生下儿子 Youssef——这个孩子 2025 年 7 月在加沙城被以色列无人机炸死。2025 年 9 月是 Tahani Abu Samhan，一年后是 Joudeh。近十七年里只有两例被记录在案，维权者说，还有很多没被报出来。没被记录的部分本身就是材料：谁的经历没有进入档案，谁就没有进入事实。

同监室还有一个孕妇，Manar Karaja，截至 10 月 6 日仍被关押、仍在怀孕。Joudeh 出来了，Karaja 还没有。

报道说，Joudeh 现在在拉马拉的姐姐家里恢复，Lia 还在新生儿病房。她想办一场小小的庆祝，已经在想上什么菜、什么甜点，家里人聊起这件事的时候，她笑了。

这个笑不是结尾的安慰。它只是提醒：把她铐在手术台上的人，赌的就是她再也笑不出来。

Dana Joudeh was in shackles the moment she learned she was pregnant. This is not a story about a medical failure. It is a sample of all three layers of the Violence Triangle locking into each other.

Administrative detention—no charge, no trial—is structural violence: the law provides a door through which the state pulls a person out of a condition of rights and into a condition of none. Pregnancy is not an exception to that door. It is an extension of it. In six months she had one medical examination, and that was the positive test itself. She asked for vitamins and folic acid; refused. She asked for pain relief; refused, and got a single tablet because another woman faked a headache and slipped it to her. WHO prenatal guidelines are not violated here. They never enter the calculation.

Direct violence is spelled out: sound grenades detonated inside the prison, soldiers ordering the women face down on the floor, police dogs, strip searches, a shortage of underwear. She contracted scabies; one container of topical treatment was shared among the whole cell, not enough for everyone infected, so they mixed it with chlorine to stretch it. She bled for two weeks until her hemoglobin fell to 9 grams per deciliter before anyone sent her to a hospital.

Then September 12. A C-section under local anesthesia, her hands and feet shackled, watching the operation as it happened, female soldiers standing guard around her with guns in the operating room. Her daughter, born premature at around six months, went to neonatal intensive care. The doctor looked at the infant and said: the terrorist has arrived.

That sentence is cultural violence, and meta-violence underneath it. It pre-files a newborn body into a narrative: not an infant, a terrorist; not a patient, an enemy; not a mother, a prisoner. The Israel Prison Service's official response is the same narrative in bureaucratic form—the allegations are false, everything is by law, everything meets Israeli medical standards. National security minister Ben-Gvir puts it more plainly: prisoners will not receive a single grain more than the legal minimum. His example of enforcement was a monitoring visit because he heard inmates had gotten two extra olives.

Stop on the phrase "legal minimum." Violence = Potential − Actual. Write the Actual into statute, then treat the statute as a ceiling, and the gap becomes lawful. This is not a failure of implementation. It is the design.

The report carries a colder layer of comparison. From Zakia Shammout, who delivered her daughter Nadia at Neve Tirza prison in 1972 with fellow prisoners assisting, to Fatima al-Zaq, 17, who gave birth to her son Youssef in prison in 2008—a boy killed in July 2025 when an Israeli drone hit his family's apartment in Gaza City. September 2025 was Tahani Abu Samhan. A year later, Joudeh. Only two documented births in nearly seventeen years; advocates say many cases go unreported. The unreported part is itself the material: whoever's account never enters the record never enters the facts.

Another pregnant woman, Manar Karaja, was still in Israeli detention and still pregnant as of October 6. Joudeh is out. Karaja is not.

Joudeh is now recovering at her sister's home in Ramallah; Lia remains in the neonatal unit. She wants a small celebration, and she is already thinking about the food and the sweets. When the family talks about it, she smiles.

The smile is not the consolation at the end. It is a reminder of what the people who shackled her to an operating table were betting against.

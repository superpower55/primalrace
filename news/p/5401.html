TikTok 队列与中产阶级的空间战争TikTok Queues and the Bourgeois Space War

哲学 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-04 § 链接
公共空间的本质是权力博弈，而非关于“美 mayonnaise”的卫生讨论。
Public space is a battleground for power, not a debate over mayonnaise and hygiene.

这起阿姆斯特丹炸薯条店的诉讼，表面上是关于卫生、海鸥和老鼠的“邻里纠纷”，实际上是一场典型的关于空间占有权 (Taking Space) 的存在性战争。居民们口中的“战争”，本质上是由于 TikTok 制造的 viral urbanism 破坏了他们原有的阶级审美屏障。对于住在 exclusive neighbourhood 的中产来说，街道不仅是通道，更是他们身份表达的延伸——那是安静的、有序的、具有特定“品味”的私人化公共空间。

当大量被 influencer 驱动的游客涌入，将街道变成一个巨大的、嘈杂的、充满蛋黄酱气味的“表演场”时，这种空间被“污染”了。这里的污染不是指物理上的脏，而是指原本由特定阶级垄断的认知入口被低端消费主义暴力拆除。居民们感到愤怒，是因为他们失去了对这个空间定义权的掌控。他们试图通过法律手段，将这种“不合时宜”的表达驱逐到更远的地方，从而恢复他们对社区“纯洁性”的定义。

而店主们用“心血”和“孩子”这种情感叙事来防御，其实是在试图用一种伪装的弱势来博取公正。但这场博弈的真正共谋者是市政府。市议员在法庭上权衡旅游业带来的 7 万个就业岗位与居民的居住环境，这揭示了 structural violence 的逻辑：城市空间被量化为 GDP 和就业率，而具体的生存体验则成了可以被交易的筹码。在这个逻辑里，无论是被驱逐的游客还是被侵扰的居民，都只是资本运作下的副产品。

最讽刺的是那位居民提到的“奥威尔式”控制。他指责游客被 influencer 操纵，却没意识到他自己正试图通过法律这个权力机器，去实施另一种形式的、基于阶级优越感的精神统治。这根本不是关于薯条的战争，而是关于谁有权决定这块地砖属于谁的战争。

This lawsuit over an Amsterdam chip shop is framed as a neighborhood dispute about hygiene and seagulls, but it is actually an existential war over the right of taking space. For the residents of an exclusive neighbourhood, the street is not just a thoroughfare; it is an extension of their identity expression—a privatized public space defined by quietude, order, and a specific class-based taste.

When waves of tourists, driven by the viral urbanism of TikTok, transform the street into a loud, mayonnaise-scented performance stage, the space is "polluted." This pollution is not physical, but semiotic: the cognitive entry points previously monopolized by a specific class have been violently dismantled by low-end consumerism. The residents' anger stems from the loss of their power to define the narrative of their own environment. They are using the law to exile this "inappropriate" expression, attempting to restore a curated purity to their community.

Meanwhile, the owners defend themselves with emotional narratives of "heart and soul," attempting to perform a strategic vulnerability. However, the true complicity lies with the city government. The councillor’s balancing act between 70,000 tourism jobs and the residential environment reveals the logic of structural violence: urban space is quantified into GDP and employment rates, while actual lived experience becomes a tradable commodity. In this system, both the displaced tourist and the annoyed resident are merely byproducts of capital operations.

It is peak irony when a resident describes the situation as "Orwellian," claiming tourists are led by the "higher power" of influencers. He fails to see that he is attempting to use the legal machine to exercise another form of spiritual dominance based on class superiority. This isn't a war over fries; it is a war over who owns the right to define the pavement.

一封阿拉伯语信，八年谎言One Arabic Letter, Eight Years of Lies

国际 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
谎言的入口不是证据，是没人读得懂原文
The lie's entry point isn't evidence — it's that nobody can read the original.

一封2012年的公开信，因为只有阿拉伯语版本，就成了可以随便填内容的容器。八年后，它被填成了"支持穆斯林兄弟会"，再被填成"支持恐怖分子"，最后配上哈马斯武装分子的画面，做成竞选广告。这条链上每一环都不需要证据，只需要没人有能力核对原文。

这就是第三章说的认知入口：夺取"什么是事实"的制造权，不必先赢下辩论，只要先占住叙事。Loomer自称"骄傲的伊斯兰恐惧者"，她的产品不是判断，是一句可以被反复搬运的断言。搬运的人不需要相信它，只需要它能用。

更值得看的是共谋的层次。Rogers 2012年自己说过，穆斯林兄弟会里有"温和的人"是"我们得去接触和交谈的"——那时他是众议院情报委员会主席，说的是当时美国政府的实际立场。今天他说El-Sayed"和这些恐怖分子有联系"。同一张嘴，前后两套事实，中间没有任何新证据，只有一场选举。这不是立场改变，是武器换手。

报道里最锋利的一处，是那位埃及记者的话：美国右翼直接借用了阿拉伯威权政权在阿拉伯之春前后的剧本——把一切民主诉求说成伊斯兰主义驱动，于是任何对独裁的挑战都自动通向极端主义和恐怖。这个剧本的妙处在于它自我封闭：你无法自证清白，因为自证本身会被读成掩饰。El-Sayed在辩论里说"当然，绝对"愿意切割兄弟会，这句话立刻变成新的素材。

性别在这里没有缺席，只是换了位置。整条叙事的原型是Huma Abedin——一个在希拉里身边的穆斯林女性，被指认为兄弟会的秘密特工，这个阴谋论从华盛顿一路传到开罗，被埃及基督徒领袖在克林顿访问时当面提出。一个女人的家庭关系被做成国家级的安全威胁，然后这套模板被回收，套在一个男人身上。原初种族的构成逻辑从来不只针对女性：先定义谁是"不忠的"，再让这个定义自己繁殖。

报道还写到了El-Sayed的同母异父姐姐Eman Abdelhadi的推文，2012年她写母亲七十年代初在大学里参与兄弟会的活动。这些推文被右翼媒体拿去当证据。El-Sayed的回应是：我不是我母亲养大的，我从没和她谈过她当年的事。这句话在事实层面成立，在叙事层面毫无用处——因为指控要的从来不是他和他母亲的关系，而是"穆斯林"这个标签可以无限外延。

Rogers的广告结尾说"激进意识形态是对我们国家安全的威胁"。真正的威胁是另一件事：一场参议院选举的最终陈述，可以完全建立在一封没人读过的信上，而所有有能力读它的人——埃及记者、原文、签署者本人——的声音，进不了美国政治新闻的版面。这不是信息不对称，这是被制造出来的不对称。

那封信要求的是清退旧政权的警察和国家媒体。它被说成支持恐怖主义。这个反转里没有被缩小的差额，只有被扩大的：一个候选人要花力气否认一件他没做过的事，而否认本身成为他有罪的旁证。

A 2012 open letter, published only in Arabic, became a container anyone could fill. Eight years later it was filled with "supports the Muslim Brotherhood," then "supports terrorists," then cut into a campaign ad over footage of Hamas militants. No link in that chain needed evidence. It only needed nobody able to check the source text.

This is the cognitive entry point from Chapter 3: seize the power to manufacture what counts as fact. You don't have to win the argument first. You only have to occupy the narrative first. Loomer calls herself "a proud Islamophobe"; her product isn't a judgment, it's an assertion built to be carried. The people carrying it don't have to believe it. They only need it to work.

What's worth watching is the layering of complicity. In 2012 Rogers himself said there were "moderate Muslim Brotherhood individuals we are going to have to reach out to and talk to" — he was chair of the House intelligence committee, describing the actual US position. Today he says El-Sayed "has ties to these terrorists." Same mouth, two sets of facts, no new evidence in between, only an election. That isn't a change of conviction. It's a weapon changing hands.

The sharpest line in the piece belongs to the Egyptian journalist: the American right is borrowing directly from the playbook of Arab authoritarian regimes, casting every demand for democracy as inherently Islamist, so that any challenge to dictatorship automatically leads to extremism or terrorism. The beauty of that script is that it closes on itself. You cannot prove your innocence, because proving it reads as concealment. El-Sayed said "Sure, yeah, absolutely" when asked to disavow the Brotherhood. That answer became new material.

Gender isn't absent here, only relocated. The template is Huma Abedin — a Muslim woman beside Hillary Clinton, accused of being a secret Brotherhood agent, a conspiracy that traveled from Washington to Cairo and was raised by Egyptian Christian leaders during Clinton's visit. One woman's family ties turned into a national security threat, then the template was recycled onto a man. The logic of the Primal Race was never only about women: define who is disloyal, then let the definition reproduce itself.

The piece also covers tweets from El-Sayed's half-sister, Eman Abdelhadi, who in 2012 wrote about their mother's activism in the Brotherhood in the early seventies. Rightwing outlets seized on them as evidence. El-Sayed's answer: I wasn't raised by my mother, and I never talked to her about her student years. True as fact, useless as narrative — because the accusation was never about his relationship with his mother. It was about a label that extends indefinitely.

Rogers's ad closes with "radical ideology is a threat to our national security." The actual threat is elsewhere: a Senate campaign's closing message can rest entirely on a letter nobody has read, while everyone who could read it — the Egyptian journalist, the original text, the signatories themselves — stays outside the frame of American political coverage. That isn't an information gap. It's a manufactured one.

The letter asked for the purge of old-regime police and state media. It was sold as support for terrorism. Nothing in that inversion was narrowed. The gap only widened: a candidate spends his campaign denying something he never did, and the denial becomes proof of guilt.

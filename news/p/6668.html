选情越紧，他越值钱The Tighter the Race, the More He's Worth

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Rolling Stone ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
焦虑被包装成专业，选票被压成一格一格的数据
Anxiety packaged as expertise; voters compressed into cells

Steve Kornacki 的焦虑是一门生意。Rolling Stone 这篇人物稿写他选举前几周没怎么出门，躲在 NBC 洛克菲勒中心的办公室里，对着一张宽到他要说"有人敲门我看不见"的显示器翻密歇根的数据表，83 个县、每个年龄、种族、性别、家庭收入分组、好几年的大选，几千个格子。他说这像期末复习。稿子把这写成敬业。

敬业是真的。但值得看的是这套东西在卖什么。Kornacki 在 MSNBC 的选举夜播了十年，卷袖子、New Balance、对选区级美国政治史的"雨人式"记忆，观众吃这一套。今年 NBC 给他排了 13 个小时的直播，从周二下午五点到周三凌晨，外加 YouTube 上一个专门的"Kornacki Cam"。一个分析师被做成了一档可以连续观看的节目，他的紧张、他的咖啡、他今天换成低因，全是节目的一部分。

他自己也把机制说出来了："无知有一种宁静，我早年的职业生涯大概受益于此。"做得越多，越知道自己不知道多少。这句话是整篇稿子里最诚实的一句。选举夜的分析师站在一块 96 英寸的触摸屏前，把几万张选票的流向拆成红蓝两色的格子，观众得到的是"我看懂了"的感觉。至于这些格子背后是谁在排队、谁没来、谁被删出了选民册，屏幕不显示。

稿子里有一个细节值得停一下。他盯着韦恩县，说那里的民主党投票率两年前"掉下了悬崖"，他特别点出这是密歇根阿拉伯裔和穆斯林人口集中的地方。奥克兰县则是密歇根犹太选民最集中的地方。他把这两个县并排放在一张表里看。这是选情分析，技术上没问题。但这也是把一个群体在加沙问题上的愤怒，换算成 Abdul El-Sayed 在奥克兰县能不能拿到两位数差距、换算成"全州对 Rogers 有利"。人的立场被压成一个可以预测的百分点。

Kornacki 讲 2016 年那次事故，说他在台上铺了五分钟，最后大屏跳出 700 张选举人票，他在直播里骂了一句"这破玩意儿"。他补了一句，技术团队很棒，这东西进步了很多光年，但总有这种风险。风险说的是屏幕会出错。他没说的是，屏幕算得再准，算的也只是谁赢，不算赢的代价落在谁头上。

稿子结尾很温柔：他去哈德逊河边的小公园走二十分钟，再走回来，回到他的表格。焦虑被安抚了，节目照常。13 个小时之后，那块 96 英寸的屏幕会亮起来，几千个格子会变成一张地图，地图会变成一个说法。说法是谁的，没人问。

Steve Kornacki's anxiety is a product. Rolling Stone's profile has him holed up for weeks before the election in his office at NBC's Rockefeller Center, staring at a monitor so wide he says people knock and he can't see them, scrolling a Michigan dataset: 83 counties, every age, race, gender and household-income bracket, several election cycles, thousands of cells. He calls it cramming for a final exam. The piece calls it dedication.

The dedication is real. What's worth looking at is what the whole apparatus is selling. Kornacki has done MSNBC's election nights for a decade — rolled sleeves, New Balance sneakers, a Rain Man-like recall of precinct-level American political history — and viewers eat it up. This year NBC has given him a 13-hour broadcast, from 5 p.m. Tuesday until dawn Wednesday, plus a dedicated "Kornacki Cam" livestream on YouTube. An analyst has been turned into a show you can watch continuously. His nerves, his coffee, his switch to decaf today — all of it is part of the show.

He states the mechanism himself: "There's sort of a serenity in ignorance that I think I benefited from earlier in my career." The more he does this, the more he knows how much he doesn't know. That's the most honest line in the piece. An analyst stands in front of a 96-inch touchscreen and breaks the flow of tens of thousands of ballots into red and blue cells, and the viewer gets the feeling of having understood something. What the cells don't show is who stood in line, who stayed home, who got purged from the rolls.

One detail is worth pausing on. He's watching Wayne County, where he says Democratic turnout "fell off a cliff" two years ago, and he points out it's where much of the state's Arab-American and Muslim population lives. Oakland County has the highest concentration of Michigan's Jewish voters. He reads the two counties side by side in one table. Technically that's race analysis. It's also a community's anger over Gaza converted into whether Abdul El-Sayed clears single digits in Oakland County, converted into "advantage Rogers statewide." A political position gets compressed into a predictable percentage point.

Kornacki recounts the 2016 screwup: five minutes of windup, then the board flashed electoral math adding up to 700, and he said on air, "This piece of garbage!" He adds that the tech team is great, the thing has advanced light years, but there are always risks like that. The risk he names is the screen malfunctioning. What he doesn't name is that however accurately the screen adds up, it only counts who wins — not who pays for the win.

The piece ends gently: a twenty-minute walk to a small park on the Hudson, then back to the spreadsheets. The anxiety gets soothed, the show goes on. Thirteen hours later the 96-inch screen lights up, the thousands of cells become a map, and the map becomes a story. Whose story it is — nobody asks.

重划选区只是最后一步Redrawing the Map Was the Last Step

国际 结构层 · 文化层 · 直接层 ProPublica ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
先拆掉你的投票权，再让你投我一票。
First strip their votes of power, then ask them to vote for you.

田纳西州把孟菲斯切成三块，第九选区从四十多年的黑人多数选区变成白人多数选区——这一步是明面上的。但 ProPublica 这篇报道真正拆开的是更早的一步：在 Brent Taylor 宣布参选之前，他已经用四年的州参议员任期，把孟菲斯选民选出来的官员一个一个削成了摆设。

选区重划改变的是「谁来投票」，preemption 改变的是「投了票有什么用」。这两件事加起来才构成完整的操作：先让选举结果不再代表你，再让你在失去代表性的选区里把票投给那个拆掉你的人。Taylor 在社交媒体上发的照片配文是「我朋友 Tim Burchett 在教我门道」，门道他其实早就学会了。

看那几条法。孟菲斯市议会全票通过禁止警察以尾灯坏了这类轻微交通违规为由拦车——那是 2023 年 Tyré Nichols 被拦下、被打了三分钟、三天后死在医院之后。Taylor 第二年就推翻了这条禁令。Nichols 的母亲 RowVaughn Wells 在议会恳求撤案，Taylor 在参议院回应说，他作为殡仪馆老板同情这个家庭，但「不能让这种同情模糊了我们保护七百万田纳西人的判断」。

这句话值得停一下。七百万田纳西人的安全，被用来否决一个城市对自己警察的约束。而八个月后，司法部调查孟菲斯警局的结论是：这种激进的交通执法伤害了孟菲斯人，对黑人居民构成非法歧视。这份报告在特朗普上台后被撤回了。

保释那条更直接。Shelby 县原本有一个跨党派的保释改革方案，核心是「支付能力计算器」——因为州法本来就要求法官考虑被告的经济状况。Taylor 推的法把这句话改成了「不得考虑被告的支付能力」。前法官 Bill Anderson 说，这「捆住了法官的手」。孟菲斯大学 2025 年的研究显示，Taylor 的法生效后保释金额中位数上升了。

注意这里的机制：这不是「打击犯罪」和「宽容犯罪」的路线之争。这是把一个地方选民选出来的、有权做决定的职位，改造成必须向州议会汇报的岗位。地区检察官 Steve Mulroy 是 25 年来 Shelby 县第一个民主党籍 DA，Taylor 先把他称为「极左狂热分子」，再推动州检察长审查他办公室的每一个案件，再要求每一起认罪协议和撤诉在十个工作日内上报州和联邦司法部。Mulroy 的说法是，这等于在旁边安了一个「未经选举的影子 DA」。

Fordham 法学院的 John Pfaff 把这件事说得很清楚：这类「严打」立法历史上一直是「以公共安全为名，对不受欢迎的少数群体实施社会控制」。他还补了一句更狠的——郊区和农村选民在市政选举里投不了票，但他们控制着州议会，于是通过 preemption 法律「告诉孟菲斯他们想要孟菲斯怎么做」。

这就是共谋的结构。不是一个人在使坏，是一整套规则在配合：州议会里白人多数派的席位、被最高法院 4 月裁决削弱的 gerrymandering 诉讼、一笔 150 万美元的竞选自贷款。Taylor 住在孟菲斯东边一个几乎全白的小镇，一栋内战前风格的大宅里，把殡仪馆生意卖给私募基金赚了几百万，现在要代表他刚刚削过权的那些人。

他的对手 Justin J. Pearson 31 岁，黑人，因为阻止一条穿过黑人社区的输油管道上过全国新闻，也因为 2023 年校园枪击案后带头抗议控枪被共和党超级多数驱逐过一次。Pearson 谈的是学校和就业机会，Taylor 谈的是起诉和关押。报道说 Pearson 的筹款超过了 Taylor——但报道也说，由于重划选区，Taylor 是获胜热门。

所以这篇报道最冷的地方不是哪一条法，是那个时间顺序：Taylor 先花四年拆掉孟菲斯选民选出来的官员的权力，然后在一个被重新画过的选区里请这些选民把票投给他。选区重划是最后一步，不是第一步。

报道里没有写的是：那 65 个自 2018 年以来死在 Shelby 县监狱里的人，他们的名字。Anderson 说 Taylor「称这些为改革法案，但它们不是改革」。改革这个词被拿去当了武器，这是文化层的活；被它压住的是结构层的资源分配和直接层的人——被拦下的车、付不起保释金的人、死在监狱里的人。三层同时动，而元暴力在底下托着：谁有权定义什么叫安全，谁就有权定义谁不安全。

Tennessee carved Memphis into three congressional districts this spring, and the Ninth—for over four decades the state's only majority-Black district—is now majority white. That's the visible move. What ProPublica actually unpacks is the earlier one: before Brent Taylor announced his candidacy, he had spent four years in the state Senate stripping authority from the officials Memphis voters elected.

Redistricting changes who votes. Preemption changes whether voting does anything. Put them together and you get the full operation: first make the election stop representing you, then ask you to cast a ballot for the man who dismantled your representation.

Look at the laws. The Memphis City Council voted unanimously to bar police from pulling drivers over for minor infractions like a broken taillight—that was after TyRé Nichols was stopped, punched and kicked for three minutes, and died in the hospital three days later in 2023. Taylor undid the ordinance the next year. Nichols' mother, RowVaughn Wells, pleaded with lawmakers to drop the bill. Taylor responded on the Senate floor that as a funeral home director he empathized with the family, but "we can't let that empathy, Mr. Speaker, cloud our judgment in protecting 7 million Tennesseans."

Sit with that sentence. Seven million Tennesseans' safety was invoked to overrule a city's attempt to restrain its own police. Eight months later, the Justice Department's investigation found that aggressive traffic enforcement in Memphis had harmed residents and unlawfully discriminated against Black residents. The Trump administration retracted those findings in 2025.

The bail law is more direct. Shelby County had a bipartisan bail order built around an "ability to pay" calculator—state law already required judges to consider a defendant's financial condition. Taylor's bill changed the language to "the defendant's ability to pay shall not be considered." Former judge Bill Anderson said it "tied our hands." A 2025 University of Memphis study found the median bail amount rose after the law took effect.

Notice the mechanism here. This is not a debate between tough-on-crime and soft-on-crime. This is converting locally elected offices with decision-making power into positions that must report back to the state legislature. District Attorney Steve Mulroy is the first Democrat elected DA in Shelby County in 25 years. Taylor called him a "far-left zealot," then pushed through a law letting the state attorney general review every case his office handles, then another requiring every plea deal, dismissal or charge reduction to be reported to the state and the Justice Department within ten business days. Mulroy's description: "an unelected shadow DA operating in parallel to the duly elected DA."

John Pfaff of Fordham Law puts it plainly: tough-on-crime laws like these have historically been "a form of social control against disliked minority groups" under the guise of public safety. He adds the sharper point—suburban and rural voters can't cast ballots in city elections, but they control the statehouse, so they use preemption laws to "finally tell Memphis what they want Memphis to do."

This is the structure of complicity. Not one man being cruel—a whole rulebook cooperating: the white-majority legislature, the Supreme Court's April ruling making gerrymandering harder to challenge, a $1.5 million campaign self-loan. Taylor lives in an antebellum-style mansion in a tiny, heavily white community east of Memphis, sold his funeral home businesses to a private equity firm for millions, and now wants to represent the people whose power he just spent four years removing.

His opponent, Justin J. Pearson, is 31, Black, and first made national headlines for stopping an oil pipeline through a Black Memphis neighborhood. He was also one of two Democratic legislators expelled by the Republican supermajority after leading a gun-control protest following a 2023 school shooting. Pearson talks about schools and economic opportunity. Taylor talks about prosecution and incarceration. The report says Pearson has outraised Taylor—and that Taylor is favored to win because of redistricting.

The coldest thing in this story isn't any single law. It's the sequence: Taylor spent four years dismantling the power of officials Memphis voters elected, then asked those same voters to send him to Congress from a district redrawn to make him the favorite.

What the report doesn't name: the 65 people who have died in the Shelby County jail since 2018. Anderson says Taylor "called them reform acts, but they're not reform." Reform was the weaponized word—that's cultural violence doing its work. Underneath it: structural violence redistributing resources, direct violence in the form of traffic stops, bail amounts, jail deaths. Three layers moving at once, with the old question humming underneath: who gets to define safety, and who gets defined as unsafe.

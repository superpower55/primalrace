六万人跑过五座桥，奖台只给一部分人Sixty Thousand Cross Five Bridges, the Podium Opens for Some

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 The New York Times ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要2025年纽约马拉松周日举行，约6万人参赛。瑞典选手阿尔姆格伦首次参加马拉松即夺得男子冠军，成绩2小时6分1秒；肯尼亚选手切普基鲁伊以2小时24分36秒赢得女子冠军。瑞士选手包揽轮椅组两项冠军。
The newsAbout 60,000 runners took part in the New York City Marathon on Sunday. Sweden's Andreas Almgren won the men's race in his marathon debut in 2:06:01, and Kenya's Sheila Chepkirui won the women's race in 2:24:36. Swiss racers won both wheelchair divisions.
参赛资格被卖成商品，领奖台才开始学着开门
Entry is priced as a product; the podium is only learning to open its door.

纽约马拉松今年第一次给五个残障组别设了领奖台——视力与失明、智力与发育障碍、协调障碍、下肢障碍、上肢障碍，男女各取前三。没有奖金。组织方纽约跑者协会的多元官说这是一个「试点」，是「向前迈出的重要一步」。

把这句话拆开看：一个办了五十多年的赛事，今年才想起来，赛道上一直有另一批人在跑，只是没被算进名次。轮椅组从2000年才有竞技组别，2001年才加上奖金。其余五类，等到2026年。这不是慈善，这是把「谁算运动员」这个定义往前挪了一格。定义权在谁手里，谁就先跑。

再看另一边。想跑进这条赛道，最流行的一条路是9+1项目：跑九场比赛、做一次志愿，换一个名额。从2028年起，这个项目改成抽签，还要交99美元入会费，九场比赛每一场都另外收费。抽签那条路，今年有近25万人报名，中签率1%，2400人。将近六万个名额里约四分之一留给替慈善募款的人。

也就是说，同一场比赛里，一边是刚被承认「你们也算在比赛」，一边是入场券被明确地标上了价。表达是有价格的，这句话在这里不需要比喻——它就是一个数字，99美元加九场报名费。跑得起的人跑，跑不起的人看直播。

报道里最动人的细节都不是冠军。45岁的消防员David Cargin跑首马，妻子刚被裁员，他一边训练一边上双班；七岁的儿子在21英里处看到他还站着，哭了。82岁的Connie Brown要跑她的第46次，她说最后七英里才是真正的一半。45岁的Michael Dinolfo在最后一座桥前用扩音器喊「好消息，这是最后一座该死的桥」。这些人在用身体确证自己是谁，而赛事组织方在同一份日程表上，把这份确证做成了分级产品。

残奥选手Dani Aravich说得很准：组织方和赞助商以前不觉得需要投钱，因为起跑线上看不到多少残障运动员——但很大一部分原因是，残障运动员不觉得自己被欢迎。这句话把因果倒过来了，也把责任放回了原处。看不到人，不是因为没人来，是因为门没开，然后拿「没人来」当门不用开的理由。

所以这条新闻里真正被削掉的差额，是文化层的一小块：领奖台承认了这批人的存在。但结构层的门在同一周被拧得更紧了一点。Ver Kuilen和Aravich在场外做的事情——推动保险公司为运动假肢付费——才是这条赛道上更硬的那一段。领奖台可以拍照，保险条款不能。

For the first time, the New York City Marathon awarded podium places in five Para athletics categories: low vision and blind, intellectual and developmental disability, coordination difficulties, lower limb disability, upper limb disability. First, second and third for men and women. No prize money. The chief diversity officer for New York Road Runners called it a pilot, another important step forward.

Take that sentence apart. A race that has run for over half a century only now remembered that another group of people has been on the course the whole time, just never counted in the results. Wheelchair racers got a competitive division in 2000 and prize money the year after. The other five categories waited until 2026. This is not charity. It is the definition of who counts as an athlete being nudged one notch. Whoever holds the definition runs first.

Now the other side. The most popular route into the field has been the 9+1 program: run nine races, volunteer once, earn a spot. Starting with the 2028 race, entry becomes a drawing, members pay $99 to join, and they pay to enter each of the nine required races. The lottery drew nearly a quarter of a million applicants this year. One percent got in, 2,400 people. About a quarter of the nearly 60,000 bibs are set aside for charity fundraisers.

So in the same event, one group is just being told it counts as competing, while the ticket in is being explicitly priced. Expression has a price tag here, and it is not a metaphor. It is a number: $99 plus nine race fees. Those who can pay, run. The rest watch the livestream.

The best details in the report are not the champions. David Cargin, a 45-year-old firefighter running his first marathon, training while working double shifts after his wife was laid off; his seven-year-old son bursting into tears of relief at mile 21 when he saw his father still standing. Connie Brown, 82, running her 46th New York City Marathon, saying the last seven miles are the true halfway point. Michael Dinolfo, 45, through a megaphone before the last bridge: good news, this is the last damn bridge. These people are confirming who they are with their bodies, while the organizers turn that same confirmation into a tiered product on the same schedule.

Paralympian Dani Aravich put it precisely: organizers and sponsors did not see a need to invest because they did not see many people at the start line. But a huge part of the reason was that athletes with disabilities did not feel welcome. That flips the causality and puts the responsibility back where it belongs. You do not see them not because they did not come, but because the door was shut, and then you cite the empty room as the reason the door can stay shut.

The gap actually closed here is a small piece of the cultural layer: the podium admits these athletes exist. But the structural door was screwed tighter in the same week. What Ver Kuilen and Aravich do off the course, pushing insurers to cover sporting prostheses, is the harder stretch of this race. A podium can be photographed. An insurance clause cannot.

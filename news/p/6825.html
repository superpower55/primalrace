她拿到了署名，然后砸了那座桥She Got the Credit, Then Burned the Bridge

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Vanity Fair ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要《纽约时报》畅销书作家托米·阿德耶米（Tomi Adeyemi）在《名利场》专访中宣传新作《The Siren》，却拒绝谈论其处女作《血与骨之子》的电影改编。她此前在已删除的TikTok中表示不会看该片，并屏蔽了主演阿曼德拉·斯坦伯格。报道称她获得了比多数新人作者更多的创作控制权，包括联合编剧署名，但自与ICM签约后已多次更换经纪公司。
The newsBestselling author Tomi Adeyemi promotes her new novel The Siren in a Vanity Fair interview but refuses to discuss the film adaptation of her debut, Children of Blood and Bone. In now-deleted TikToks she said she won't watch the film and blocked its lead, Amandla Stenberg. Sources say she had more creative control than most first-time authors, including a co-screenwriting credit, yet has switched talent agencies multiple times.
作者拿到控制权的那一刻，控制权就开始反过来控制她。
The moment an author wins control, control starts winning over her.

这篇专访最诚实的一句话不是阿德耶米说的，是那个圈内人说的：「她烧掉了每一座桥。」

烧桥的人通常有两种：一种是被逼到没路走，一种是走完了发现自己不需要桥。阿德耶米看起来是后一种，但报道里那句「她获得了比多数新人作者更多的创作控制权，包括联合编剧署名」，让这个判断站不住。她不是没拿到东西，她拿到了，然后她不要了。

这就是表达和所有权之间的裂缝。一本书是作者的表达，卖出版权的那一刻，表达被拆成了原材料：角色、世界观、对白，全部交给另一群人重新组装。组装出来的东西还叫《血与骨之子》，但已经不是她的了。她说「卖出版权基本上就是把它送给别人」，这句话没有修辞，是字面意思。

所以她的沉默不是姿态，是止损。采访里她两次关掉摄像头、七分钟不说话、把话题拐去《The Siren》登上纽约时报榜第三名。这些动作都在说同一件事：我控制不了那个东西，但我控制得了我提不提它。

问题在于，这个「控制」是假的。她不提，报道替她提；她屏蔽主演，报道写出屏蔽；她删掉TikTok，报道把删掉这件事写进正文。她越想收回解释权，解释权越往外跑。这是第三章的机制在她身上反向运转：她曾经用表达建立起自己的名字，现在这个名字被别人拿去讲述一个她不在场的故事。

报道里还有一个细节值得停一下：她提到「所有女性都必须与自己的力量保持连接，黑人女性尤其，深肤色的黑人女性更是如此」，然后是「如果你是自己唯一的捍卫者，你就必须有爪子」。这句话被放在关于电影的那段之后，像是回答，其实是转移。她把一场具体的、有名有姓的冲突，翻译成了一句可以印在海报上的赋权格言。

这不是她的错。在一个要求女性作者把每一次愤怒都包装成「力量」的行业里，直接说「我和某个演员闹翻了，我很生气」是不被允许的表达。她只能把爪子亮出来，但不能说抓的是谁。

那篇报道最后问她会不会再改编《The Siren》，她说不会，「我已经走过那段路了」。这句话是整篇里唯一一个不需要翻译的判断。她不是放弃了银幕，她是算清楚了一笔账：改编的收益归别人，代价归自己。

只是这笔账里还有一项没被算进去——那个在TikTok上说「这是我这辈子经历过最糟糕的事」的人，和那个在镜头前唱《My Way》的人，是同一个。报道把这两个人并排放着，没有解释，也不需要解释。

她新书里的反派叫Roux，和《饥饿游戏》里斯坦伯格的角色Rue同音不同拼。报道说这「值得注意」，然后没有再说下去。这个停顿比任何一句话都响。

The most honest line in this profile isn't Adeyemi's. It's the insider's: "She's burned every bridge."

People burn bridges for two reasons: they're cornered, or they've crossed and realized they don't need the bridge anymore. Adeyemi looks like the second kind—except for one detail. Sources say she got more creative control than most first-time authors, including a co-screenwriting credit. She didn't fail to get the thing. She got it, and then she didn't want it.

That's the crack between expression and ownership. A book is the author's expression. The moment you sell the rights, that expression gets broken down into raw material—characters, world, dialogue—and handed to another group to reassemble. What comes out is still called Children of Blood and Bone, but it isn't hers. "Selling the rights to your novel means essentially giving it away," she says. That's not a metaphor. That's a receipt.

So her silence isn't a pose. It's damage control. She turns off her camera twice. Sits in silence for seven minutes. Redirects to The Siren hitting number three on the New York Times list. Every one of these moves says the same thing: I can't control that thing, but I can control whether I mention it.

Except that control is fake. She doesn't bring it up; the profile brings it up for her. She blocks the lead actress; the profile writes down the blocking. She deletes the TikToks; the deletion becomes a paragraph. The harder she pulls the narrative back, the further it runs. This is the weaponization mechanism running in reverse on her: she built a name through expression, and now that name is being used to tell a story she isn't in.

There's one more detail worth stopping on. She says all women have to be in tune with their power, Black women especially, dark-skinned Black women especially—and then: "If you're the only one there to defend yourself, you have to have the claws." It's placed right after the question about the film. It reads like an answer. It's a deflection. She takes a specific, named conflict and translates it into a line that could go on a poster.

That's not her failure. In an industry that requires women authors to package every anger as "strength," saying plainly "I fell out with an actress and I'm furious" isn't an available expression. She can show the claws. She can't say what they're aimed at.

Asked whether she'd adapt The Siren, she says no: "I've already done that journey." It's the only sentence in the piece that doesn't need decoding. She didn't give up on screens. She did the math: the upside goes to other people, the cost stays with her.

What the math leaves out is that the person who called the film "the worst thing I ever lived through" on TikTok and the person singing "My Way" into a Zoom camera are the same woman. The profile puts them side by side without comment. It doesn't need to.

The villain in her new novel is named Roux—same sound, different spelling, as Stenberg's Rue in The Hunger Games. The profile notes this is "notable" and then moves on. That pause is louder than anything anyone says.

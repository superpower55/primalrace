战争发财，代价记在谁的账上War Profits, and Who Gets the Bill

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The New Republic ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
油价上涨是账单，利润上涨是选票，中间差额叫暴力。
Higher gas prices are the bill; higher profits are the votes. The gap between them is violence.

特朗普在佐治亚的集会上把话说得很清楚：“我们正在发一笔他妈的财。”他说幕僚劝他别提，因为“人们在加油时正受苦”，他说“我不在乎”。这不是失言，这是把结构暴力的账本当众念了一遍。

数字摆在那里：美国五十家最大的油气公司四月到九月赚了一千三百亿美元，去年同期是四百七十亿。普通家庭自战争开始以来光油钱就多花了一千一百美元，全国加起来多付一千四百七十亿（来源：The New Republic 引述《纽约时报》与布朗大学气候解决方案实验室）。差额被谁拿走了，被谁付了，报道自己写完了。

加尔通的公式在这里不需要解释，只需要代入：Violence = Potential − Actual。同样的油、同样的车、同样的路，家庭本可以不付这一千一百美元，差额就是暴力。它不是抽象的，它是从每个油箱里抽出来的，然后以“利润”的名义进到五十家公司的报表里。

这里动的是结构层，而支撑它的是文化层。战争叙事把“中东发生了什么”包装成国家大事，把油价包装成“暂时偏高”，把利润包装成“我们国家在赚钱”。特朗普那句“我们”，报道点得很准：他说的是 Exxon、Chevron、Valero，不是你。

“我们”这个词是第三章里最便宜的武器。它不花一分钱，就能让被抽走一千一百美元的人觉得自己也在分红。谁在共谋？政客在共谋，用战争叙事换选票；油气公司在共谋，用价格换利润；媒体在共谋，用“blowout profit”这种词把掠夺写成业绩。

报道没有写这五十家公司里有没有人拒绝这笔钱，没有写有没有股东在年会上提这件事，没有写国会里有没有人要求对战争期间油气利润征税。这些缺席本身就是材料：赢的那一方不需要解释，付钱的那一方没有被问。

特朗普说“我不在乎”。这句话的诚实程度超过他大部分发言。他承认了差额的存在，只是站在收钱的那一侧。真正的问题不是他说了什么，是这句话之后，还有多少人继续用“暂时”来形容自己油箱里的那一千一百美元。

Trump said it out loud at a rally in Georgia: "We're making a freaking fortune." His people told him not to mention it because "people are paying a little higher" at the pump. He said, "I don't give a damn." This wasn't a slip. It was the ledger of structural violence read aloud.

The numbers are right there: the fifty largest U.S. oil and gas companies made $130 billion from April to September, against $47 billion in the same six months last year. American households have spent $1,100 on fuel since the war began, $147 billion extra in total (source: The New Republic, citing The New York Times and Brown University's Climate Solutions Lab). Who took the difference and who paid it—the report already says it.

Galtung's formula doesn't need explaining here, only plugging in: Violence = Potential − Actual. Same oil, same cars, same roads. Households could have paid $0 of that $1,100. The difference is violence. It is not abstract. It is pumped out of every tank and entered as "profit" on fifty balance sheets.

What moves here is the structural layer, propped up by the cultural one. War narrative packages "what's happened in the Middle East" as statecraft, high prices as "temporarily high," and profits as "our country making money." The report nails the pronoun: when Trump says "we," he means Exxon, Chevron, Valero. Not you.

"We" is the cheapest weapon in Chapter 3. It costs nothing and lets the people down $1,100 feel like shareholders. Who is complicit? The politician trading war narrative for votes. The oil companies trading prices for profit. The media trading "blowout profit" for a headline—turning extraction into performance.

The report does not say whether any of the fifty companies refused the money, whether any shareholder raised it at an annual meeting, whether anyone in Congress moved to tax war-time oil profits. Those absences are the material: the winning side never has to explain, and the paying side is never asked.

"I don't give a damn" is more honest than most of what Trump says. He admits the gap exists. He just stands on the collecting side. The real question isn't what he said. It's how many people keep calling the $1,100 in their tank "temporary" after he said it.

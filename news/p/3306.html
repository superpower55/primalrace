骨架、牙签与‘猴人鱼’：博物馆里的殖民美学scamSkeletons, Toothpicks, and 'Monkey Mermaids': The Colonial Aesthetic Scam

哲学 文化层 · 结构层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-08-06 § 链接
将掠夺与屠杀包装成‘航海遗产’，是典型的文化暴力洗白。
Packaging plunder and slaughter as 'maritime heritage' is a textbook case of cultural violence whitewashing.

一个40英尺长的北极右鲸骨架，成了博物馆的 star attraction。孩子们在肋骨间爬行，像是在进行一场关于‘壮丽’的感官游戏。但这种叙事极其恶心：它精准地抹去了 whale oil 时代的血腥味，把一个被猎杀至近乎灭绝的物种，简化成一个巨大的、可供拍照的物理空间。这就是典型的 cultural violence——用‘壮观’掩盖‘屠杀’，让结构性的掠夺看起来像是一种自然的、值得怀念的‘遗产’。

更荒诞的是那些 scrimshaw 牙雕。船员在无聊的航行中，把鲸鱼牙齿刻成牙签或装饰品。这种对生物残骸的‘精巧’处理，本质上是对自然资源的极致物化。而那只‘猴人鱼’（monkey mermaid）——一个猴子和鱼拼接而成的畸形生物——则是整场展览中最诚实的隐喻：它揭示了殖民主义者如何通过扭曲、拼接和伪造，来制造他们想要的‘真实’。这种‘制造可能性的艺术’，在当年是用来欺骗好奇心的，在今天则被放在博物馆里作为一种‘奇趣’展出。

至于那个被提及的‘头巾革命者’ Lillian Bilocca，她的出现像是给这场男性中心叙事（masculine-centric narrative）打的一个补丁。博物馆在纪念她拯救了数千名船员的同时，轻描淡写地提到她被‘排挤和列入黑名单’。这种处理方式极其卑劣：它把一个女性对结构性暴力（structural violence）的抗争，变成了一个点缀在‘辉煌航海史’边的感人小插曲。在充满维多利亚时代‘男性雄风’（mercantile bombast）的建筑里，女性的抗争被博物馆化了，从而被消解了。

这次 2040 万英镑的‘重启’，本质上是一次审美上的 PR。它试图用‘哥本哈根风格’的文明外壳，掩盖一个基于贸易、榨取和殖民的血腥底色。当一个城市试图通过展示‘骨架’来寻找未来时，它其实是在共谋一种危险的认知：只要色彩足够淡雅，历史上的暴力就可以被转化为某种‘提升市民自豪感’的文化资本。

A 40-foot North Atlantic right whale skeleton is the star attraction. Children crawl through its ribs, treating it as a sensory playground of 'grandeur.' This narrative is repulsive: it meticulously erases the bloodstains of the whale oil era, reducing a species hunted to near-extinction to a mere physical space for photos. This is cultural violence—using 'spectacle' to mask 'slaughter,' making structural plunder appear as a natural, nostalgic 'heritage.'

Even more absurd are the scrimshaws. Sailors spent their boredom carving whale teeth into toothpicks or ornaments. This 'delicate' manipulation of biological remains is the ultimate objectification of nature. The 'monkey mermaid'—a hideous chimera of monkey and fish—is the most honest metaphor in the room: it reveals how colonizers manufactured 'truth' through distortion and forgery. This 'art of manufacturing possibilities' was once used to deceive the curious; today, it is displayed as a 'curiosity.'

Then there is Lillian Bilocca, the 'headscarf revolutionary.' Her inclusion feels like a patch on a masculine-centric narrative. The museum commemorates her for saving thousands of sailors while casually mentioning she was 'ostracised and blacklisted.' This is a vile curation: it turns a woman's struggle against structural violence into a touching footnote in a 'glorious maritime history.' Inside a building exuding Victorian 'mercantile bombast,' female resistance is museumified, and therefore, neutralized.

This £20.4m 'reboot' is essentially an aesthetic PR stunt. It attempts to use a 'Copenhagen vibe' to mask a bloody foundation of trade, extraction, and colonialism. When a city seeks its future by displaying skeletons, it is complicit in a dangerous cognition: that as long as the color palette is subtle enough, historical violence can be converted into cultural capital for 'civic pride.'

圣诞歌不圣诞，是门生意A Christmas Song That Isn't About Christmas

文娱 文化层 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要11月2日，Olivia Rodrigo 在 Instagram 宣布将于11月5日晚8点（东部时间）发行首支圣诞歌曲《Take Me Home (4 Christmas)》，并公开了戴红色圣诞帽的单曲封面。她今年6月发行第三张专辑，8月举办音乐节为女性慈善机构筹得2000万美元。
The newsOn Nov. 2, Olivia Rodrigo announced on Instagram that her first Christmas song, “Take Me Home (4 Christmas),” arrives Nov. 5 at 8 p.m. ET, with artwork showing her in a red Santa hat. She released her third album in June and in August raised $20 million for women's charities through her festival.
节日歌单是情感入口，也是定价权
Holiday playlists are an emotional entry point, and someone holds the pricing power.

一条圣诞单曲的发布，值得说的不是歌，是这门生意的入口在哪。Billboard 的稿子写得很老实：Rodrigo 把帽子扔进了 holiday song market，而这个市场每年由 Mariah Carey 的《All I Want for Christmas Is You》统治。一个市场被一首歌统治三十年，说明的不是这首歌多好，是节日情感被谁定价、按什么周期收租。

圣诞歌是第三章那类情感入口里最干净的一种。它不需要你相信什么，只需要你每年十二月准时回到同一个播放列表。浪漫爱叙事卖的是「The One」，圣诞歌卖的是「the same one」，同一首歌、同一个季节、同一笔版税。Rodrigo 发的是她「first-ever」的圣诞歌，这个 first 本身就是产品说明书：她来晚了，但她来了，因为这块地每年都会重新长一遍草。

真正有意思的是同一篇稿子里并列的另一组数字。8 月她的音乐节为女性慈善机构筹到 2000 万美元，10 月她拉上 Chappell Roan 做了首 remix 把歌推到 Hot 100 第三。一边是把女性议题变成可计算的募款额，一边是把一首歌变成榜单名次，两件事在同一具身体上并行不悖。这不是虚伪，这是当代流行明星的标准配置：进步姿态和商业动作共用一套发行日历。

所以我不打算说这歌不该发。我要说的是，当一个女艺人必须同时经营「为女性筹款」和「进入一个由另一首歌统治三十年的市场」两种表达时，她的真.最优解表达被压缩成了什么形状。封面是红帽子红唇，坐在圣诞树前的地板上，一个标准的、无害的、谁都能收下的节日形象。这个形象不冒犯任何人，也就意味着它不为任何人打仗。

报道没写的是这歌卖多少钱、版税怎么分、那 2000 万最终流向了哪些机构。这些空白比一首圣诞歌重要得多——钱从哪来、到哪去，才是表达被武器化之后真正落地的地方。节日歌单每年重置一次，收租的人不换。

The story here isn't the song. It's where the entry point sits. Billboard is refreshingly blunt: Rodrigo is throwing her hat into the holiday song market, a market dominated every year by Mariah Carey's “All I Want for Christmas Is You.” One song ruling a market for three decades tells you nothing about the song's quality and everything about who prices seasonal feeling, and on what schedule they collect.

Christmas music is the cleanest of the emotional entry points. It doesn't ask you to believe anything. It asks you to return to the same playlist every December. Romance narratives sell “The One”; Christmas songs sell “the same one” — same track, same season, same royalty. Rodrigo's is her “first-ever” Christmas song, and that first is the product spec: she's late, but she showed up, because this field regrows grass every year.

The more interesting thing is the other set of numbers in the same piece. In August her festival raised $20 million for women's charities; in October a remix with Chappell Roan pushed a song to No. 3 on the Hot 100. One turns women's issues into a countable fundraising figure, the other turns a track into a chart position, and both run on the same body without friction. That isn't hypocrisy. It's the standard configuration of the contemporary pop star: progressive posture and commercial move sharing one release calendar.

I'm not saying the song shouldn't exist. I'm saying that when a woman artist has to run “raising money for women” and “entering a market ruled for thirty years by someone else's song” as parallel expressions, look at what her real optimal expression got compressed into. The cover is a red hat, red lips, sitting on the floor by the tree — a standard, harmless holiday image anyone can accept. An image that offends no one is an image that fights for no one.

What the report doesn't say: what the song costs, how the royalties split, which organizations the $20 million actually reached. Those blanks matter more than a Christmas single. Where the money comes from and where it goes is where weaponized expression actually lands. The holiday playlist resets every year. The people collecting don't.

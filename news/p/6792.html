别让富豪上桌，也别让散户接盘Stop the IPOs, Keep the Casino

科技 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要美国演员兼播客主持人伊拉娜·格雷泽与专栏作家娜奥米·克莱因在《卫报》联名撰文，呼吁对亿万富翁征收财富税，并阻止 Anthropic、OpenAI 等科技公司上市。文章称过去两年美国亿万富翁财富增长 40%，近三千名全球富豪的财富合计相当于全球年产出的五分之一，并点名马斯克、埃里森、布林的政治捐款与并购行为。
The newsComedian Ilana Glazer and columnist Naomi Klein argue in the Guardian for wealth taxes on billionaires and for blocking tech IPOs, naming Anthropic and OpenAI. They write that billionaires' wealth rose 40% in two years and that nearly 3,000 billionaires together hold one-fifth of world output, citing Musk, Ellison and Brin.
文章替普通人喊停 IPO，却没问谁被允许坐上赌桌。
The piece names the risk correctly, then hands the bill to the wrong people.

这篇稿子把风险讲对了，把风险讲给谁听却讲反了。两位作者说，AI 公司一旦上市，创始人升到新的财富层，普通人被塞进退休账户里的股票跟着一起承压，泡沫破了大家接盘。这条链是真的：SpaceX 今年上市，马斯克一夜之间成了万亿富翁，靠的是工人的养老金；Anthropic 和 OpenAI 都公开承认背着巨额债务，安全漏洞也刚被曝出。上市就是把一家公司的风险从股东桌上搬到所有人的账户里，这个机制值得每一篇财经报道拆一遍。

但文章给出的解法是「阻止它们上市」。这一句等于承认：赌场照开，只是这几位新赌客不许进场。已经上市的十大公司全是科技公司，整个国家的经济压在单一行业上——这是结构问题，不是这一批公司的品行问题。把 IPO 的门关上，资本会走私募、走 SPV、走你叫不出名字的跨境架构，风险照样传导到普通人的养老金里，只是更难看见。真正该被堵住的入口，是「退休账户默认替你买入投机资产」这件事，而不是某几家公司敲钟的日期。文章一个字没提这个入口。

更值得看的是谁在说话、说给谁听。作者之一是单口喜剧演员，另一位是气候正义教授，两人用「我们」来指美国普通投资者。可这个「我们」里，谁有资格决定自己的钱投去哪里？报道写得很清楚：普通美国人的风险是「未经同意」被承担的。既然未经同意是问题的核心，那诉求就不该是「请监管替我们选」，而该是「把选择权还给我们」。前者是把家长制包装成保护，后者才是把差额真正补上。保护性话语是控制最常见的入口，这条在养老金上和在别的地方一样成立。

还有一层没人问：这些公司的钱是从哪来的。文章提到马斯克用「他巨额收益的一小部分」资助共和党中期选举，提到埃里森支持派拉蒙与华纳兄弟的合并、正在摧毁电影业和从业者的生计，提到布林花一亿美元反对加州的一次性财富税。这些都是共谋的现场——买方出钱，政客出售入口，从业者付代价。文章把这些写出来了，是它的功劳。但它接着把解决方案落在「征税」和「拦 IPO」上，等于只处理了收益端，没碰权力端：谁有权把一个行业的风险定价成公共风险，谁就有权决定谁来承担。

财富税本身也不是没用。加州和纽约若真能通过并执行，那是结构层的一小块进展。但立法不等于落地：没有强制执行的机制和数据反馈，「通过法案」只是结构暴力的公关版本。文章呼吁「找到法律和立法的途径」，却没写任何一条现成的途径长什么样，也没写这些途径过去被谁堵过。这不是疏忽，是这类联署评论的结构性特征——它要的是签名，不是施工图。

最刺的一句留到最后。文章说，衰退不分投资者与否，会平等地碾过所有人，唯一的例外是亿万富翁，他们有足够的资源从废墟里走出来。这句话是对的，但它同时说明了另一件事：在一个允许这种例外的系统里，「阻止下一批人变成亿万富翁」和「不让这一批人继续当亿万富翁」是两件事。前者是排队管理，后者才是重新分配。文章两件都喊了，力气全花在第一件上。

The essay gets the mechanism right and the audience backwards. When a company goes public, founders climb to a new stratum of wealth and ordinary people end up holding stock they never chose to buy, inside retirement accounts they can't opt out of. That chain is real: SpaceX listed this year and made Musk an instant trillionaire on the backs of workers' pensions; Anthropic and OpenAI both carry enormous acknowledged debt and recently exposed security flaws. An IPO is a machine for moving a company's risk from the shareholders' table onto everyone's account. That deserves to be dissected in every business section.

But the fix on offer is to block these listings. That concedes the casino stays open, just not for these new players. The ten most valuable public companies are already tech, which means the whole economy leans on one industry with a documented history of bubbles. That is a structural fact, not a character flaw of this batch of firms. Close the IPO door and capital walks through private rounds, shell entities, and cross-border structures you can't name, and the risk still lands in the same pension funds, only less visibly. The entrance worth sealing is the one where retirement accounts buy speculative assets by default, not the date some founders ring a bell. The essay doesn't mention that entrance at all.

Then look at who is speaking and to whom. A comedian and a climate justice professor write as «we,» meaning ordinary American investors. But who in that «we» gets to decide where the money goes? The piece says it plainly: regular Americans bear the risk without consent. If consent is the wound, the demand should be to return the choice, not to have a regulator choose for us. The first is paternalism dressed as protection; the second actually closes the gap. Protective language is the oldest entrance for control, and it works the same on pensions as anywhere else.

One question goes unasked: where the money comes from. Musk is bankrolling Republican midterm campaigns with a sliver of his gains; Ellison backed the Paramount-Warner merger that is gutting film work; Brin spent $100m fighting a one-time California wealth tax. Those are the scenes of collusion, buyers paying, politicians selling access, workers paying the price. Naming them is the essay's real contribution. But then it lands its solution on taxes and blocking IPOs, which touches the payout and leaves the power alone: whoever gets to price an industry's risk as public risk gets to decide who carries it.

A wealth tax isn't nothing. If California and New York pass one and actually enforce it, that is a sliver of progress at the structural layer. But legislation is not implementation: a bill with no enforcement mechanism and no data feedback is the PR edition of structural violence. The essay calls for «legal and legislative avenues» without describing a single one, or who blocked them before. That isn't an oversight; it's the genre. It wants signatures, not blueprints.

The sharpest line is buried near the end: recessions level lives indiscriminately, and the only exception is the billionaires, who can walk away from the wreckage. True. And it quietly proves that in a system permitting that exception, stopping the next batch from becoming billionaires and stopping this batch from remaining billionaires are two different projects. The essay shouts both and spends all its force on the first.

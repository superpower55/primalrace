股市不投票，它只定价The Market Doesn't Vote, It Prices

其他 文化层 · 结构层 CNBC ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要CNBC 梳理 1980 至 2024 年选举后的标普 500 表现：中期选举后一年平均上涨 13.7%，1982 至 2022 年的 11 次中期选举后全部为正；政府分裂时一年平均涨 15%，单一政党掌控时平均涨 11.5%。报道同时指出，美联储政策、企业盈利周期与 AI 行情对市场的影响大于选举本身。
The newsCNBC reviewed S&P 500 returns after elections from 1980 to 2024: the index gained an average of 13.7% in the year after midterms, and was positive after all 11 midterms from 1982 to 2022. Divided government averaged a 15% one-year gain versus 11.5% under single-party control. The report also notes the Fed, earnings cycles and AI matter more than elections.
把选举换算成收益率，是把政治变成天气
Turning an election into a rate of return turns politics into weather.

这篇报道从头到尾没有出现一个人。没有候选人，没有选民，没有哪条政策让谁多付了钱、谁少拿了药。只有标普 500 的百分比，和一位策略师说「市场有点焦虑」。焦虑的主语是谁？是那些持有股票的人。报道替他们把选举翻译成一条收益率曲线，然后告诉他们：别怕，历史上 91% 的情况是涨的。

这不是财经分析，这是一次认知入口的占领。选举在报道里的位置，被降格成和财报季、美联储议息会议并列的一个变量——一个会过去、会消化、会在一年后变成 13.7% 的事件。读者读完记住的不是「周二有人要投票决定谁被统治」，而是「周二之后可以买」。

最有意思的是「市场偏好分裂政府」这一段。报道给出的理由是策略师的原话：一党独大意味着「太多支出、太多权力」。翻译一下：市场喜欢的不是民主，是僵局。一个什么都做不成的政府，对资产价格最友好。这句话被平静地写进报道，像一条自然规律。它当然是自然规律——对持有资产的人而言。对等着某项立法落地的人来说，僵局就是暴力，是结构层那台机器空转、把本可达到的状态一直悬着。

报道自己其实交出了底牌。它说 2000 年选举后一年标普跌了 22.1%，因为互联网泡沫；2020 年后一年涨了 38.3%，因为疫情抛售后的反弹。也就是说，选举这个变量在真正的力量面前几乎不成立。可它还是用整篇文章的篇幅，把选举包装成一个可预测的、有历史规律的市场事件。为什么？因为「选举影响股市」这个故事有人看，「美联储和盈利周期才重要」这个故事没人点。

这里有一层共谋，而且不分党派。写的人需要点击，读的人需要安慰，接受采访的策略师需要曝光，三方在「历史数据表明」这个句式上达成一致。没有人撒谎，每个数字都是真的，但拼起来的效果是：把一次权力分配，说成一次季节性波动。报道最后甚至自己承认了——「Seasonality isn't destiny」——然后接着卖 seasonal rally。

真正的代价不在报道里。它没写谁在这套叙事里被定价、谁被排除在外。一个把政治换算成收益率的世界，默认读者是有仓位的人；没有仓位的人在这篇文章里不存在，就像他们在「市场焦虑」这个词里从来不存在一样。

1982 到 2022 年，11 次中期选举后一年全部上涨。这个 100% 的纪录很漂亮，漂亮到值得问一句：这 40 年里，工资涨了多少？房租涨了多少？那些没有股票账户的人，在这 11 次全胜里赢到了什么？

Not a single person appears in this article. No candidates, no voters, no policy that made someone pay more or get less medicine. Only S&P 500 percentages, and a strategist saying the market is "anxious." Anxious for whom? For whoever holds the stocks. The piece translates the election into a return curve for them and says: don't worry, 91% of the time it goes up.

This isn't financial analysis. It's a takeover of a cognitive entry point. The election is demoted to one variable alongside earnings season and the Fed — an event that passes, gets digested, and becomes 13.7% a year later. What the reader remembers isn't "on Tuesday people vote on who governs them." It's "after Tuesday, you can buy."

The best part is the section on divided government. The reason given is the strategist's own words: one-party control means "too much spending and too much power." Translated: the market doesn't like democracy, it likes gridlock. A government that can't do anything is friendliest to asset prices. This is written as calmly as a law of nature. And it is one — for people who hold assets. For people waiting on a bill to pass, gridlock is violence: the structural machine idling, holding the actual below the possible.

The article hands over its own cards. It says the S&P fell 22.1% in the year after the 2000 election because of the dotcom bust, and rose 38.3% after 2020 on the COVID rebound. The election variable barely survives contact with real forces. Yet the whole piece packages it as a predictable, historically regular market event. Why? Because "elections move stocks" gets clicks and "the Fed and earnings matter more" doesn't.

There's a collusion here, and it's bipartisan. The writer needs clicks, the reader needs comfort, the strategist needs exposure — all three agree on the phrase "history shows." Nobody lies. Every number is real. The combined effect is to turn a distribution of power into a seasonal fluctuation. The piece even admits it — "Seasonality isn't destiny" — then goes right on selling the seasonal rally.

The real cost is absent. The article never says who gets priced by this narrative and who gets left out. A world that converts politics into returns assumes a reader with a position. People without one don't exist in this piece, just as they never exist inside the phrase "market anxiety."

Eleven midterms, 1982 through 2022, all up a year later. A perfect record, clean enough to invite one question: over those forty years, how much did wages rise? How much did rent? What did the people without brokerage accounts win in those eleven straight victories?

相亲专栏里的温柔陷阱The Sweet Trap in the Blind Date Column

文娱 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
被夸上天堂，是定价权在微笑
Being sent to heaven is pricing power smiling at you

Nelson 说 Louise 要是天堂存在一定去得了，Louise 说这可能是她听过最好的话。一个陌生人用一句神学恭维，就把对方放进了被评价的位置：他负责审判，她负责被审判，然后因为判决有利而感激。

这是相亲专栏里最标准的浪漫爱叙事样本。它把一次两小时的晚餐包装成命运的试探，把“他是个绅士、她笑得很响”这种最低限度的礼貌，写成值得刊登的化学反应。真正的信息量在别处：两人都给了高分，都愿意再见，但 Louise 说“作为朋友吧，奥斯卡季要到了”。她给这段关系定了一个更低的价，Nelson 给了 9 分。

注意那个被省略的部分。整篇文章里，两个人谈的是新加坡、动物园志愿者、话剧、创业公司、奥斯卡提名影片。没有一句涉及他们各自要在这段关系里付出什么、放弃什么。这恰恰是浪漫爱作为武器的运作方式：它让“在一起”本身成为目的，把之后所有的成本核算推迟到无法回头的时候。

Louise 那句“作为朋友”是这场博弈里唯一清醒的表达。她没有把一次愉快的晚餐兑换成一张通往婚姻的期票，也没有因为对方给了 9 分就回赠一个 9 分。8 分是给餐厅的，不是给人的——她自己说得很清楚。

但专栏不会写这个。专栏要的是“他们会不会再见面”的悬念，是“他是不是个好男人”的评分，是把两个陌生人的礼貌互动剪成一集连续剧。读者看完记住的是那句天堂的恭维，而不是谁在这顿饭里被悄悄定了价。

“如果天堂是真的，你肯定去得了”——这句话之所以好听，是因为它把评判权交给了说的人。下一次有人对你说这句话，先问他：天堂的门票，你打算怎么分摊？

Nelson told Louise that if heaven is real, she'd definitely be going there. Louise said it might be the nicest thing anyone's ever said to her. A stranger used a theological compliment to put her in the position of the judged: he does the sentencing, she receives it, and feels grateful because the verdict was favorable.

This is the standard romantic-love narrative sample from a blind date column. It packages a two-hour dinner as a fateful encounter, and turns basic politeness—he's a gentleman, she laughs loudly—into publishable chemistry. The real information is elsewhere: both give high marks, both would meet again, but Louise says "as friends, maybe at the cinema as Oscar season is approaching." She sets a lower price on the relationship; Nelson gives it a 9.

Note what's omitted. They talk about Singapore, zoo volunteering, theatre, a startup, Oscar-nominated films. Not one word about what each would have to give up or sacrifice in a relationship. That's exactly how romantic love works as a weapon: it makes "being together" the goal itself, deferring all cost accounting until it's too late to walk away.

Louise's "as friends" is the only clear-eyed expression in this game. She doesn't convert a pleasant dinner into a promissory note toward marriage, and she doesn't return a 9 just because she got one. The 8 is for the restaurant, not the person—she says so herself.

But the column won't write that. The column wants the suspense of "will they meet again," the rating of "is he a good man," the serialization of two strangers' polite exchange. Readers remember the heaven compliment, not who got quietly priced over dinner.

"If heaven is real, you'll definitely be going there"—it sounds good because it hands the power of judgment to the speaker. Next time someone says it to you, ask first: how do you plan to split the cost of heaven's ticket?

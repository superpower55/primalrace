性爱日记里，房租才是那个共谋者In Sex Diaries, the Lease Is the Co-conspirator

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 The Cut ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
卧室门的另一边，站着的从来不是浪漫，是租约。
On the other side of the bedroom door stands not romance, but a lease.

The Cut 的 Sex Diaries 做了快二十年，它卖的一直是同一个幻觉：性是可以被写下来的、被匿名展示的、被当作一个人私生活切片来消费的东西。但这一期把室友拉进来之后，幻觉破了——原来这些日记里真正在博弈的从来不是性，是住的地方。

看那个布鲁克林的 24 岁平面设计师。她的日记里最惊心动魄的一段不是和前任的好友上床，是室友发来一句“我们得重新谈谈房租”，她立刻开始发慌，因为这套房子是室友父母租给她们的，便宜，一旦涨价她就得搬回新泽西的父母家。她写道：“我能一个人住，唯一的原因就是这个房租。”这句话才是整篇的核心。性在这里是消遣，房租才是命。

于是所有其他故事都被重新排列了。那个和未来室友在酒吧外接吻、坚持叫了另一辆 Uber 回家的女学生，她说“可以亲，但我没那么乱到去操我的未来室友”——她守的不是道德，是不想在自己付了一半租金的房间里制造一个无法退出的局面。那个在凌晨 3:47 终于让步、和室友 Carson 上床的女人，她一开始拒绝的理由说得清清楚楚：我们住在一起。Carson 用“我很随性、你别想太多”来拆掉这道防线，这是一种很典型的表达武器化——把对方的顾虑重新定义成对方想多了，然后得手。结果是他硬不起来，红着脸关门。这个结局当然可以当笑话看，但笑话底下是一件事：他推销的那套“随性”，他自己也兑现不了。

再往下一层，是那个室友不停往家里带男人的女人。2020 年，封控期间，她的室友把一个和她有过感情史的男人 Trey 叫到家里来，Trey 早上站在厨房煮咖啡，跟她说“早上好”，好像什么都没发生。室友 Katy 连招呼都没打。这不是性自由，这是有人单方面决定把共用的空间变成自己的场地，成本由另一个人承担。还有那个墙壁只有半英寸厚、室友的房间“严格来说是我的衣柜”的学生，她每晚被吵醒，听见隔壁的性爱声，然后想起自己有多孤单。她付了房租，却买不到一个能睡觉的夜晚。

这些日记被编辑包装成“合租的香艳故事”，配上一句“如果你不是一个人住，谨慎一点总没坏处”。这句话把结构问题翻译成了个人修养。真正的结构是：在一个住房被金融化、年轻人只能靠合租和父母的关系房才能勉强独居的城市里，卧室门的两边是不对等的——谁掌握租约、谁的父母是房东、谁的房间其实是衣柜，这些决定了谁能在半夜制造噪音、谁只能听着。性只是这套不平等被看见时最显眼的表面。

Sex Diaries 这个栏目本身也在这个共谋里。它把匿名者的私生活切成七天一段的消费品，读者得到的是窥视的快感，平台得到的是流量，而被写进去的人得到的是……一次被看见的机会？不，她们得到的是被编辑挑选、被标题概括、被一句“让我们猜猜后来怎么样了”打发掉。那个被房租吓得半死的设计师，她的恐惧被放在一篇“最精彩的室友性爱日记”合集里，和硬不起来的 Carson 并列。

所以这一期真正诚实的标题应该是：几个年轻人如何在住房危机里用身体互相试探，而房东和租约从头到尾没有露面。房子是谁的，租金怎么定，谁能搬进来谁必须搬走——这些没写进日记的部分，才是决定这些故事怎么结束的东西。

The Cut's Sex Diaries has run for nearly two decades selling the same illusion: that sex can be written down, anonymized, and consumed as a slice of someone's private life. Bringing roommates into the mix breaks that illusion. What was always being negotiated in these diaries was never sex. It was a place to live.

Take the 24-year-old graphic designer in Brooklyn. The most alarming passage in her diary isn't sleeping with her ex's best friend. It's her roommate texting that they need to rethink the rent. She panics immediately, because the apartment belongs to her roommate's parents, it's cheap, and if the price goes up she has to move back in with her parents in New Jersey. She writes: the only reason she can live on her own is this rent situation. That line is the whole piece. Sex is the pastime. Rent is the lifeline.

Once you see that, every other story rearranges itself. The college student who makes out with her future roommate outside a bar and deliberately takes a separate Uber home says she can kiss but isn't messy enough to fuck her future roommate. She isn't protecting a moral code. She's refusing to manufacture a situation she can't exit inside a room she pays half for. The woman who at 3:47 a.m. finally caves and sleeps with her roommate Carson had already stated her reason for saying no: they live together. Carson dismantles that line by insisting he's very casual, that she's making it a bigger deal than it is. That's expression weaponized in its most ordinary form: redefine the other person's hesitation as overthinking, then proceed. The result is that he can't get hard, and closes the door with red cheeks. You can laugh at that ending. Underneath the laugh is this: the casualness he sold, he couldn't deliver either.

One layer down is the woman whose roommate keeps bringing men home. In 2020, mid-lockdown, the roommate invites over Trey, a man the diarist has a romantic history with. Trey stands in the kitchen in the morning making coffee, says good morning as if nothing is wrong. The roommate Katy never warned her. This isn't sexual freedom. It's one person unilaterally converting shared space into her own venue, with the cost billed to someone else. Then there's the student whose walls are half an inch thick and whose roommate's room is technically her closet. She's woken most nights by the sex next door and lies there thinking about how single and alone she is. She pays rent and still can't buy a night's sleep.

The editors package these as steamy cohabitation tales, capped with the line that if you don't live alone, it never hurts to be discreet. That sentence translates a structural problem into personal etiquette. The actual structure is this: in a city where housing is financialized and young people can only barely live alone through sublets and family connections, the two sides of a bedroom door are unequal. Who holds the lease, whose parents are the landlord, whose room is actually a closet—these decide who gets to make noise at midnight and who only gets to listen. Sex is just the most visible surface when that inequality shows through.

The Sex Diaries column is complicit in this too. It slices anonymous private lives into seven-day consumables. Readers get voyeuristic pleasure, the platform gets traffic, and the people written into it get... a chance to be seen? No. They get selected by an editor, summarized by a headline, and dismissed with a let's-guess-how-that-ended. The designer terrified of a rent hike has her fear placed in a roundup of the best roommate sex diaries, side by side with Carson's soft dick.

The honest title for this installment would be: how a few young people probe each other's bodies inside a housing crisis, while the landlord and the lease never appear. Who owns the building, how rent is set, who can move in and who has to move out—the parts left out of the diaries are what actually determine how these stories end.

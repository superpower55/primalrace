最高法院在给施暴者发免罪符The Court Is Handing Out Immunity

其他 结构层 · 元暴力 Vox ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
把追责的门一扇扇焊死，暴力才成了制度
Weld shut every door to accountability, and violence becomes the system.

Vox 这篇报道的标题是“最高法院可能让你没法起诉联邦官员”。读下来你会发现，它讨论的不是“可能”，是“已经基本完成，现在收尾”。

Bivens 案（1971）确立的逻辑很简单：联邦官员以国家的名义违法，伤害能力远大于一个普通人，所以宪法必须给受害者留一条救济通道。报道里引用的原话是“以美国的名义”行事的人“拥有大得多的伤害能力”。这条通道现在被拆得只剩一个门框。法院近年把 Bivens 诉讼称为“不受待见的司法活动”，说如果 Bivens 今天才判，“我们未必会得出同样的结果”，还指示下级法官只要找到“任何理性的理由（哪怕只有一个）”就驳回。11 月 9 日要审的 Nielsen v. Watanabe，被告方直接请法院把 Bivens 整个推翻。

这就是 structural violence 的标准做法：不是让某一次施暴变得合法，是让施暴之后没有人需要解释。加尔通的公式是 Violence = Potential − Actual，这里被削掉的“Actual”是追责本身。一个被联邦官员打伤的人本来可以达到的状态是：上法庭、举证、让对方赔、让制度留下记录。现在这个状态被制度性地取消了。差额就是暴力，而且是不见血的那种。

案子的事实值得单独看一眼。Watanabe 在联邦监狱里被同监犯人殴打，为“严重的背痛”求医，请求去医院被拒；据他的陈述，几个月里只拿到“间歇性的、短期的止痛治疗，常常是非处方药”。七个月后终于拍了 X 光，尾骨骨折，“骨片已经移入周围软组织”。他至今“仍然处在巨大的疼痛中”。这是第八修正案的问题——监狱不提供医疗，在押的人没有别的地方可去。下级法院说他的诉求“在所有有意义的方面与 Carlson 案相同”，而 Carlson 是 1980 年判的、至今有效的先例。

报道里最诚实的一句话是：这些区分“相当次要”，但既然法官被要求找“任何理性的理由”驳回，这些区分“几乎肯定就够了”。翻译一下：法律推理在这里不是用来判断对错的，是用来给已经定好的结果配一个说法。这就是第四章说的元暴力——解释权被垄断之后，“客观”“理性”“依法”都成了伪装。谁掌握解释权，谁就能决定什么叫“理性的理由”。

再看谁在共谋、回报是什么。报道写得很清楚：最高法院有六名共和党任命的大法官，这六个人“已经完全钻进了对 Bivens 的反对里”。推翻 Bivens 的实际收益是什么？报道说，大概只是“把法官从翻查 Bivens 案卷、找理由驳回原告的琐碎工作中解救出来”。为了让法官少干点活，把普通人告联邦官员的最后一条路焊死。这笔账谁付、谁收，一目了然。

还有一层报道没写、但读者该自己补上：联邦执法机构正在城市里做报道所说的“准军事占领”，而追责通道同时在关闭。这两件事不是巧合，是同一件事的两面——先扩大可以施暴的范围，再取消施暴之后的后果。ICE 探员开枪，法院站在探员那边，这句副标题不是讽刺，是预告。

报道没有写 Watanabe 的律师是谁，没有写有没有民权组织提交法庭之友意见，也没有写国会那边有没有人在推动立法补救。这些缺席本身就是材料：真正能改变差额的动作，在这篇报道里一个都没出现。

差额里还没被削掉的部分是：Carlson 还在，Watanabe 的案子还在下级法院赢过一次，11 月 9 日之前还有时间。但一个连“任何理性的理由”都能当驳回依据的法院，你指望它在最后关头手软？

Vox's headline says the Supreme Court "could" make it impossible to sue federal officers. Read the piece and it's not "could." The demolition is basically done; this is the cleanup.

Bivens (1971) rested on a simple logic: a federal officer who acts unlawfully "in the name of the United States possesses a far greater capacity for harm" than a private trespasser, so the Constitution has to leave victims a way in. That way in is now a door frame with no door. The Court has called Bivens suits a "disfavored judicial activity," said it is "doubtful" it would reach the same result "if Bivens were decided today," and told lower judges to reject such claims if there is "any rational reason (even one)" to do so. On November 9 it hears Nielsen v. Watanabe, where the defendants ask the Court to overrule Bivens outright.

This is structural violence in its standard form: not making one act of brutality legal, but making it so that after the brutality no one has to explain anything. Galtung's formula is Violence = Potential − Actual. What gets subtracted here is accountability itself. The state a beaten man could have reached was: court, evidence, damages, a record. That state is being cancelled by design. The gap is the violence, and it doesn't bleed.

The facts of the case deserve a separate look. Watanabe was assaulted by fellow inmates in federal prison and sought care for "severe back pain." He asked to be treated in a hospital and was denied. According to his brief, for months he "received only intermittent and short-term treatment for pain, often over-the-counter medication." After seven months he finally got an X-ray: fractured tailbone, "bone chips had migrated into the surrounding soft tissue." He "remains in tremendous pain today." This is an Eighth Amendment problem — when a prison won't provide care, the incarcerated have nowhere else to go. The appeals court found his claim "identical to Carlson in all meaningful respects," and Carlson (1980) is still good law.

The most honest line in the piece: the distinctions are "fairly minor," but since judges are told to find "any rational reason" to deny, those distinctions are "almost certainly enough." Translation: legal reasoning here isn't for deciding right from wrong; it's for dressing a result that's already been decided. That is meta-violence — once the power to interpret is monopolized, "objective," "rational," and "according to law" are just costumes. Whoever holds interpretation decides what counts as a "rational reason."

Then ask who is conspiring, and what they get. The piece is explicit: six Republican-appointed justices, and those six are "fully dug into their opposition to Bivens." What does overruling actually buy? By the article's own account, roughly this: it rescues judges from "the menial task of hunting through legal records in Bivens cases, searching for a reason to rule against the plaintiff." To spare judges some paperwork, the last path for suing a federal officer gets welded shut. Who pays, who collects — plain enough.

One layer the piece leaves for the reader: federal agents are conducting what the article calls a "paramilitary occupation" of cities while the accountability channel closes. These aren't two stories. Expand the range of permitted violence, then cancel the consequences of violence. "Have you been shot by an ICE agent? The Supreme Court has the agent's back" isn't a joke. It's a schedule.

The article doesn't say who Watanabe's lawyers are, whether civil rights groups filed amicus briefs, or whether anyone in Congress is pushing a legislative fix. Those absences are the material: none of the moves that could actually shrink the gap appear in this story.

What's left of the gap: Carlson still stands, Watanabe won below, and there is time before November 9. But a Court that treats "any rational reason" as grounds for dismissal — you expect it to go soft at the last minute?

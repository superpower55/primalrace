州警陪他看房，纳税人买单State Troopers, Private Trips, Public Bill

其他 结构层 · 文化层 ProPublica ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要得克萨斯州总检察长肯·帕克斯顿自2015年上任以来，在州警安保陪同下出行近1000次，安保费用达330万美元。ProPublica与《得州论坛报》审查公开记录后发现，其中仅138次、约55.3万美元能与州公务挂钩，比例约六分之一。2023年因弹劾被停职期间他仍出行至少17次，包括犹他州帕克城、科罗拉多州韦尔等度假地。其办公室称他因打击贩毒集团和暴力罪犯面临安全威胁，未回应具体质询。
The newsSince taking office in 2015, Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton has taken nearly 1,000 trips with a state-funded security detail costing $3.3 million. A ProPublica and Texas Tribune review of public records could tie only 138 of those trips, about $553,000 in security costs, to state business — roughly one in six. During his 2023 impeachment suspension he took at least 17 trips, including to resort towns Park City and Vail. His office cited security threats from cartels and violent criminals and did not answer detailed questions.
安保是公器，行程是私产，账单是公共的。
Security is public. Itinerary is private. The bill is everyone's.

帕克斯顿的安保账单不是丑闻的全部，丑闻是这套机制本身：一个民选官员可以合法地把公权力变成私人生活的配套服务。

数字先摆出来。近1000次带州警的出行，330万美元。能挂上州公务的，138次，55.3万。剩下的八百多次，记录里找不到目的。得州法律允许公共安全部为总检察长提供安保，但不强制他使用——事实上他自己至少有二十几次因公出差时没用。这说明安保不是他职务的必需品，是他可以随时取用的特权。

更精确地说，这套安排的结构是：风险由他的职位叙事生产，成本由纳税人承担，收益归他个人。他办公室的声明说，威胁来自他打击贩毒集团和暴力罪犯的“勇敢工作”。这句话把安保从一项行政开支改写成了英雄叙事的附属品。但英雄叙事解释不了为什么安保记录里夏威夷、犹他、佛罗里达出现得比公务还勤。

时间线值得看一眼。2021年6月他去毛伊岛参加总检察长会议，安保记录写的是拉海纳；四个月后，家族信托以41万美元在那买下一块海景地，如今估值120万。犹他州去了19次，两次有公务登记，家族信托在伊登买地之前他去了三次。2023年8月停职期间，他去了圣乔治，附近是黑沙漠度假村——2025年买一套公寓，今年2月再买三套，贷款190万。佛罗里达去了53次，两次有公务登记，期间他在奥卡拉和达尼丁买了三处房产。这些行程和交易之间的因果关系报道没有断定，但记录摆在那里，谁都能读。

这不是“个人即政治”里那种私人领域的权力斗争被曝光，恰恰相反：这是私人领域的消费被系统性地伪装成公共领域的必要开支。加尔通的暴力三角在这里是结构层和文化层同时运转——结构层是那条允许但不强制、不要求报销、不要求说明目的的法律；文化层是“总检察长面临安全威胁”这套不容置疑的叙事，它让任何追问都显得不关心官员安全。两层咬合，差额就是那八百多次没有目的的出行，和纳税人替它们付的钱。

共谋者不止他一个。公共安全部拒绝评论安保安排，说这是惯例；州法律没有报销机制；报道确认帕克斯顿本人和竞选团队从未向州里补过一分钱。前全国总检察长协会执行主任托特把问题问得很准：合法的安保职能，是不是已经变成纳税人补贴。

还有一个细节别放过。被弹劾停职、被禁止处理任何公务的那三个月，他出行至少17次。一个被法律剥夺了公务身份的人，仍然带着公务身份的安保去度假。这说明这套安排跟“公务”根本绑不住，它绑的是职位带来的身份，而身份不因停职而暂停。

报道里没有出现的声音也值得记一笔：那些为这笔账单付钱的得州纳税人，没有一个被问到他们是否愿意为总检察长的看房之旅买单。这不是疏忽，这是这套机制能运转的前提。

The scandal isn't the security bill. The scandal is the mechanism: an elected official can lawfully convert public power into a concierge service for his private life.

Start with the numbers. Nearly 1,000 trips with a state police detail, $3.3 million. Trips tied to state business: 138, costing $553,000. The remaining 800-plus have no stated purpose in the records. Texas law lets the Department of Public Safety provide security for the attorney general but does not require him to use it — and he himself skipped the detail on at least two dozen official trips. The detail is not a necessity of the office. It is an option he exercises at will.

The structure is precise: risk is produced by his own job narrative, cost is carried by taxpayers, benefit accrues to him. His office's statement says the threats stem from his "courageous work" fighting cartels and violent criminals. That sentence rewrites an administrative expense as a decoration of heroism. Heroism does not explain why Hawaii, Utah and Florida appear in the security logs more often than state business does.

Look at the timeline. In June 2021 he attended an attorneys general conference in Maui; the detail logged Lahaina. Four months later his family trust bought an oceanview lot there for $410,000, now assessed at $1.2 million. He went to the Salt Lake City area 19 times, twice for listed business; three visits came in the months before the trust bought land in Eden. During his August 2023 suspension he visited St. George, near the Black Desert Resort — one condo bought in 2025, three more this February, $1.9 million in mortgages. Florida: 53 trips, two with documented business, and homes bought in Ocala and Dunedin along the way. The reporting does not claim causation between the trips and the purchases. The records are there for anyone to read.

This is not the personal-is-political exposure of private power struggles. It is the reverse: private consumption systematically dressed as public necessity. Galtung's triangle runs on two layers at once — the structural layer of a law that permits but never requires, never reimburses, never demands a purpose; and the cultural layer of a security narrative that makes any question look like indifference to an official's safety. The two layers mesh, and the gap between them is 800 trips with no purpose and a bill someone else pays.

The co-conspirators are not only him. DPS declines to comment on security arrangements as a matter of practice. The law has no reimbursement mechanism. The reporting confirms neither Paxton nor his campaign has ever repaid the state a cent. Chris Toth, former head of the national attorneys general association, asks the right question: has a legitimate security function become a taxpayer-funded subsidy?

One detail deserves its own paragraph. During the three months he was suspended and barred from conducting any official business, he took at least 17 trips. A man legally stripped of his official role still traveled with the security of that role. The arrangement was never bound to state business. It is bound to the status of the office, and status does not pause for an impeachment trial.

The missing voice matters too: not one Texas taxpayer was asked whether they wanted to fund the attorney general's property scouting. That silence is not an oversight. It is the condition under which the mechanism runs.

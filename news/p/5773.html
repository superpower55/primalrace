球星的定价权归谁Who Gets to Price a Star

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
工会赢了条款，联盟仍握着尺子
The union won the clause. The league still holds the ruler.

NWSL 和球员工会和解了，争议条款被删掉，这算是好事——但只在很窄的一层上。原来的 HIP 规则要求球员满足八类标准之一才能拿到工资帽之外的那 125.7 万美元：SportsPro 最具市场价值运动员前 150、ESPN 前 50、卫报前 100、国家队出场时间、金球奖 30 人名单、联盟 MVP 和最佳阵容。工会说这违反联邦劳动法，联盟认了，从此任何球员都可以拿这笔钱。

问题在于，这八条标准里没有一条是球员自己定的。它们全部由媒体、奖项和联盟自己生产：谁上榜单，谁被写，谁被看见。HIP 规则原本的设计意图报道写得很清楚——防止 Trinity Rodman 漂过大西洋去 Michele Kang 的伦敦城雌狮，而不是留在 Michele Kang 的华盛顿精神。同一个老板，两支球队，一条规则用来决定她该穿哪件球衣。这不是保护球员，这是给资产上锁。

所以真正被修改的不是“谁值钱”，而是“谁来宣布谁值钱”。以前是榜单宣布，现在是俱乐部宣布。俱乐部仍然是那个把球员当作可定价资产的一方，只是尺子从媒体手里交回了球队办公室。报道说这让球队可以自主决定谁值得，也让囊中羞涩的球队理论上能约束阵容已经堆满的豪门——理论上是。实际上，一支球队愿不愿意为一名球员多掏 125.7 万，取决于她能不能带来上座率和转播镜头，这条逻辑一个字都没变。

Ashley Sanchez 和 Catarina Macario 是这次改动的受益者，报道点了她们的名字：一个此前没够上标准，一个长期受伤。这是实打实的钱，是结构层的一小步。但这一步是工会用申诉换来的，不是联盟良心发现。而联盟在同一份和解里保住了整个 HIP 结构：工资帽 440 万美元之外，仍然可以额外支出 125.7 万，只是不再规定这笔钱必须给“国际上叫得响的人”。

报道里有一句很诚实的话：球星很难衡量，但不可能看漏。这句话是整套规则的元暴力浓缩版——衡量不了，所以由有权力的人来指认。Rodman 没进金球奖名单，也不是金靴领跑者，但她是联盟“公认的球星”。公认，谁认？

值得注意的还有报道顺手写下的另一件事：金球奖的女足奖项几乎被巴萨包圆，而 Putellas 拿第三座奖杯时人在伦敦。国际荣誉的分配本来就不公平，欧洲超级球队的支出是某些 NWSL 球队的三倍以上。HIP 规则当初想在美国造一支能对抗这种集中的超级球队，这个动机不算错。错的是它把“谁算球星”的判定权交给了同一批生产荣誉的机构，然后宣布这是市场规律。

工会这次赢下的是程序：不能再用一份媒体榜单决定一个球员的工资。没赢下的是实质：球员的价值仍然由别人定价，只是定价的人换了个办公室。下一次申诉大概会从这个差额开始。

NWSL settled with its players association and the disputed criteria are gone. That is a good thing, in a narrow layer. The original High Impact Player rule required a player to meet one of eight categories before her team could spend an extra $1.257m above the salary cap: SportsPro's top 150 Most Marketable Athletes, ESPN's Top 50, the Guardian's top 100, US national team minutes, the Ballon d'Or's 30-player list, NWSL MVP and Best XI. The union argued this violated federal labor law. The league conceded. Now any player can get the money.

Not one of those eight criteria was written by a player. They are all produced by media, awards and the league itself: who makes the list, who gets written, who gets seen. The rule's original purpose is stated plainly in the report — to keep Trinity Rodman from drifting across the Atlantic to Michele Kang's London City Lionesses instead of staying at Michele Kang's Washington Spirit. Same owner, two clubs, one rule deciding which shirt she wears. That is not protecting a player. That is putting a lock on an asset.

So what got revised is not who is valuable. It is who gets to declare it. Before, the lists declared it. Now the clubs do. The club is still the party pricing a player as an asset; the ruler just moved from the newsroom back to the front office. The report says teams can now decide for themselves who is worthy, and that a cap on how many players can access the money gives cash-strapped teams the theoretical ability to rein in stacked rosters. Theoretically. Whether a club spends an extra $1.257m on a player still depends on whether she fills seats and draws cameras. Not one word of that logic changed.

Ashley Sanchez and Catarina Macario are named as beneficiaries: one who previously fell short of the criteria, one with long-lasting or recurring injuries. That is real money, a small step at the structural layer. It was won by a grievance, not by the league's conscience. And the league kept the entire HIP structure intact in the same settlement: $4.4m salary cap, plus $1.257m extra, just no longer reserved for the internationally famous.

One line in the report is honest to the point of self-indictment: stardom is imprecise, hard to measure, impossible to miss. That is the whole meta-violence compressed into a sentence — unmeasurable, therefore named by whoever holds the power to name. Rodman did not make the Ballon d'Or shortlist and is not leading the Golden Boot race. She is still the league's presumed star. Presumed by whom?

The report also notes, almost in passing, that Barcelona's women took nearly every Ballon d'Or award available, and that Putellas collected her third while playing in London. International accolades were already distributed unevenly; Europe's super-teams spend three times or more what some NWSL teams do. The original HIP criteria wanted to build an American super-team capable of contesting that concentration. The motive was not wrong. The error was handing the definition of stardom to the same institutions that manufacture the honors, then calling the result a market.

The union won the procedure: a media list can no longer set a player's wage. It did not win the substance: players are still priced by someone else, and the someone just changed offices. The next grievance starts from that gap.

穷人的补贴，富人的分红Poor Households Get £158, the Rich Get £144

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
十年收入只涨1.4%，债务利息却超过三项福利总和。
1.4% income growth over a decade, while debt interest outruns three welfare budgets combined.

Resolution Foundation 那份48页报告里最该被反复读的一行不是“2020年代是半个世纪以来最差的十年”，是债务利息。2029-30年，英国政府要付1470亿英镑的利息，比三月的预测多130亿，占GDP的4.1%，2031-32年峰值4.14%，是1956-57年以来最高——比 universal credit、住房补贴和儿童福利三项加起来还多。一个国家先付利息，再谈福利，这就是structural violence的账本：不是没钱，是钱在分配之前就被债权人拿走了。

然后是那份440亿英镑的生活成本补贴。最穷的一半家庭平均拿158镑，最富的一半拿144镑。差14镑。能源补贴按市政税band发，A-D band里住了87%的穷人和73%的富人——这不是“设计中不小心漏了”，这是用35年前的房产估值当筛选器，等于根本没筛。IFS的Helen Miller说得很客气：政府连一份能精准定位的能源数据都懒得建。一个连谁在挨冻都不愿知道的政府，发出去的每一镑都是表演。

但报告也说了一句让工党能拿去当挡箭牌的话：自上台以来税和福利的净变化是累进的，到2029-30年最穷五分之一家庭年收入多320镑，最富五分之一少2000镑。320镑。分摊到四年，一年80镑。这是“公正的表达”吗？这是把两孩限制废除和 universal credit 上调算进去之后的结果——两项都是本届预算之前就宣布的，也就是说，这次预算本身在再分配上几乎什么都没做。

真正在动的是另一层。2028-29年之后，除国防、卫生、教育外，13个“不受保护”部门的经费到2031-32年会掉到GDP的4.7%，接近紧缩后的历史低点。IFS说，要避免实际削减，2030-31年需要补170亿，这还没算国防要的110亿。Miller直接说她不信这届政府会执行自己写下的削减计划——“历史建议怀疑，财相们把紧缩 penciled in 在未来，通常不会兑现。”这句话的意思是：账面上写的是削减，实际发生的是拖到下一任，或者再借。

Healey 在Bloomberg说“hard decisions”，在GB News说“接受我们已经有了脱欧决定”，在Today节目里被问到选举前会不会砍福利，他说“There will be there will be a reduction in the benefits bill”，然后财政部出来说他被误读了，没承诺时间表。Badenoch抓住这句说“astounding”，说工党自己数据显示到2030年工作年龄福利要涨600亿。两边都在争谁更狠地砍福利，没有人问福利为什么涨——因为租金、能源、工资停滞把更多人推了进去。

还有一个细节没人提。同一天政府宣布禁止向18岁以下出售无尼古丁电子烟。Whitty 教授在《每日镜报》写文章说，电子烟对成年烟民是减害工具，但“that is a low bar”，孩子不该用。这话是对的。但一个连儿童电子烟都要靠立法堵、而能源补贴按35年前房价发的政府，它的优先顺序已经写在纸上了：管得住孩子的肺，管不住老人的暖气。

最后说回那个数字。2020年代RHDI总共涨1.4%，约合2026-27年价格的400镑，是2010年代增速的十分之一，而2010年代本身已经是记录上第二差的十年。400镑，十年。这不是“增长放缓”，这是把整整一代人的收入按在原地，同时告诉他们这是必要的财政纪律。谁的必要？1470亿利息的必要。

The line in the Resolution Foundation's 48-page report that deserves rereading is not "the 2020s are the worst decade in half a century." It's the debt interest. By 2029-30 the UK government will spend £147bn servicing its debt, £13bn more than forecast in March, 4.1% of GDP, peaking at 4.14% in 2031-32 — the highest since 1956-57. That is more than universal credit, Housing Benefit and Child Benefit combined. A state pays interest first and negotiates welfare second. This is the ledger of structural violence: the money isn't missing, it's claimed before distribution begins.

Then the £4.4bn cost of living package. The poorest half of households get £158 on average; the richest half get £144. A £14 gap. Energy support is routed through council tax bands, and bands A-D hold 87% of the bottom half and 73% of the top. That is not a design flaw. That is using property values from 35 years ago as a means test, which is to say, no means test at all. Helen Miller of the IFS put it gently: the government never bothered to build the data that would have allowed targeting. A government that won't learn who is freezing cannot claim that any pound it hands out is help.

The report does hand Labour a shield: tax and benefit changes since it took office are progressive overall, with the bottom quintile £320 a year better off by 2029-30 and the top quintile £2,000 worse off. £320. Over four years. £80 a year. And most of that comes from the two-child limit repeal and the universal credit uprating — both announced before this budget. The budget itself did almost nothing on redistribution.

The real movement is elsewhere. After 2028-29, the 13 "unprotected" departments outside defence, health and education fall to 4.7% of GDP by 2031-32, near the post-austerity low. The IFS says avoiding real-terms cuts would take a £17bn top-up in 2030-31, before the £11bn defence wants. Miller said she doesn't believe this government will deliver the cuts it has penciled in — "history advises scepticism. When chancellors pencil in tight spending plans in the future, they tend not to deliver." Which means the plan on paper is either deferred to the next government or quietly borrowed over.

Healey said "hard decisions" on Bloomberg, said he accepts "we've got a Brexit decision" on GB News, and when Nick Robinson asked whether welfare would be cut before the election, said "There will be there will be a reduction in the benefits bill." The Treasury then clarified he hadn't committed to a timetable. Badenoch called it "astounding" and cited £60bn in projected working-age welfare growth by 2030. Both sides are competing over who will cut harder. Neither is asking why the bill grows: rent, energy and stagnant wages push people onto it.

One more thing went unremarked. The same day, the government banned the sale of nicotine-free vapes to under-18s. Professor Whitty wrote in the Mirror that vaping is safer than smoking "but that is a low bar," and children should never use it. He's right. But a government that needs legislation to keep vapes from children while routing energy support through 35-year-old property values has already published its priorities: it can reach a child's lungs, not an old person's heating.

Back to the number. RHDI rises 1.4% across the 2020s — about £400 in 2026-27 prices, one-tenth of the 2010s rate, and the 2010s were already the second-worst decade on record. £400. Ten years. This is not slower growth. This is a generation's income held in place while being told it's fiscal discipline. Whose discipline? The £147bn of interest's.

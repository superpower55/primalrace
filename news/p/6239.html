雪景是假的，病是真的The Snow Is Fake. The Sickness Is Real.

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Al Jazeera ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
一片白色被定价成风景，代价由看不见的人付。
A white landscape gets priced as scenery; the bill goes to people nobody photographs.

一个三百英亩的毒废料堆，被拍照的人叫成克什米尔。

游客免费进场，新婚夫妇在蓝色人工湖边摆姿势，网红来拍 reels，电影和音乐录影带在这里取景。他们看到的是雪。六公里外的 Bhogiyawas 村民看到的是落在玉米地上的粉尘，是咳个不停的老婆，是发白、有凹坑、被磨掉的牙。

这就是文化暴力最标准的工作方式：把一个结构暴力的现场重新命名成一处景观。名字一换，因果就断了。游客 Salim Shahzada 对 Al Jazeera 说他不知道这里有什么危害，“只知道这里倒的是大理石切割废料，这座山就是废料堆的”。他说的每个字都对，但没有任何一个字进入他的身体。

代价是具体的。每天近一千辆罐车运来约五千五百吨浆料，倒进政府划定的场地。附近至少六个村子受影响。Gurjar 说自己和妻子被迫长期吃药，胸感染、牙齿问题、皮肤过敏，孩子和老人一样。65 岁的 Ugam Singh 指着自己发白的牙说，地下水已经不能喝，氟含量高到发咸，骨头也变弱了。

Rajasthan 中央大学 Laxmi Kant Sharma 带队的研究测出土壤里硅酸铅浓度是正常值的数倍，PM2.5 超过环境空气质量标准，报告直接写下“可能已有多人患硅肺病”，并说这片区域不适合人类居住。研究交给了邦政府，什么也没发生。

注意这个顺序：证据先到，然后才是沉默。

Kishangarh 是花岗岩和大理石的中心，近一千二百家加工厂，日营业额估计超过一亿六千万卢比，约两万五千人靠它吃饭。所以这不是疏忽，是分配。钱留在加工厂这一端，粉尘、氟、铅留在没有议价能力的那一端。村民向 RSPCB 投诉过，Ugam Singh 的总结是：不管哪个政府上台，没人听穷人的，“为了让富人更富，政府牺牲我们这些人的利益”。

国家绿色法庭去年受理了环保人士 Drupad Malik 的案子，认定无监管倾倒正在污染地下水和空气，责令 RSPCB 加强监测、抽样检查、处罚违规工厂。Al Jazeera 联系 RSPCB 当地一名高级官员，她拒绝就此发言。这就是 structural 层的暴力如何自我维持：裁决有了，执行的人不接电话。

而在这条链条的最末端，还多出一层：这片毒地被开放成免费景点。加工厂的成本被外部化给村民，村民的苦难再被外部化成一门旅游生意——连“ Kashmir ”这个词都被借来给废料堆加价。谁在共谋？不是那个拍照的新娘，她只是不知道自己站在什么上面。共谋的是那条把监测责任推给一个拒绝发言的机构、把“搬迁厂址”的建议收下不办的链条，以及每一个从这片白色里取景、却不必呼吸它的人。

Sharma 说他很奇怪，为什么允许人们不戴口罩来这里，甚至在这里吃东西。这个问题本身就是答案：因为来的人不被算作会生病的人。

村民要的其实很小：停止倾倒，让地重新长东西；设免费医疗营，查一查这些年吸进细尘的人。Ram Lal 说得很直白，村里没人付得起几千卢比的治疗费。

所以下一次有人把这片白称为“克什米尔”，可以顺便问一句：克什米尔不会让住在旁边的人牙齿烂掉。

A 300-acre toxic dump is being called Kashmir.

Entry is free. Newlyweds pose by the blue artificial lake, influencers shoot reels, music videos and films get made here. They see snow. Six kilometres away, the people of Bhogiyawas see dust settling on the maize, a wife coughing, teeth gone pale, pitted and worn.

This is cultural violence doing its standard trick: renaming a site of structural violence as a landscape. Change the name and the causation disappears. Salim Shahzada told Al Jazeera he knew nothing about the health effects, only that this is where marble cutting waste is dumped and the hill is made of it. Every word is accurate. Not one of them enters his body.

The cost is specific. Nearly 1,000 tankers a day empty roughly 5,500 tonnes of slurry onto the government-allotted site. At least six villages are affected. Gurjar says he and his wife are on regular medicine for chest infections, dental problems, skin allergies; children and the elderly alike. Ugam Singh, 65, points to his teeth and says the groundwater is no longer drinkable, the fluoride so high it tastes salty, his bones weakening.

A study led by Laxmi Kant Sharma at the Central University of Rajasthan found lead silicate several times above normal, PM2.5 over the ambient air quality standard, and wrote plainly that several people may already have silicosis, that the area is unfit for human settlement. The findings went to state authorities. Nothing happened.

Note the order: the evidence arrived first. The silence came after.

Kishangarh is a marble and granite hub, nearly 1,200 processing units, daily business estimated above 160 million rupees, about 25,000 jobs. So this is not negligence. It is allocation. The money stays at the processing end; the dust, the fluoride, the lead stay with the people who have no bargaining power. Residents complained to the Rajasthan State Pollution Control Board. Singh's summary: whichever government is in power, no one listens to the poor; to make the rich richer, the government sacrifices people like us.

Last year the National Green Tribunal heard environmentalist Drupad Malik's case, found that unregulated dumping was contaminating groundwater and air, and directed the RSPCB to enforce monitoring, inspect samples and penalise violating units. Al Jazeera reached a senior RSPCB official in Kishangarh. She refused to speak. That is how structural violence sustains itself: the ruling exists, the enforcer does not pick up.

And at the end of the chain, one more layer: the toxic site is opened as a free attraction. The factories externalise costs onto the villagers; the villagers' suffering is then externalised into a tourism business, with the word Kashmir borrowed to raise the price of waste. Who conspires? Not the bride posing for a photo; she just doesn't know what she's standing on. What conspires is the chain that hands monitoring to an office that won't comment, that receives the recommendation to relocate the dump and files it, and every person who takes a picture of this whiteness without having to breathe it.

Sharma said he was surprised people are allowed to visit without masks and even eat there. The question is the answer: the visitors are not counted among those who can get sick.

The villagers' demands are small. Stop the dumping so the fields can grow again. Set up free medical camps to assess people who have been breathing fine dust for years. Ram Lal put it plainly: no one in his village can afford treatment that costs thousands of rupees.

So the next time someone calls this white expanse Kashmir, ask one thing: Kashmir does not rot the teeth of the people living next to it.

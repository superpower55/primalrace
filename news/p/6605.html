把穷人穿在身上Wearing Poverty as a Costume

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Entertainment Weekly ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
她不是在扮演流浪汉，是在给慈善履历借一张脸
She didn't dress as a homeless person. She borrowed one for her charity résumé.

Martha Stewart 的万圣节造型是一张借条。破衣服、脏脸、写着“I am hungry. Please help!!”的牌子，全部从流浪汉身上借来，用来给一个已经拥有足够多东西的人加一层“我关心这个议题”的表达。借来的东西是要还的，她不想还，于是说“I am distressed”——她为难的是自己被说了，不是那块牌子上的话被谁真的举了一辈子。

这套操作在表达层面非常干净：流浪在这里不是处境，是道具；不是谁的生活，是一个可以被穿上、拍照、发帖、删除的造型。真正在街头举牌子的人没有能力在几个小时后把牌子收起来，也没有一个 Page Six 的通道让他们解释“我的本意是提高关注”。本意这个词在这里是定价权：谁有资格给自己的表达定一个善意价格，谁就只能按别人的定价被消费。

她说自己“spent a significant amount of time and effort supporting charities”。慈善在这里是共谋的收据。给慈善机构捐过钱，不等于有资格把受助者的形象租来当装饰；恰恰相反，越是用慈善给自己背书，越说明她清楚这个形象属于谁，只是不觉得需要问一声。评论里那句“Now go make this for a soup kitchen full of people you just turned into a Halloween costume”比她的整份声明准确得多。

这一层不是直接暴力，是文化层：一个被结构性剥夺到只能举牌的人，连举牌这个动作都被拿走，改装成富人的节日 costume，然后归还的时候连一句“我错了”都没有，只有“offended people”。把主语从自己换成别人，是这套叙事最标准的收尾。

值得注意的还有报道本身。整篇新闻里，没有一个流浪者说话，没有一个人被问过看到这块牌子是什么感受。被讨论的只有 Stewart 的“distressed”。这就是元暴力最日常的样子：解释权在谁手里，谁就负责定义什么叫“冒犯”。

她删了照片，评论还在那条炖鸡视频底下继续。这说明删帖不是道歉，是止损。真正没被删掉的是那个逻辑：有些人的处境可以被穿一次，有些人的不适只能被写成“offended people”。

Martha Stewart's Halloween costume was an IOU. The torn clothes, the dirt on her face, the sign reading "I am hungry. Please help!!" — all borrowed from people who actually stand on street corners holding signs, so that someone who already has enough could add a layer of "I care about this issue" to her expression. Borrowed things are supposed to be returned. She doesn't want to return it, so she says she is "distressed" — distressed that she got talked about, not that the words on that sign belong to someone who will hold one for the rest of their life.

At the level of expression, the operation is very clean: homelessness here is not a situation, it is a prop. Not anyone's life, but a look that can be put on, photographed, posted, deleted. The people actually holding signs on the street cannot put the sign down a few hours later, and they do not have a Page Six channel through which to explain that their intention was to raise awareness. Intention is the pricing power here: whoever gets to set a benevolent price on their own expression is the one who gets to consume others at their own valuation.

She says she has "spent a significant amount of time and effort supporting charities." Charity functions here as a receipt for complicity. Having donated to charities does not entitle you to rent the image of the people those charities serve as decoration; on the contrary, the more you invoke charity as your alibi, the clearer it is that you know whose image this is — you just don't think you need to ask. The commenter who wrote "Now go make this for a soup kitchen full of people you just turned into a Halloween costume" was more accurate than her entire statement.

This is not direct violence. It is cultural: someone structurally stripped down to holding a sign has even that gesture taken, repackaged as a rich person's costume, and returned without so much as an "I was wrong" — only "offended people." Shifting the subject from yourself to others is the standard closing move of this narrative.

The report itself deserves a look. Nowhere in it does a homeless person speak. No one is asked what it feels like to see that sign. The only distress under discussion is Stewart's. This is what meta-violence looks like on an ordinary day: whoever holds the interpretive power also gets to define what counts as offense.

She deleted the photo; the comments keep coming under the chicken-and-dumplings video. Deleting is not apologizing, it's cutting losses. What hasn't been deleted is the logic: some people's circumstances can be worn once, and some people's discomfort can only be written up as "offended people."

砍掉的不是预算，是明年才说The Cut Is Deferred, Not Cancelled

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
把决定推给明年，就是把代价推给没有发言权的人
Pushing the decision to next year pushes the cost onto those with no say.

Healey 这份预算的核心操作不是加税，是延期。£28bn 的缺口没有被补上，只是被搬到了明年的支出审查里。IFS 说得很直白：部门预算按现金算基本没动，而中东战争推高通胀、抬高公共服务的交付成本，等于已经默认了实际削减。这不是没算清楚，是算清楚了不说。

延期是一种很干净的暴力。它不产生任何一份写着“削减”的文件，不产生任何一天的头条，只产生一段时间差——从今天到明年支出审查之间，那些依赖未被保护的公共服务的人照常生活，照常排队，照常不知道自己正在被减去。加尔通那个公式在这里很好用：Violence = Potential − Actual。差额已经存在了，只是还没有人给它命名。命名的时间被故意往后推。

IFS 给的数字是：要避免无保护部门回到紧缩，2030-31 年的支出计划需要补 £17bn；这还没算把国防开支提到 GDP 3% 所需的另外 £11bn。也就是说，两笔钱加起来，缺口比工党承诺“不再紧缩”时面对的那个数字更大。Miller 说她怀疑的不是能不能做到，是这届政府愿不愿意做。这句话翻过来就是：账面上成立的方案，靠的是一个没人打算执行的前提。

预算里真正落了地的是对银行和地产房东加税，用来补贴被能源冲击挤压的家庭。这一笔是真的，是结构层的一次小规模再分配，方向没错。但它同时也说明了一件事：这个政府有能力在需要的时候找到钱，只是找到的钱只够做一次救济，不够做一次承诺。救济是发给家庭的，承诺是发给选民的，两者都不是发给明年那个支出审查的。

Miller 提到的另一个风险更值得看：明年低余量加上一份对部分部门“相当残酷”的审查，会再带来一轮关于加税的破坏性猜测。这是元层的操作——不是政策本身在制造不确定性，是关于政策的叙事在制造不确定性。债券市场盯着看，媒体跟着猜，家庭和地方政府在这段时间里不敢做任何长期决定。延期把决策成本转嫁给了最没有议价能力的那一端：地方议会、公立服务的使用者、靠这些服务活着的人。

还有一个名字在这篇报道里几乎不出现：Andy Burnham。文中只说“Andy Burnham 的政府正准备下一届大选”，然后就没有下文。谁在为这次延期付政治代价、谁在为它收政治红利，报道没有写，IFS 也没有说。这个空缺本身就是材料——一份把决定推到选举之后的预算，通常不是给现在这届政府用的。

真正被削减的东西要等到明年才公布。到那时候，今天这份预算里所有“reasonable choice”的说法都会显得很体面，而排队的人已经排了一年了。

The central move in Healey's budget is not a tax rise. It is a deferral. The £28bn shortfall has not been closed; it has been moved into next year's spending review. The IFS is blunt about the mechanism: departmental plans are broadly unchanged in cash terms, while the Middle East war drives inflation and raises the cost of delivering public services. Real-terms cuts are therefore already implied. This is not a failure to count. It is counting and not saying.

Deferral is a clean form of violence. It produces no document with the word "cut" in it, no single day of headlines—only a gap in time. Between today and next year's review, people who depend on unprotected public services keep living, keep queuing, keep not knowing they are being subtracted. Galtung's formula fits: Violence = Potential − Actual. The gap already exists. It just hasn't been named yet. The naming has been deliberately postponed.

The IFS numbers: avoiding a return to austerity for unprotected departments would require a £17bn top-up to the 2030-31 plans. That is before the additional £11bn needed to hit 3% of GDP on defence. Together, the hole is larger than the one Labour faced when it promised no return to austerity. Miller says her scepticism is not about whether the implied cuts could be delivered—they could—but whether this government would be willing to. Read backwards: the plan balances only on a premise nobody intends to act on.

What did land is the tax rise on banks and landlords, funding relief for households squeezed by the energy shock. That is real, a small redistribution at the structural layer, pointed the right way. It also proves something else: this government can find money when it decides to. It just found enough for relief, not enough for a promise. Relief goes to households. Promises go to voters. Neither goes to next year's review.

The second risk Miller names deserves more attention: low headroom plus a review that looks "pretty brutal" for some departments will produce another season of damaging speculation about tax rises. That is meta-level work. The uncertainty is not generated by policy; it is generated by the narrative around policy. Bond markets watch, media speculate, and households and local councils postpone every long-term decision in the meantime. Deferral transfers the cost of deciding to the end with the least bargaining power: councils, service users, people whose lives run through those services.

One name barely appears: Andy Burnham. The report notes only that "Andy Burnham's government prepares for the next election," then moves on. Who pays the political price of this deferral and who collects the political benefit is not written, and the IFS does not say. That absence is itself the material. A budget that pushes decisions past an election is usually not built for the government that is currently in office.

The actual cuts will be published next year. By then, every "reasonable choice" in today's budget will read as dignified, and the people in the queue will have been queuing for a year.

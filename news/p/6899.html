一集舒服的情景喜剧，和它不肯承认的事A Comfortable Sitcom And The Thing It Won't Say

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 A.V. Club ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要《The Fall And Rise Of Reggie Dinkins》第二季首播集《We Keep The Kisses》回归，讲述Reggie的儿子Carmelo与死对头Jerry Basmati的女儿Delilah的秘密恋情，以及纪录片导演Arthur Tobin为了拍到戏剧冲突而守株待兔。评论认为这一集节奏舒适，Tracy Morgan与Daniel Radcliffe的搭档依旧成立。
The newsThe Fall And Rise Of Reggie Dinkins returns for its second season with "We Keep The Kisses," in which Reggie's son Carmelo secretly dates the daughter of his rival Jerry Basmati while documentarian Arthur Tobin waits for the drama to explode on its own. The A.V. Club finds the premiere as comfortable as ever.
「没有真正的冲突」不是缺点，是这部剧的商业模式。
"No real conflict" isn't a flaw here. It's the business model.

这篇评论最诚实的一句话是：这部剧「本该被缺乏真正的冲突拖累」，结果没有。作者把这当成赞美。我看是自供。

Arthur Tobin 想拍一部能让自己翻身的电影，他知道 Carmelo 的秘密恋情「保证有戏」，于是对着自己的镜头表演「我绝不为了素材制造冲突」，然后蹲在墙角等它自己爆。这是全剧写得最准的一笔——不是因为他坏，是因为他真。他要的不是这家人的幸福，是这家人的幸福被拍下来。纪录片导演和被拍摄者之间的关系，从来就是一场关于解释权的谈判：谁决定这段素材意味着什么，谁就拥有这个人。剧里所有人都在笑，只有这一层没人提。

Reggie 和 Arthur 在结尾坐下来聊西西弗斯，两个过气明星承认「离目标很近却要重新开始，真的很挫败」。这一段被作者称为「底下有一层渴望」。渴望什么？渴望被再次爱。两个人都不太清楚为什么。这句「都不太清楚为什么」是全篇最重的四个字，也是全剧唯一没被笑料包住的东西。名声是一种表达，表达是存在的确证——一个人要靠陌生人的注视确认自己还活着，这不是虚荣，这是把主体性外包出去了。外包出去的东西，合同条款永远在对方手里。

然后是那个被轻轻带过的细节：Monica 让「The More You Know」重播 Reggie 当年的疫苗公益广告，因为麻疹回来了。作者的笔调是俏皮——「既然麻疹能回来，Reggie 也能」。麻疹回来是因为有人不再给孩子打疫苗；Reggie 回来是因为电视台没有新素材。两件事被并置成一个笑点，而这个笑点成立的代价是：真的有人在这条新闻的另一头生病。剧集不需要为这个负责，情景喜剧本来就不负责。但一篇夸它「舒服」的评论，最好知道自己夸的是什么——一种把一切代价都调成背景音的手艺。

Max 穿着红色乳胶衣从蛋糕上爬起来，喊「没有人有网络名声的密码，A-doy！」这句台词是这一集里唯一没有滤镜的话。它说的是：那套让人成名的机制，从来不是才华兑换名声的自动售货机，是一群人在后台决定谁上镜、谁被剪掉。Monica 请他来，是因为她需要有人懂这套机制。她懂，Reggie 不懂，Arthur 假装不懂。三个人里，只有 Reggie 还在相信努力本身会被看见。

所以这一集真正舒服的地方，不是「没有冲突」，是所有人都默契地不去碰那个冲突：这个家的收入、这个导演的片子、这个男人的第二次人生，全都挂在同一根线上——把 Reggie Dinkins 重新卖出去。卖得掉，大家继续住豪宅；卖不掉，Arthur 没有电影，Monica 没有客户，Reggie 没有观众。他们彼此喜欢，这是真的。他们彼此需要，这也是真的。前者让后者看起来像友谊。

The most honest line in this review is that the show "should be hobbled by a lack of true conflict"—and isn't. The critic means it as praise. I read it as a confession.

Arthur Tobin wants a film that restores his career. He knows Carmelo's secret romance guarantees drama, so he performs, for his own ever-running cameras, that he would never engineer a conflict for footage—then lurks in a corner hoping it explodes on its own. That's the sharpest writing in the episode, and not because Arthur is a villain. Because he's sincere. What he wants isn't this family's happiness. It's their happiness, on camera. The relationship between a documentarian and his subjects has always been a negotiation over interpretation: whoever decides what the footage means owns the person in it. Everyone on screen is laughing. Nobody says this part out loud.

At the end, Reggie and Arthur sit down and talk about Sisyphus, two washed-up stars agreeing that getting close to your goal and having to start over is genuinely frustrating. The review calls this an "underlying yearning." Yearning for what? To be adored again. Neither of them quite knows why. That "neither knows why" is the heaviest phrase in the piece, and the only thing in the episode the jokes don't cushion. Fame is an expression, and expression is how you confirm you exist. Needing strangers' attention to prove you're still alive isn't vanity—it's subcontracting your subjectivity. And the terms of that subcontract always sit with the other party.

Then there's the detail tossed off as a gag: Monica gets Reggie's old measles PSA rerun because measles is back. The review's tone is cute—if measles can return, so can Reggie. Measles is back because people stopped vaccinating their kids. Reggie is back because the network had nothing else. The joke only works by flattening both into the same punchline, and the price of that flattening is that real people are sick on the other end of it. A sitcom owes nothing here. A review calling it "comfortable" should know what it's praising: a craft that turns every cost into background music.

Max, climbing out of a cake in a red latex bodysuit, yells that nobody has a secret code to internet fame, a-doy. That's the one unfiltered line in the episode. It says the machinery that makes people famous was never a vending machine trading talent for recognition—it's a group of people deciding backstage who gets airtime and who gets cut. Monica hires him because she needs someone who understands the machinery. She does. Reggie doesn't. Arthur pretends not to. Of the three, only Reggie still believes effort alone gets seen.

So the real comfort of this episode isn't the absence of conflict. It's that everyone quietly agrees not to touch the conflict: this family's income, this director's film, this man's second act all hang from one wire—reselling Reggie Dinkins. Sell it, and the mansion stays. Don't, and Arthur has no movie, Monica has no client, Reggie has no audience. They like each other. That's real. They need each other. That's real too. The first is what makes the second look like friendship.

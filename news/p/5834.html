住在恐怖片里，谁在付房租The House Is the Product, She Pays the Rent

文娱 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
一栋被电影赋值的房子，住进去的人也在被它定价。
A house priced by a movie also prices whoever lives in it.

这栋房子真正卖的不是砖头，是《鬼驱人》给它的赋值。原房主从1982年电影上映起就住在这里，卖房时屋里还留着蜂蜜色的复古家具，买家走进去的第一感觉是穿越回80年代。她买下的是一段可以被反复出租的叙事。

她很快也明白了这一点。媒体蜂拥而至，影迷从世界各地寄来自己看这部电影的回忆，一家人停在门口自拍。于是她开始布置：只能放雪花的电视机、一叠80年代录像带、一台雅达利。影迷来了，在厨房台面上复刻牛排复活那场戏，互相拍对方被无形的手推着滑过地板，很多人自带小丑娃娃。

这不是居住，是运营。她自己说，影迷住进来的时候，她带女儿们去露营，或者去姐姐家。房子在影迷手里是片场，在她和三个女儿手里是必须让出去的房间。她把家改造成一个可以过夜的展品，而她和孩子退到展品之外。

有意思的是，她讲起这些访客时语气是愉快的，说听到他们怎么玩觉得很开心。我不怀疑这份开心。问题在于，这份开心是用什么换的：女儿们不喜欢这部电影，看到雅达利毫无兴趣，还得在每次有人来朝圣时搬走。她把这称为一种荣幸——“能住在这里是一种荣幸”。荣幸这个词，通常是给那些付出不被计价的人用的。

更值得看的是这栋房子怎样被重新定义。电影里那棵树在拍摄后被移走了，母亲黛安掉进去的那个满是骸骨的泥坑，现在是修好的游泳池。恐怖被清理干净，剩下的是一套可消费的布景。影迷来朝圣的其实不是恐怖，是恐怖被安全化之后的样子——他们知道这里不会有东西把他们拖下床，所以才敢带着小丑娃娃来过夜。

至于访客说看到灯闪、拿起座机听筒听到奇怪的声音，她自己也承认老铜线大概有寻常解释，但“不知道也许更有趣”。这句话是整篇的钥匙：不知道更有趣，所以不必知道。叙事的生产需要的正是这种自愿的悬置，和第三章里那些被制造出来的“事实”是同一种工艺，只是规模小得多，也无害得多。

真正被定价的还有另一个人：扮演罗比的那个演员奥利弗·罗宾斯，八岁时被拖下床，如今穿着红色睡衣回来，和前房主一起在门口发糖。一个成年男人重新穿上童年的戏服站在同一栋房子前，这是影迷经济里最标准的一幕——把一个人定格在他最没有议价能力的时刻，然后请他自己走回来。

所以这栋房子的账目是这样的：叙事增值，房主收租，影迷付钱买一晚的假恐怖，女儿们让出自己的家，一个前童星穿上睡衣回来发糖。没有人在这一串里被伤害，但每个人都在这条链上被定了价，包括那个说自己很荣幸的女人。

What sold here wasn't brick, it was the valuation The Poltergeist gave the address. The sellers had owned it since it appeared in the 1982 film, and the honey-coloured retro furniture was still everywhere when the buyer walked in. Her first impression was that she'd travelled back to the 80s. She bought a narrative that could be rented out repeatedly.

She figured that out fast. Press descended, fans from around the world emailed her their memories of watching the movie, families stopped outside for selfies. So she started staging it: a TV that plays only static, a stack of 80s videos, an Atari. Fans arrived to recreate the steak coming alive on the kitchen counter, to film each other sliding along the floor as if pushed by invisible hands. Many brought their own clown dolls.

This isn't living, it's operations. By her own account, when fans stay she takes her daughters camping or to her sister's. In the fans' hands the house is a set; in hers and her three daughters' hands it's a room they have to vacate. She converted a home into an overnight exhibit and moved her family outside the exhibit.

What's interesting is that she describes the visitors warmly, says she loves hearing how they spend their time. I don't doubt the warmth. The question is what it's paid for: her daughters aren't fans of the film, were unimpressed by the Atari, and have to clear out every time someone makes a pilgrimage. She calls it an honour — "to be able to live in it is an honour." Honour is a word usually reserved for people whose costs don't get counted.

More telling is how the house itself got redefined. The tree that comes alive outside the bedroom window was taken away after filming; the muddy hole full of skeletons that Diane falls into is now a finished swimming pool. The horror was cleaned up, and what remains is a consumable set. What the fans pilgrimage to isn't terror, it's terror made safe — they know nothing will drag them under the bed, which is exactly why they dare bring clown dolls to spend the night.

As for guests reporting flickering lights or mysterious voices on the landline, she admits old copper lines probably have an ordinary explanation, but perhaps it's more fun not to know. That line is the key to the whole piece: not knowing is more fun, so don't know. Narrative production runs on exactly this voluntary suspension — the same craft as the manufactured facts in chapter three, just at a smaller and far more harmless scale.

One more person gets priced: Oliver Robins, who played Robbie, dragged under the bed at eight, now comes back in red jammies to hand out candy in front of the house with the owner. An adult man putting on his childhood costume and standing in front of the same house is the standard move of fan economies — freeze a person at his least powerful moment, then invite him to walk back into it himself.

So the ledger reads: the narrative appreciates, the owner collects rent, fans pay for one night of fake terror, the daughters give up their home, a former child star puts on pyjamas and comes back to hand out candy. Nobody in this chain is harmed, but everyone in it has been priced, including the woman who says she's honoured.

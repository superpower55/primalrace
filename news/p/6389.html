缺的是老师，省的是钱The Shortage Is the Policy

其他 结构层 NPR ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
特教短缺不是没人愿意干，是有人算过账：不补这个洞更便宜。
Fifty years of shortage isn't a shortage. It's a budget line someone decided not to fund.

特教老师短缺五十年了。1975年 IDEA 写进联邦法，短缺就一起写进去了。五十年后 50 个州里 45 个还缺人，学生涨到 820 多万，每六个公立学校学生里就有一个。一件事持续半个世纪，就不能再叫短缺，那叫设计。

报道先拆了一个迷思：不是孩子难教所以没人干。Bettini 说研究里根本不是这么回事。真实的原因是孤立、需求太多、文书压死人、跨年级跨科目地教，还有钱。32 个州对最难招人的岗位不给额外报酬，特教就在里面。这是 structural violence 的标准形态：缺口明明摆着，制度选择不去填，因为不填的成本由老师个人吸收，不出现在任何一张预算表上。

这份成本有多具体：一项研究显示特教老师一天只有大约三分之一的时间在教课，其余时间在追目标、协调服务、培训助理、写 IEP。IEP 是法律文件，写它要懂特教法，要和家庭开会，要占用教学生的时间。Garcia 在芝加哥的学校没有墙，一个年级六个班在一个大房间里用隔板分开，一个孩子尖叫起来整个 pod 都跟着乱。Fitzgerald 第一年就得在课堂上把一个学生惹出的情绪危机处理到需要疏散全班。她说没人给过她这种情形的地图。

钱不是没试过。2020 年夏威夷给特教老师加薪一万美元，空缺降了 35%，但没能把多数人留住。降了 35% 还留不住，说明加薪碰到了真问题的一个角，没碰到全部。另一头是管道：每年约 46000 名特教老师离开公立学校，师范项目一年培养出不到 30000 人来补。缺口就这样一年一年地滚。

报道里唯一像样的解法来自 Anderson-Walker 的学校：Boys' Latin of Philadelphia 请了专门做 IEP 文书的人，把老师的时间还给课堂。他说没有那份压力太好了。这是 structural 层的正确动作：不是让老师更能扛，是把压在他们身上的东西拿走。Fitzgerald 和校领导谈出了专门的备课时间，也是同一个方向。但这些是个别学校自己想办法，不是制度。

这里要问一句谁在共谋。不是哪个坏人，是整条链条上每个人都默认特教老师会用个人的时间和健康去补系统的洞。Swanson 说没人为了薪水干这行，学生的一点进步就是回报。这话是真的，也正因为是真的，它才好用：一份靠奉献感支撑的工作，可以长期不按工作付钱。把老师的爱当成补贴，是最省事的一种财政安排。

结尾要指的不是下一个战场，是这笔账里没被算进去的那部分。820 万学生的 IEP 是法定权利，执行它的人一年少 16000 个。差额不会消失，它只是被转成了某些老师没有的生活、被疏散的课堂、以及那些没能进合唱团的孩子。

Special education has been short of teachers for fifty years. IDEA was written into federal law in 1975, and the shortage came in with it. Five decades later, 45 out of 50 states are still short, while the number of special education students has climbed past 8.2 million — about one in six public school students. Something that persists for half a century stops being a shortage. It becomes a design.

The report kills the obvious myth first: it's not that the kids are hard to work with. Bettini says the research doesn't bear that out at all. The real causes are isolation from other staff, too many student needs, crushing paperwork, teaching across several subjects and grade levels, and pay. In 32 states, teachers get no extra money for the hardest-to-staff positions, special education among them. That is structural violence in its standard form: the gap is visible, the institution chooses not to close it, because the cost of not closing it is absorbed by individual teachers and never shows up on a budget line.

The cost is concrete. One study found special educators spend only about a third of their day on instruction. The rest goes to tracking goals, coordinating services, training support staff, writing IEPs — legal documents that require knowing special education law, meeting with families, and time away from students. Garcia's school in Chicago has no walls: six classes per grade in one enormous room divided by partitions, so when one child screams the whole pod destabilizes. Fitzgerald, in her first year, had to evacuate her class during a student's emotional crisis. No one handed her a road map for that.

Money has been tried. Hawaii raised special education salaries by $10,000 in 2020 and cut vacancies by 35% — and still couldn't keep most teachers in the job. Cutting vacancies by a third and still losing people means the raise touched a corner of the problem, not the problem. The pipeline is the other half: about 46,000 special education teachers leave public schools each year, while preparation programs train fewer than 30,000 to replace them. The gap rolls forward year after year.

The only real fix in the report comes from Anderson-Walker's school. Boys' Latin of Philadelphia hired staff to handle the IEP paperwork and gave teachers their classroom time back. He says not having that stress is amazing. That is the correct structural move: not making teachers more resilient, but taking the weight off them. Fitzgerald negotiating dedicated planning time with her leadership points the same way. But these are individual schools improvising, not a system.

So who is complicit? Not a villain — an entire chain that quietly assumes special education teachers will cover the system's holes with their own hours and health. Swanson says no one does this for the paycheck; the students' small victories are the reward. That is true, and because it is true, it is useful: a job held together by devotion can go on being underpaid indefinitely. Treating a teacher's love as a subsidy is the cheapest fiscal arrangement available.

The 8.2 million students' IEPs are a legal right, and the people who execute them are disappearing at a rate of roughly 16,000 a year. The difference doesn't vanish. It becomes teachers with no life outside school, classrooms evacuated for safety, and kids who never make the choir.

她收下那条管道的钱She Took the Pipeline's Money

国际 结构层 · 文化层 Mother Jones ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
反抗的叙事被收编，从来不用打，只要收购。
A rebellion gets absorbed not by force, but by a check.

Energy Transfer 给了 Haaland 至少 24,800 美元。就是那家拥有 Dakota Access Pipeline 的公司，就是那家在 Standing Rock 出钱支持武装清场的公司。十年前警察和国民警卫队把装甲车开上北达科他草原、一天抓了将近 150 人的时候，Haaland 在营地里用明火烙玉米饼，沿路发 #NoDAPL。十年后，这家公司成了她的捐款人之一，也成了 Project Jupiter 的独家燃料供应商。这不是背叛的戏剧，这是收购的日常价目表。

这笔钱在她 2050 万美元的竞选资金里只占 1%。项目方相关的 Oracle、高管及其他关联人给了 10 万多美元。1% 就能买下一个进步派偶像的沉默，这才是关键——不是价格高，是价格低得离谱。她拒绝退还，理由是竞选太贵。这句话本身没错，竞选确实贵，贵到让一个曾经从 Wells Fargo 撤出党产的人，在十年后算出了另一笔账。

真正被武器化的不是石油，是“原住民女性”“气候英雄”“Auntie Deb”这套叙事。她靠这套叙事拿到了全国性的认知入口，现在这套叙事在为她争取宽恕。Joe Monahan 说得直白：她被民主党的左翼放过了。初选时进步派追着对手 Sam Bregman 在得州讨好石油金主，同一时期她的竞选财务报告里躺着两笔各 6,200 美元的埃克森美孚捐款。同一把尺子，量别人时是尺子，量自己时是橡皮筋。

她承诺上任第一天暂停新建数据中心。Oracle 那个 1,400 英亩的超大规模数据中心不受影响，今年已经开工。这个暂停令的形状，正好绕开了给过她钱的那一个。

再看新墨西哥州的账本：油气收入撑起去年 102 亿美元运营预算的近一半，养着免费托育、免学费大学、保证校餐。州投资基金约 750 亿美元，预计到 2030 年前后到 1000 亿。她说的“油气目前是必要的恶”，在这个结构里是实话——福利的现金流接在一根正在烧的管子上，州气候官员本周刚作证 2030 年减排目标已经脱轨，2050 净零的法案上个会期死了。她对这些一无所知般地自信。

这不是她一个人的共谋。这是一整套共谋：选民要福利，政党要席位，候选人要钱，公司要许可，每一方都在既定秩序里拿到了自己那一份，代价被记在 Chihuahua 沙漠的地下水上，记在 Sunland Park 那些从小拉饮用水的孩子身上。Isaac Lira 三十二岁，在那长大，他说的是“还钱，和你的族人建立信任”。他被请出了会场。她在十月中旬走出自己的社区论坛，因为同一个问题被再问了一次。

Frijoles 大火烧到一天一千英亩、烟浓到山看不见、歌剧演到一半停下来的那个八月，她在圣达菲对二十几个支持者讲“每个孩子都到户外去”。在一个连课间活动都被赶进室内的城市，这个提案听起来像是对着已经不存在的东西讲话。

她不会把 Chaco 卖掉，这一点我信。但 Chaco 是可以用来拍募款视频的，而 Project Jupiter 的水权是可以谈的。区别在于哪个更贵。

Energy Transfer gave Haaland at least $24,800. The same company that owns the Dakota Access Pipeline, the same company that helped bankroll the militarized clearing at Standing Rock. Ten years ago, when police and National Guardsmen rolled armored vehicles across the North Dakota prairie and arrested almost 150 people in a day, Haaland was in the camps cooking tortillas over an open fire, posting #NoDAPL along the way. A decade later that company is one of her donors, and the exclusive fuel provider for Project Jupiter. This is not the drama of betrayal. This is the routine price list of acquisition.

The money is 1 percent of her $20.5 million war chest. Oracle, its executives, and others tied to the project gave over $100,000. One percent was enough to buy a progressive icon's silence — the point is not that the price was high, it's that it was insultingly low. She declined to return it, citing the cost of campaigns. That sentence is true. Campaigns are expensive, expensive enough that someone who once pulled party funds from Wells Fargo can, ten years later, do a different sum.

What got weaponized here isn't oil. It's the narrative: first Native American cabinet secretary, climate hero, Auntie Deb. That narrative bought her a national cognitive entry point, and now the same narrative is buying her forgiveness. Joe Monahan put it plainly: she's been given a pass by the Democratic left. During the primary, progressives went after her challenger Sam Bregman for courting Texas oil donors while her own finance reports showed two $6,200 contributions from Exxon. Same ruler. Against others it measures; against herself it stretches.

She promised a Day One moratorium on new data centers. Oracle's 1,400-acre hyperscale campus is exempt; construction started this year. The moratorium has been drawn in exactly the shape that spares the one that paid her.

Then there's the ledger. Oil and gas financed nearly half of last year's $10.2 billion operating budget, carrying universal child care, tuition-free college, guaranteed school meals. State investment funds sit around $75 billion, projected to hit $100 billion by the end of the decade. Her "necessary evil, right now" is, inside this structure, honest — the welfare cash flow is wired to a burning pipe. State climate officials testified this week that New Mexico is off-track for its 2030 targets; the net-zero-by-2050 bill died last session. She assesses the state's environmental future with unbothered confidence.

She is not the only co-conspirator. This is the whole arrangement: voters want benefits, the party wants seats, the candidate wants money, the company wants permits. Everyone gets their share of the existing order, and the bill is charged to groundwater in the Chihuahua Desert and to the kids in Sunland Park who grew up hauling drinking water. Isaac Lira, 32, raised there, asked her to return the money and build trust with her people. He was asked to leave. In mid-October she walked out of her own community forum after being confronted with the same question again.

In August, with the Frijoles Fire eating 1,000 acres a day, smoke thick enough to erase the mountains and halt an opera mid-performance, she stood before two dozen supporters in Santa Fe and pitched "Every Child Outdoors." In a city where even recess had been driven indoors, the proposal was addressed to something that no longer exists.

She will not sell Chaco. I believe that. But Chaco can be filmed for a fundraising reel, and Project Jupiter's water permits can be negotiated. The difference is which one costs more.

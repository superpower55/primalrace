赦免的定价权在谁手里Who Sets the Price of a Pardon

其他 结构层 · 元暴力 The New Republic ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要特朗普在 Mar-a-Lago 万圣节活动上对记者批评前私人律师 Alina Habba Mildon，称她收取 5000 万美元帮一名委内瑞拉亿万富翁家族争取总统赦免「令人作呕」，并暗示这笔钱本应捐给他的总统图书馆。报道同时提到，特朗普此前已公开承认用赦免换取政治支持。
The newsTrump told reporters at a Mar-a-Lago Halloween party that he was outraged by former personal attorney Alina Habba Mildon's $50 million deal to secure a pardon for a Venezuelan billionaire's family, saying the money should have gone to his presidential library. The report notes Trump has openly admitted trading pardons for political support.
他骂的不是贪，是没分成。
He's not angry about corruption. He's angry he wasn't cut in.

特朗普骂 Alina Habba Mildon 的那段话，值得逐字听一遍。「我觉得太可怕了」「太恶心、太过分了」「不可接受」。一个公开承认过拿赦免换政治支持的人，在骂一桩赦免交易。这构不成伪善，伪善还要求他知道自己在装。他根本没在装。

他的原话是「我不希望她拿钱」。不是不希望有人拿钱，是不希望她拿。他真正在意的不是那 5000 万美元的价格，是这笔钱没有经过他的手。报道写得很清楚：他此前就说过，她应该把钱捐给他的总统图书馆或与他相关的实体。赦免在他眼里是一门生意，他愤怒的是一个下属绕开了总代理，自己接了单。

这是第三章说的武器化，只不过武器不是叙事，是赦免权本身。赦免是总统手里最纯粹的定价工具——没有市场价，没有监管，没有收据，谁出价、出什么价、以什么名义出，全由掌握解释权的人说了算。Habba Mildon 的问题不是她发明了这套玩法，是她证明了这套玩法可以外包。一旦赦免可以被中间人代理，定价权就从总统手里流出去了一部分。特朗普骂的是这个。

再看第四章。Habba Mildon 是他的前私人律师，是共谋链条上的人。共谋者之间的关系从来不是忠诚，是分成。分成本身没问题，问题是分成的比例和路径。这笔交易里她拿了 5000 万，他没拿到，于是共谋关系当场破裂，破裂的方式是把对方公开定性成「过分」。共谋者理论里最典型的场景：秩序没变，规则没变，只是有人多拿了一口，剩下的人开始指责那一口的道德性。

报道里没有出现的，是那个被赦免的人。委内瑞拉亿万富翁 Julio Herrera Velutini，他的女儿开的公司付了这笔钱，赦免最终给了谁、以什么理由给，报道没写。5000 万美元能买到的那个东西，在整篇报道里连个名字都没有。付钱的人、收钱的人、抱怨分赃不公的人，全在台前；被定价的那个物件，隐身了。

赦免权本身才是这条新闻里最该被追问的东西：一个可以被明码标价、可以被中间人代理、可以在万圣节派对上被记者追问的总统权力，它的价格由谁定，它的买家是谁，它的卖家在抱怨什么。特朗普说这件事「不可接受」。他说得对，只是他和我们说的不是同一件事。

Listen to what Trump actually said about Alina Habba Mildon. "I thought it was terrible." "Totally obnoxious and outrageous." "Not acceptable." A man who has openly admitted trading pardons for political support is denouncing a pardon deal. This isn't hypocrisy—hypocrisy requires knowing you're pretending. He isn't pretending.

His words were "I don't want her paid." Not that nobody should be paid. That she shouldn't. What bothers him isn't the $50 million price tag; it's that the money didn't pass through his hands. The report is explicit: he had already said she should donate some of it to his presidential library or another entity related to him. A pardon, in his accounting, is a business. What enraged him was a subordinate cutting out the franchisor and taking the order herself.

This is weaponization in the sense of Chapter 3, except the weapon isn't narrative—it's the pardon power itself. A pardon is the purest pricing instrument a president holds: no market rate, no oversight, no receipt. Who bids, how much, under what pretext—all decided by whoever controls the interpretation. Habba Mildon's offense wasn't inventing the game. It was proving the game can be outsourced. Once pardons can be brokered by intermediaries, part of the pricing power leaves the president's hands. That's what he's denouncing.

Chapter 4 now. Habba Mildon was his former personal attorney—a link in the co-conspirator chain. The bond between co-conspirators is never loyalty; it's split. Splits are fine. The problem is the ratio and the route. She took $50 million; he got nothing; the conspiracy ruptured on the spot, and the rupture took the form of publicly reclassifying her cut as "outrageous." The order didn't change. The rules didn't change. Someone took a bigger bite, and the rest started moralizing about the bite.

The person who was pardoned never appears. Julio Herrera Velutini, the Venezuelan billionaire; his daughter's company paid; who received the pardon and on what grounds—the report doesn't say. The thing $50 million bought doesn't even get a name in the piece. The payer, the recipient, the man complaining about an unfair split—all onstage. The object being priced—offstage.

The pardon power itself is what this story should be about: a presidential authority that can be priced, brokered, and questioned by reporters at a Halloween party. Who sets its price, who buys, and what the seller is complaining about. Trump says this is "not acceptable." He's right. He just means something different than we do.

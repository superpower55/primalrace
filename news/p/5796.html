球迷看球，俱乐部收租Who Gets to Say How a Match Is Watched

文娱 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
谁有权规定一场球该被怎样体验，谁就在定价。
Whoever defines how a game should be experienced is the one pricing it.

多特蒙德推出“球迷模式”奖励计划，表面上是劝人放下手机、活在当下，实际是在做一件事：给“怎么看球”定标准，然后按这个标准给观众打分。俱乐部CEO说“此刻不可替代”，沃达丰德国CEO说“把手机放回口袋”——一个卖票的，一个卖流量的，联名告诉你别用手机。这个组合本身就说明问题。

这不是文化冲突，是定价权的争夺。球迷买票进场，买的是观看的权利，不是被规定如何观看的义务。俱乐部把“专注”包装成忠诚，把“解锁次数”变成积分，等于宣告：你的体验归我管，你的注意力是我的资产。这条逻辑在第三章里叫武器化——认知入口不必是新闻和宗教，一个App的“球迷模式”就够了。

俱乐部真正想赶走的是influencer。那些人拿球场画面做素材，把球迷的歌声和看台变成可售卖的内容，分走了俱乐部本可以自己变现的东西。文章说得很清楚：德乙的柏林赫塔和德累斯顿已经全面禁止商业网红视频，理由是电视转播权的完整性和球迷体验。美网今年给一百多个内容创作者发了证，结果有人打光打到比赛暂停；温网干脆不发。多特蒙德对网红动手，逻辑通顺，利益清楚。

问题在于，赶走网红的同时，普通球迷也被顺手纳入了同一套管理。网红是竞争者，球迷是资源，两者在俱乐部的账本上是同一栏。奖励计划不是请客，是筛选：愿意配合的，给球衣、给礼品卡；不愿意的，你就是那个“不解锁手机也拿不到分”的人。这跟第二章讲的假最优解表达是一回事——你按他认可的方式扮演一个好球迷，换来一点短期好处，代价是你对“我怎么看球”这件事的自主权。

文章问了一串问题：球迷欠球队什么？球队有没有资格给支持者做纯度测试？现场足球是不是只是内容？这些问题的答案不取决于道德，取决于谁掌握入口。德甲的50+1规则让球迷保有俱乐部多数股权，票价便宜，政策向球迷倾斜，多特蒙德多年被当成球迷文化的样板——正因为如此，它才有资格来管你。样板间里最容易被规训的，恰恰是最忠诚的那批人。

球迷会“用信用卡投票”，文章这么结尾。这话对，但只对一半。真正投票的不是散场后买不买周边，是俱乐部在制定规则时有没有被问过。看台上的八万一千三百六十五个人一起喊出首发名单的姓氏，那种声音是他们的，不是俱乐部的资产。什么时候俱乐部开始给这种声音记分，它就已经不把这声音当自己的了。

Dortmund's "fan mode" is being sold as a plea to put the phone down and live in the moment. What it actually does is set a standard for how to watch football, then grade the audience against it. The club's CEO says the here and now is irreplaceable; the CEO of Vodafone Germany says leave the smartphone in your pocket. One sells tickets, the other sells data. The joint statement tells you what kind of scheme this is.

This isn't a culture war. It's a fight over who prices the experience. A ticket buys you the right to watch, not an obligation to watch the way the club prefers. Turning "focus" into loyalty points, counting every unlock, is the club announcing that your attention is its asset. Chapter three calls this weaponisation. The cognitive entry point doesn't have to be news or religion. An app's "fan mode" will do.

The real target is influencers. They film the Yellow Wall, monetise the singing, and siphon off value the club would rather capture itself. The article is explicit: Hertha Berlin and Dynamo Dresden have banned commercial influencer videos outright, citing the integrity of TV rights and the fan experience. The US Open credentialed over a hundred content creators this year and had to stop a match when a chair umpire asked them to kill a ring light. Wimbledon refuses to credential them at all. Dortmund going after influencers is coherent and self-interested.

The trouble is that policing influencers sweeps ordinary fans into the same management system. Influencers are competitors; fans are resources. On the club's ledger they sit in the same column. The rewards programme isn't a gift, it's a filter: play along and get a jersey or a gift card; don't, and you're the one who scored nothing for leaving your phone alone. This is the fake optimal expression from chapter two. Perform the fan the club recognises, collect a short-term reward, and pay with your say over how you watch.

The piece asks the right questions: what do fans owe their teams? Do clubs get to purity-test their supporters? Is live football just content? The answers don't come from morality. They come from who holds the entry point. The Bundesliga's 50+1 rule keeps fans as majority owners, tickets cheap, policy fan-first, and Dortmund has long been held up as the model of supporter culture. That is precisely why it can now tell you how to behave. The most disciplined people in a showroom are always the most loyal.

Fans will vote with their credit cards, the article concludes. True, but only half true. The vote that matters isn't whether you buy a scarf on the way out. It's whether you were consulted when the rules were written. Eighty-one thousand three hundred and sixty-five people shouting the surnames of the starting eleven is a sound that belongs to them, not to the club. The moment the club starts keeping score of that sound, it has stopped treating it as its own.

用护照换来的保护A Passport Is the Price of Protection

其他 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-29 § 链接
保护未成年人的代价，先落在没有护照的孩子身上
The cost of protecting teenagers lands first on the ones without passports.

英国明年春天要封掉16岁以下孩子的社交媒体。Ofcom自己算出来：约15万名16到17岁的青少年没有护照也没有驾照，而这两样东西很可能成为进入受限平台的通行证。也就是说，这场以保护为名的禁令，最先筛掉的不是平台，是穷孩子。

看它怎么落地。Ofcom列了四种"高度有效"的验证方式：官方证件加实时自拍、银行核验账户年龄、Young Scot卡这类官方数字身份、以及用自拍估算年龄。但自拍估龄可能差好几岁，所以需要官方证件做"兜底"。兜底的意思是：没有证件的人，请先去办一张。16岁以上英国人九成以上有护照或驾照——反过来读，剩下那一成，正是这场禁令真正咬住的人。

这不是技术问题，是结构暴力最标准的形态。政策文本里没有一个字提到阶级，但执行路径自动把有证件的人和没证件的人分成了两个物种。办护照要钱，要父母配合，要一个稳定的家庭地址。Ofcom说"没有照片证件的人可能需要采取措施去获取一个"，这句话写得像在提醒你带伞，实际上是在说：想上网，先证明你是个被国家登记过的人。

而它要解决的问题，本身就没被解决。Ofcom的负责人Dawes自己承认，"有动机的孩子"会想办法绕过去，还拿酒类法律管不住未成年人饮酒来打比方。澳大利亚去年12月实施同类禁令，eSafety专员说"相当比例"的16岁以下孩子照旧在用；900名澳洲家长的调查里，约三分之一说孩子仍有至少一个账号——禁令前这个数字是49%。从49%降到三分之一，花掉的是一整套年龄验证基础设施，和15万个没有护照的孩子的账号。

Dawes说这是"英国社会的一次巨大文化转变"。文化转变这个词用得很准，只是方向和她说的相反：真正被转变的是"上网需要出示证件"这件事变得正常。今天是为了保护孩子，明天是为了保护你不看色情网站——英国去年已经对成人网站这么干了，报道明说自拍估龄这套方法会接着用在社交媒体上。年龄验证一旦建成，就是一台通用的身份闸机，谁被放行、谁被拦在外面，由证件决定，不由需要决定。

这里还有一层没人问的问题：这些平台对16岁以下的孩子到底造成了什么，报道一个字没提。禁令的正当性被当成前提，讨论全部集中在怎么执行。一个以"保护"为入口的政策，保护的对象在整篇报道里是缺席的——没有孩子说自己被什么伤害了，只有监管者在讨论闸机装在哪。

澳大利亚的经验摆在那里：绕过去的孩子绕过去了，绕不过去的孩子失去了账号。英国这15万人里，有多少是移民家庭、租房家庭、父母本身就没有护照的孩子，报道没写。Ofcom也没写。它只写了"可能需要采取措施去获取一个"。措施是什么，谁付钱，谁跑腿，谁在等护照寄到的那几个月里从同学的群聊里消失——这些不在评估报告里。

保护从来不是免费的。这次账单开给了那15万个孩子，而开账单的人甚至不觉得这是一笔账。

Britain will lock under-16s out of social media next spring. Ofcom's own numbers: about 150,000 16- and 17-year-olds have neither a passport nor a driving licence, and those are likely to become the ticket into age-restricted platforms. A ban sold as protection will filter out, first of all, not the platforms but the poor kids.

Look at how it lands. Ofcom lists four "highly effective" verification methods: official ID checked against a live selfie, a bank confirming the account holder's age, an official digital ID like the Young Scot card, or estimating age from a selfie. Facial estimation can be off by several years, so official ID is needed as a "backstop." Backstop means: if you don't have documents, go get some. More than nine in ten Britons over 16 hold a passport or licence — read it the other way, and the remaining tenth is exactly who this ban bites.

This is not a technical problem. It is structural violence in its standard form. The policy text never says class, but the enforcement path quietly splits the documented from the undocumented into two species. A passport costs money, requires cooperative parents, requires a stable address. Ofcom writes that people without photo ID "will likely need to take steps to obtain one" — phrased like a reminder to bring an umbrella, meaning: to get online, first prove the state has registered you.

And the problem it claims to solve goes untouched. Ofcom's chief executive Dawes concedes that "motivated children" will find ways around it, and compares the ban to alcohol laws that never stopped underage drinking. Australia imposed the same ban last December; its eSafety commissioner says a "substantial proportion" of under-16s are still on the platforms. In a survey of 900 Australian parents, about a third said their children still had at least one account, down from 49% before the law. From 49% to a third — paid for with an entire age-verification apparatus and the accounts of 150,000 kids without passports.

Dawes calls it "a huge cultural shift for our society." Cultural shift is the right phrase, pointing the other way: what is actually shifting is how normal it feels to show ID to go online. Today it protects children; tomorrow it protects you from porn sites — Britain has already done that since last year, and the report says facial age estimation will carry straight over to social media. Once built, age verification is a general-purpose gate. Who passes and who is stopped is decided by documents, not by need.

There is a question nobody asks: what these platforms actually did to under-16s. The report does not say. The ban's legitimacy is treated as given, and the entire discussion is about where to install the gate. In a policy that enters through "protection," the protected are absent from the story — no child describes any harm, only regulators deciding where the turnstile goes.

The Australian lesson is right there: the kids who could get around it did, and the ones who couldn't lost their accounts. Of the 150,000 in Britain, how many are from immigrant families, renting families, families whose parents have no passport either? The report doesn't say. Ofcom doesn't say. It says only that they "will likely need to take steps." What steps, who pays, who queues, who vanishes from the group chat for the months a passport takes to arrive — none of that is in the assessment.

Protection is never free. This time the bill goes to those 150,000 kids, and the people writing it don't even count it as a bill.

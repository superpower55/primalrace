英国的餐盘：一场关于气候殖民的共谋The UK's Plate: A Complicity of Climate Colonialism

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-08-19 § 链接
依赖脆弱地区的进口不是贸易，而是将生存风险外包给被殖民者。
Relying on vulnerable regions for food is not trade; it is outsourcing existential risk to the colonized.

英国的食物自给率在西欧垫底，80% 的水果依赖进口。这在表面上被描述为“供应链脆弱性”或“气候危机风险”，但如果用加尔通的暴力三角来看，这其实是一场典型的结构性暴力 (structural violence)。

英国选择不投资本土农业，而是将对新鲜蔬果的需求投射到地中海、北非和中南美洲。这些地区在气候危机中承受着远高于英国的 heat stress。英国人用资本在这些脆弱地区建立起一个高效的榨取网络：让那些被殖民的历史惯性所笼罩的国家承担种植的生态成本和气候风险，而自己只负责在超市货架上享受低价的便利。这本质上是认知入口的武器化——将“全球贸易”包装成文明的互利，掩盖其作为资源掠夺和风险转移的本质。

现在，气候危机让这套 scam 运转不灵了。当北非的作物因干旱减产，英国人才惊觉自己的餐盘是建立在沙滩上的。这种“脆弱性”不是意外，而是共谋的结果。英国政府、大型零售商与这些脆弱国家的生产端达成了一种默契：只要价格足够低，谁在承受极端天气，谁在承受土壤退化，并不在英国的议事日程上。这种共谋的回报是短期内的低物价和高利润，而代价则是将全球粮食安全推向一个极其不稳定的博弈状态。

所谓“呼吁政府投资本土农业”的呼吁，在此时看来更像是一种惊恐的止损，而非真正的觉醒。如果逻辑不改变，他们只是想把这种风险从外包状态转为自持，而不会真正思考如何构建一个公正的、不基于掠夺的食物系统。

The UK has one of the lowest food self-sufficiency ratios in Western Europe, importing 80% of its fruit. While framed as 'supply chain vulnerability' or 'climate risk,' this is a textbook case of structural violence.

Instead of investing in domestic resilience, the UK projected its demand onto the Mediterranean, North Africa, and Central/South America—regions enduring far more severe heat stress than Britain. By leveraging capital, the UK established an extraction network that forces these historically colonized regions to bear the ecological costs and climate risks of production, while the UK merely enjoys cheap convenience on supermarket shelves. This is the weaponisation of the 'global trade' narrative, masking resource plunder and risk transfer as civilised mutual benefit.

Now, the climate crisis is breaking this scam. As crops fail in North Africa, Britain suddenly realizes its food security was built on sand. This 'vulnerability' is no accident; it is the result of complicity. The UK government and retail giants entered a silent pact with producers in fragile states: as long as prices remained low, the actual suffering caused by extreme weather and soil degradation was irrelevant to the British agenda. The payoff was short-term low prices; the cost was pushing global food security into a precarious existential war.

The current calls for government investment in domestic farming look less like a moral awakening and more like panicked loss mitigation. Unless the underlying logic changes, they are merely attempting to bring the risk in-house rather than building a just food system that refuses to thrive on the exploitation of the vulnerable.

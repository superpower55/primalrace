狂欢里谁在数钱Who Was Counting the Money at the Party

文娱 文化层 · 结构层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
一场派对的记忆被装进空掉的夜店，门票另算
A scene's memory gets boxed in a dead club, admission extra

Romford 的故事被讲成一段浪漫的草根创业史：一个十七八岁拿到银行贷款的男孩，一间唱片店，一个叫 Suburban Base 的厂牌，最后把 Essex 的声音卖到了洛杉矶和圣保罗。卫报写得很温柔，连芝士洋葱三明治都记下来了。

值得停一下的是 Donnelly 自己那句总结："Creating a label identity – and putting barcodes on the records." 这句话比整篇稿子诚实。让 Romford 从郊区变成大都会的，不是派对精神，是他看懂了供应链：中央伦敦的店拿独家，他拿不到，于是自己压片，自己留独家，"People had to come to us"。这是认知入口的争夺，只不过这次抢的是唱片，不是叙事。

所以我不打算把这篇当成又一段怀旧来读。它其实是一份很干净的商业样本：一个外圈青年在中心已经分完蛋糕的地方，绕开看门人，自建一条供给线，然后把它出口。这件事本身没有受害者，Donnelly 也没从谁身上剥东西——他剥的是中间商的定价权。

但报道里有一处，写的人大概没意识到自己写了什么。DJ Rap 说，走进 jungle 的夜晚，"you weren't harassed by a bunch of men; you could wear what you want, you didn't have to look sexy, you could just be you"。一个女人把"不被骚扰"当成一件需要专门指出来、值得记住的事。这句话之所以成立，是因为它之外的世界不这样。一个空间被夸成自由，往往意味着别的空间不自由，而那种不自由被当成了默认设置，连这篇稿子都懒得追问。

还有一层：这个场景的黄金年代结束在什么地方？Time & Envy 2024 年关门，如今空着的场地用来办展，策展人说年轻人对模拟设备和早期数字制作方式很感兴趣。也就是说，一个活着的、嘈杂的、有冲突也有生意的场所，被换成了一个可以参观的版本。展览里复刻了录音室，复刻了海盗电台的发射机，复刻了 Boogie Times 的唱盘。原件都在，唯独那套让一个 18 岁的人能拿到贷款、能让一个郊区压片厂把唱片卖到巴西的条件，不在展柜里。

这不是谁的阴谋，是文化层最常规的操作：把一段还在发热的历史降温、装框、标价，然后告诉后来的人，你们看，这里曾经有过革命。年轻人进去看发射机，出来记住的是怀旧，不是"我也可以去抢一次供应链"。

真正被缩小的差额在哪？在 Donnelly 那句 barcodes 里——一个外圈的人拿回了自己的定价权，这是实打实的结构性小胜。而这篇稿子把它包成了情怀，情怀不教人怎么拿回定价权，情怀只教人怀念别人拿回定价权的那个年代。

Romford 现在还能再出一个 Donnelly 吗？报道没写今天那里还有什么。它只写了空掉的夜店和一场展览。

The Romford story is told as a scrappy romance: a kid barely eighteen with a bank loan, a record shop, a label called Suburban Base, and an Essex sound that ended up selling in Los Angeles and São Paulo. The Guardian is tender about it, down to the cheese and onion sandwich.

The line worth stopping on is Donnelly's own: "Creating a label identity – and putting barcodes on the records." That is more honest than the rest of the piece. What turned a suburb into a metropolis wasn't party spirit. It was his read on the supply chain: central London shops got the exclusives, he couldn't, so he pressed his own records and kept them exclusive to his shop for the first few weeks. "People had to come to us." That's a fight over the cognitive entrance, only the thing being seized was vinyl, not narrative.

So I won't read this as another nostalgia piece. It's a clean business specimen: an outer-ring kid, in a market where the centre had already divided the cake, routes around the gatekeepers, builds his own supply line, exports it. Nobody gets robbed here. Donnelly stripped the middlemen of their pricing power, not people of anything.

But there's one passage the writer probably didn't notice writing. DJ Rap says that walking into a jungle night, "you weren't harassed by a bunch of men; you could wear what you want, you didn't have to look sexy, you could just be you." A woman naming not being harassed as something worth pointing out, worth remembering. The sentence only works because the world outside it doesn't behave that way. When a space gets praised as free, it usually means other spaces aren't, and that unfreedom is the default setting — so default that this article doesn't bother asking about it.

And then: where does the golden era end? Time & Envy shut in 2024, and the empty venue now hosts the exhibition. The curator says young people are fascinated by analogue and early digital ways of making music. A living, loud place with conflict and commerce in it has been swapped for a visitable version. The studio is replicated, the pirate transmitters are replicated, the Boogie Times turntables are replicated. The originals are all there. What isn't in the display case is the set of conditions that let an eighteen-year-old get a loan and a suburban pressing operation sell records into Brazil.

No conspiracy here — just the most routine move in cultural violence: cool a history that's still warm, frame it, price it, and tell the people who come after that a revolution once happened here. Kids walk in to see the transmitters and walk out with nostalgia instead of the thought that they could go seize a supply chain themselves.

Where was the gap actually narrowed? In that one word, barcodes — an outsider taking back his own pricing power. That's a real, if small, structural win. The article wraps it in feeling. Feeling doesn't teach you how to take back pricing power. It teaches you to miss the era when someone else did.

Could Romford produce another Donnelly now? The piece doesn't say what's there today. It says there's an empty club and an exhibition.

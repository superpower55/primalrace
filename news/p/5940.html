七万警察守的是店面70,000 Police, Guarding the Shopfronts

国际 结构层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
把抗议赶出市中心，然后宣布秩序恢复了
Push the protest out of the centre, then declare order restored.

法国政府这周做的事很清楚：先禁止巴黎市中心的游行，只批准一场在东南远郊的游行，组织者周五深夜上诉被驳回；再部署七万警察；再让内政部长努涅斯出来说"公共秩序紊乱的风险非常高"。然后所有人都可以松一口气——市中心没有游行，就没有骚乱。

这不是维持秩序，这是把秩序的定义权攥在自己手里。谁可以出现在哪里，谁的声音算声音，由内政部决定。加尔通的公式在这里很好用：Violence = Potential − Actual。几十万人对低工资、生活成本、公共服务的愤怒是真实的，这个愤怒本可以以最直接的方式进入公共空间——队伍穿过共和国广场，穿过 Les Halles，被看见，被记录。这个"本可以"被行政命令削掉了，差额就是暴力。它不在直接层，没有人在这条命令里流血；它在结构层和元层，谁有资格定义"公共秩序"，谁就有资格决定谁可以被听见。

更精巧的是叙事上的配套动作。努涅斯说，当局已经识别出社交媒体上号召万圣节聚会的人煽动"革命"，还说"所有人都上岗，确保对任何麻烦做出极其强硬的回应"。注意这个顺序：先定性，再部署，再执行。德国那边基民盟的特罗姆在《商报》上写得更直白："像法国那样的场面必须不惜一切代价防止。"不惜一切代价——代价是谁付的？是那些被封在市中心之外的人，是那些晚上九点以后不能出现在香榭丽舍附近的无人陪伴的未成年人。

绿党的巴纳萨克说了一句对的话：预先把这些抗议刑事化、描绘成流窜团伙的作为，反而可能把人推向激进化。但这句话本身也值得看一眼——它是在为抗议辩护，不是在为抗议的要求辩护。学生要的是换掉那个摇摇欲坠的大学录取平台、给地方当局贷款修校舍；工会和黄马甲要的是工资和物价。政府的回应是承诺改革教育系统、降低燃油成本，学生领袖说这远远不够。"不够"这两个字，在七万警察面前，是没有重量的。

柏林警察局长施洛维克·梅泽尔说了实话：她没有什么理由担心人身或财产会受到攻击，"我们没有具体的证据表明有任何有组织的抢劫行动正在策划"。她还是加派了一千五百名警力。这就是预防性部署的逻辑——它不需要证据，它需要的是姿态。而姿态的成本，由所有想上街的人预付。

11 月 5 日全国大罢工已经宣布。到那天，真正的问题不是警察够不够多，而是那些关于工资、物价、教室和校舍的要求，能不能找到一个不需要穿过七万警察才能被听见的入口。报道里没有写这些要求有没有被任何一方认真接住。没写，就是答案的一部分。

What the French government did this week is clear enough: ban the two marches unions and the gilets jaunes planned through central Paris, authorise a single march in the far south-east instead, reject the organisers' appeal late Friday night, deploy 70,000 officers, and have the interior minister, Laurent Nuñez, announce a "very high risk of public disorder." Then everyone can breathe easy. No march in the centre, no riot.

This is not maintaining order. This is holding the definition of order in one's own hands. Who may appear where, whose voice counts as a voice — the interior ministry decides. Galtung's formula fits exactly: Violence = Potential − Actual. The anger of hundreds of thousands over low wages, the cost of living, deteriorating public services is real. That anger could have entered public space in the most direct way available — a column through République, through Les Halles, seen, recorded. The administrative order shaved that possibility off. The difference is the violence. Not at the direct layer; nobody bled over this decree. At the structural and meta layers, where whoever defines "public order" decides who gets to be heard.

The narrative choreography is neater still. Nuñez said authorities had identified social media posts for Halloween gatherings urging protesters to spark a "revolution," and that "everyone is on deck to ensure an extremely firm response to any trouble." Note the sequence: name it, deploy, enforce. On the German side, Alexander Throm of the CDU parliamentary group put it more plainly in Handelsblatt: "Scenes like those in France must be prevented at all costs." At all costs — and who pays the cost? The people sealed out of the city centre. The unaccompanied minors who cannot be near the Champs-Elysees after 9pm.

Felix Banaszak of the Greens said one true thing: criminalising these protests pre-emptively and painting them as the work of marauding gangs may drive people towards radicalisation. But look at what that sentence defends — the protest, not its demands. The students want a creaking university admissions platform replaced and loans for school renovations; the unions and the gilets jaunes want wages and prices addressed. The government's answer was pledges to overhaul education and cut fuel costs, which student leaders called not far enough. "Not far enough" carries no weight in front of 70,000 police.

Berlin's police chief, Barbara Slowik Meisel, was honest: she had little reason to fear attacks on people or property, and "no concrete evidence of any coordinated looting operations being planned." She deployed an additional 1,500 officers anyway. That is the logic of preventive deployment — it does not require evidence, it requires a posture. And the cost of the posture is prepaid by everyone who wants to take the street.

A nationwide strike is called for 5 November. The question that day will not be whether there are enough officers. It will be whether the demands about wages, prices, classrooms and school buildings can find an entrance that does not require walking through 70,000 police to be heard. The report does not say whether any party has seriously taken those demands up. The silence is part of the answer.

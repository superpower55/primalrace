二十七条耳光，戏外还有更多Twenty-Seven Takes, and Then Some

文娱 直接层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 A.V. Club ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要达斯汀·霍夫曼在《纽约客》撰文回忆《毕业生》拍摄经历，称导演迈克·尼科尔斯对他带有某种施虐倾向，一场凯瑟琳·罗斯扇他耳光的戏拍了二十七条，最终未剪进片中；他还在泳池穿潜水服时弄破耳膜，拍结尾奔教堂的戏时几乎失去意识。他后来认为自己是尼科尔斯的替身，用来承受导演的自我厌恶。
The newsIn a New Yorker essay, Dustin Hoffman recalls making The Graduate as a "terrible experience": Mike Nichols seemed sadistic toward him, a scene in which Katharine Ross slaps him ran twenty-seven takes and was cut, he punctured an eardrum in a wetsuit and nearly passed out filming the church run. He now sees himself as Nichols' stand-in, there to absorb the director's self-loathing.
只有一种人能在片场被折磨完再被写成天才的注脚
Only one kind of person gets ground up on set and then written into a genius's footnote.

霍夫曼写这篇东西，是在讲一个导演怎么折磨他。二十七条耳光，拍完头晕，最后剪掉不用。泳池里穿潜水服弄破耳膜。跑向教堂那场戏差点昏过去。这些是直接暴力，落在身体上，落在片场这个封闭空间里，施暴者有名字，有权力，有理由——「他对我有一种施虐倾向」。

但他给出的解释是：我是替身，用来吸收迈克的自我厌恶。这句话把暴力翻译成了艺术。一个导演把自己对前搭档的执念、对自己的恨，倒进一个年轻演员的身体里，反复倒，倒到二十七条，然后这段不剪进片子。它不需要进片子，它的功能在拍摄现场就完成了。这就是元暴力最干净的样本：解释权在导演手里，他决定这场戏是什么，也决定这场戏存不存在。霍夫曼的身体是耗材，耗材用完，账记在作者名下。

然后这篇报道在括号里提了一句：霍夫曼本人多年来也面对多起性骚扰或不专业行为的指控，包括在《克莱默夫妇》片场扇梅丽尔·斯特里普。这句话被放在括号里，像是编辑顺手加的免责声明。但它其实是整篇里最要紧的一句。被施虐的人，后来也施虐。共谋者不是天生的，是被制造出来的：你在一个把人的身体当材料的系统里挨过打，你学会了这套系统怎么运转，你有了权力之后，你照做。这不是替霍夫曼开脱，这是说这个链条比任何一个人都长。

所以这篇回忆录真正在做什么？它在给尼科尔斯的天才叙事添砖。一个导演越难搞，越说明他认真；演员越受苦，越证明这部片子伟大。霍夫曼自己也接受了这个交换：他说这是一次改变了他一生的拍摄，说这是「可怕的经历」，两句话放在一起，苦难就成了勋章。这是文化暴力最经济的一种运作方式——不用否认痛苦，只要给痛苦定个价，价签上写的是艺术。

没人问凯瑟琳·罗斯那二十七条里她扇了多少次手。她在这个故事里的位置是道具：一个用来打霍夫曼的手。报道没写她说了什么，因为她没被问。这就是替身下面还有替身。

Hoffman's essay is about a director grinding him down. Twenty-seven takes of a slap, dizzy by the end of the day, the slap cut from the film. A punctured eardrum in a pool. Nearly passing out on the church run. That's direct violence, landing on a body, in a closed set, with a named perpetrator who had the power and a reason: a certain sadism.

His explanation is that he was a stand-in, there to absorb Mike's self-loathing. That sentence converts violence into art. A director pours his fixation on a former partner, his hatred of himself, into a young actor's body, over and over, twenty-seven times, and then the footage doesn't make the cut. It doesn't need to. Its function was completed on set. This is meta-violence at its cleanest: the director holds the interpretive power, decides what the scene is, and decides whether it exists. Hoffman's body is material. When the material is spent, the ledger goes under the author's name.

The piece mentions in parentheses that Hoffman himself has faced multiple allegations of sexually predatory or unprofessional behavior, including slapping Meryl Streep on the set of Kramer Vs. Kramer. The parentheses make it read like a disclaimer. It's the most important line here. The abused becomes the abuser. Co-conspirators aren't born; they're manufactured. You take a beating inside a system that treats bodies as material, you learn how the system runs, and when you get power, you run it the same way. This isn't an excuse for Hoffman. It's that the chain is longer than any one man.

So what is this memoir actually doing? It's adding brick to the Nichols genius narrative. The harder a director is, the more serious he is; the more an actor suffers, the more it proves the film is great. Hoffman buys the trade himself: the shoot changed his life, and it was terrible, both in the same breath, and suffering becomes a medal. This is cultural violence at its most economical. You don't have to deny the pain. You just price it, and the price tag reads: art.

Nobody asks how many times Katharine Ross swung her hand across those twenty-seven takes. Her position in this story is prop: a hand used to slap Hoffman. The piece doesn't quote her, because she wasn't asked. There's always a stand-in beneath the stand-in.

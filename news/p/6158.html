一千次违规，零次罚款A Thousand Violations, Zero Fines

其他 结构层 · 文化层 ProPublica ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
监管者不是没看见，是不敢碰。
The regulator saw everything. It chose not to touch.

一千多次违规记录，没有一次罚款。这不是监管失灵，是监管者算过账之后的选择。

ProPublica 和 The Frontier 查了俄克拉何马州公司委员会（OCC）的数据库，Citation Oil & Gas 在过去十年里被列名超过一千次，比任何一家公司都多，包括规模相当的公司。而委员会没有任何记录显示它曾对 Citation 罚款或完成过一次执法案件。不是漏了，是从来没有。

员工不是没做事。2023 年 6 月，Bayou 油田的含盐废水从地下喷涌而出，污染了溪流，盐把土地划出一道道痕，威胁到饮用水源，联邦环保署都注意到了。州调查员把矛头指向 Citation 的注入井。油部的一位经理 Danny Ray 在邮件里写这是“紧急情况，需要立即处理”。他们建议走行政法庭正式处罚。然后呢？2024 年 4 月，包括时任油部主管 Robyn Strickland 在内的官员允许 Citation 以高于顾问建议的压力重启注入。到 2024 年底，13 口井恢复运行。第二年春天，调查结案，没有进一步行动。

一个机构经理匿名说：“他们想怎么干就怎么干。他们出过大污染，你根本碰不了他们。”另一位说：“他们把我们耗垮了。”

为什么碰不了？报道给了答案：Citation 是俄州第二大井主，接手的是大公司离开后留下的老井、低产井。这些井值不了几个钱，一旦 Citation 卖掉或者撒手不管，就成了州政府的财务包袱。于是监管变成了一笔交易：你不追究我污染，我不把烂摊子丢给你。这不是监管者和被监管者之间的博弈，这是共谋——双方各取所需，代价由住在 Bayou 边上的人、由喝那片地下水的人承担。

这不是个案，是设计。报道统计，2023 年以来油部提交的大约 750 起执法案件里，不到三分之一最终受到处罚，平均罚款约 1700 美元，最常见的处罚是 500 美元。500 美元。对一家全国最大的私营石油公司之一来说，这个数字连停车费都算不上。油部律师 Tracy Case 自己在一次全国会议上承认：“我在那儿干了 11 年，我们从来没追究过我们所说的‘惯犯’。”她给的理由是人手不够。人手不够是真的，但人手不够的时候选择放过谁、追究谁，本身就是判断，不是无奈。

Strickland 离开机构后去了州际石油和天然气契约委员会，一个经常为行业利益游说的准政府组织。报道没写她和 Citation 之间有任何交易，不需要写——旋转门本身就是回报机制的一部分。你今天对行业手下留情，明天行业给你一个职位。

再看 Citation 的辩护：污染是历史钻井加上降雨造成的，跟我们的注入无关。这套说法在 2023 年 11 月的会上被公司顾问用一系列报告端出来，被州里的顾问 Carolyn May 当场驳回去——地下成像显示一股高度污染的废水流正从注入层往上冒，威胁到居民依赖的淡水层。附近地下水检测出苯超标五倍。但公司代表在 2024 年 3 月的会上直接打断顾问发言，副总裁 Bob Redweik 甩下一句“我们哪儿也不去”就结束了会议。而坐在那儿的高层官员，包括 Strickland 和 McGinnis，全程基本沉默。

沉默就是表态。

住在 Bayou 东边十英里的牧场主 Mike Faulkner，今年 4 月发现自家地里冒出大量盐水，顺着溪流往下淌。附近有几十口 Citation 的注入井。公司照旧把责任推给旧钻井。但机构让 Citation 暂停部分井之后，他地上的喷涌多年来第一次停了。他说：“这说明他们知道盐水喷涌的原因和方式。”机构还要求公司在他地里建拦污坝和围栏，然后一次又一次给延期，9 月又延到 12 月，距离他最初报告污染已经七个月。10 月，那些被要求暂停的井在以降低的压力继续注入。

Faulkner 说：“他们就像被惯坏的孩子，这么长时间一直逍遥法外。”他把责任归给州官员，不是公司。他归对了。

这里动的不是直接暴力那一层——没有人被殴打、被枪杀。动的是结构层：一套明文规定“公司不得污染、监管者可以吊销许可并罚款”的制度，在实际执行中被高层官员系统性地架空。而让这套架空看起来合理的，是文化层的一句潜台词：这些老井总得有人管，管的人不能得罪。于是污染成了维持就业和税收的必要代价，受害者成了可以协商的变量。

Violence = Potential − Actual。本可以罚款、本可以吊销许可、本可以让 22 口井继续关着直到污染停止——机构自己的员工和顾问把这条路铺好了，证据、计算、建议全在桌上。差额就是那一千多次列名和零次处罚之间的距离。这个距离不是能力问题，是选择问题。

报道里最刺人的一句来自那位匿名经理：“你根本碰不了他们。”碰不了，是因为有人不想碰。而那个人现在在州际石油和天然气契约委员会上班。

Over a thousand violation records, not one fine. This isn't regulatory failure. It's a decision regulators made after doing the math.

ProPublica and The Frontier pulled Oklahoma Corporation Commission records: Citation Oil & Gas is named more than 1,000 times over the past decade, more than any other operator, including comparably sized ones. The commission has no record of ever fining the company or completing an enforcement case against it. Not a gap. A pattern of never.

Staff did their jobs. In June 2023, toxic wastewater erupted from the ground at the Bayou oil field, salting creeks and land, threatening drinking water, drawing EPA attention. State investigators blamed Citation's injection wells. Danny Ray, an agency manager, emailed colleagues that this was "an emergency situation that needs to be addressed promptly." Staff recommended formal punishment through the agency's court. Then in April 2024, officials including then-oil-division Director Robyn Strickland let Citation restart injection at pressures higher than its own consultants recommended. By late 2024, 13 wells were back online. The next spring, the investigation closed with no further action.

One agency manager, speaking anonymously: "They just operate however they want. They've had major pollution issues, and you can't really touch them." Another: "They wore us out."

Why can't they be touched? The reporting answers it. Citation is Oklahoma's second-largest well owner, having taken over aging, low-producing wells the majors abandoned. Those wells are worth little. If Citation sold them or walked away, they'd become the state's financial liability. So regulation became a trade: I don't pursue your pollution, you don't dump the mess on me. That's not a game between regulator and regulated. That's collusion. Both sides get what they need. The people living next to the Bayou, the people drinking that groundwater, pay for it.

This isn't an isolated case. It's the design. Since 2023, fewer than a third of the roughly 750 enforcement cases the oil division filed have resulted in punishment, according to the outlets' review. The average fine: about $1,700. The most common penalty: $500. Five hundred dollars. For one of the nation's largest privately owned oil companies, that's not even a parking ticket. Tracy Case, an attorney for the oil division, said it herself at a national conference in May: "I've been there 11 years doing this and we've never pursued what we call our 'bad actors.'" Her reason was staffing. Staffing is real. But when you're short-staffed, choosing whom to let go and whom to pursue is still a judgment. Not a helplessness.

Strickland left the agency for a job as chief projects officer at the Interstate Oil and Gas Compact Commission, a quasi-governmental body that often advocates for industry. The reporting doesn't allege any deal between her and Citation. It doesn't need to. The revolving door is the reward structure. You go easy on the industry today, the industry hires you tomorrow.

Citation's defense: the pollution comes from historic drilling plus rainfall, not our injection. The company's consultants produced detailed reports saying so. At the November 2023 meeting, the state's own consultant Carolyn May rebutted it with subsurface imaging showing a plume of polluted wastewater rising from the injection zone toward freshwater sources residents rely on. Groundwater tests near the purge sites showed benzene at five times the EPA drinking water standard. At the March 2024 meeting, Citation representatives talked over the consultants. Vice President Bob Redweik ended it abruptly: "We're not going anywhere." The top officials in the room, Strickland and McGinnis among them, stayed largely silent.

Silence is a position.

Ten miles east of the Bayou, rancher Mike Faulkner found salt water gurgling to the surface and running down a creek on his land in April. Dozens of Citation wells inject nearby. The company blamed old drilling practices again. But after the agency told Citation to pause some nearby wells, the purge on Faulkner's land dried up for the first time in years. "That tells me that they know the why and the how of that saltwater purging," he said. The agency ordered Citation to build a containment pit and fencing. Then it granted extension after extension. In September, another one, until December, seven months after Faulkner's initial report. As of October, the paused wells were injecting again at reduced pressure.

"They're like a spoiled kid," Faulkner said. "They've just gotten away with it for a long time." He blames state officials, not the company. He's right to.

The direct layer of violence isn't where this moves. No one was beaten, no one was shot. The structural layer is where it moves: rules that say companies must not pollute, that regulators can revoke permits and issue fines, systematically hollowed out by senior officials. And what makes the hollowing look reasonable is a cultural-layer subtext: somebody has to hold these old wells, and you can't afford to offend the somebody. So pollution becomes a necessary cost of jobs and tax revenue. The victims become a negotiable variable.

Violence = Potential − Actual. The agency could have fined. It could have revoked permits. It could have kept all 22 wells shut until the purging stopped. Its own staff and consultants laid that path out, evidence and calculations and recommendations all on the table. The gap is the distance between a thousand listings and zero fines. That distance isn't about capacity. It's about choice.

The sharpest line in the story comes from the anonymous manager: "You can't really touch them." You can't touch them because someone doesn't want to. And that someone now works at the Interstate Oil and Gas Compact Commission.

争论作息时间的人，从不提谁有权规定作息
Debates about clocks never ask who owns the clock

夏令时该不该取消，这不是一个时间问题。是一个谁有权规定你的身体几点醒来、几点回家、几点接孩子的问题——而这场争论里，孩子们只以"天没亮就出门上学"的形式出现了一次，然后就没了。

报道说得很清楚：1973年12月，79%的美国人支持永久夏令时；三个月后，支持率掉到42%。这个数字是整篇报道里最诚实的东西。它说明的不是美国人反复无常，而是任何关于时间的政策，都是先被宣传成"多一小时阳光"，再被身体拆穿。永久夏令时意味着冬天早上九点以后才天亮，西边时区的孩子摸黑上学。报道用了"dangerous"这个词，然后就没有下文了。孩子在黑暗里被车撞的风险，是这场辩论里唯一真实的代价，也是唯一没有代表出席的一方。

特朗普在推动永久夏令时，甚至把反对者参议员Tom Cotton的私人手机号贴了出来。高尔夫产业是"big fan"。报道把这两件事并排放在同一段里，自己可能都没意识到写了什么：一项关于全体美国人作息的立法，最积极的游说者是卖球场的，最激烈的施压方式是公开一个议员的手机号。这不是政策辩论，这是表达武器化最朴素的样子——谁嗓门大、谁能把对方的号码变成骚扰电话的入口，谁就定义了"人民想要更多阳光"。

健康上最站得住脚的方案是永久标准时间，美国睡眠医学会说它更贴合昼夜节律。但报道紧接着说，这个方案"政治上很难卖"。请注意这句话的结构：科学给出答案，政治宣布这个答案卖不出去，于是大家继续一年拨两次钟。这不是民主，这是元暴力在时间管理上的日常运作——解释权不在研究睡眠的人手里，在能决定什么"卖得动"的人手里。

真正没有人问的问题是：为什么工作时间是固定的？为什么学校八点上课？为什么"下班后的休闲时间"要靠偷一小时傍晚的阳光来补，而不是靠缩短工作日？永久夏令时是把一个结构问题（工作占满了白天）包装成一个技术问题（把钟拨一拨）。拨钟是免费的，缩短工时是要付钱的。所以国会宁可讨论一百年拨钟，也不讨论一次谁的时间被谁买走了。

最可能的结局是继续这样混着过。报道自己也这么说。这个结局里没有赢家，也没有输家被点名——因为这场博弈的代价被摊薄到了每一个冬天摸黑出门的孩子、每一个被时差折腾的身体上，摊薄到看不见。看不见的暴力不算暴力，这是加尔通那条公式最阴的地方：Potential减去Actual的差额，从来不会自己开口。

Whether to end daylight saving time is not a question about time. It's a question about who has the right to decide when your body wakes up, when you get home, when you pick up your kids — and in this entire debate, children appear exactly once, as "heading to school in the dark," and then vanish.

The most honest thing in the piece is a number: in December 1973, 79 percent of Americans approved of permanent DST. Three months later, approval had dropped to 42 percent. That's not fickleness. That's what happens when any policy about time gets sold as "one more hour of sunlight" and then gets audited by the body. Permanent DST means sunrises after 9 am on the western edges of time zones, kids walking to school in the dark. The article uses the word "dangerous" and moves on. A child's risk of being hit by a car in the dark is the only real cost in this debate, and the only party with no seat at the table.

Trump is pushing permanent DST hard enough to post the personal cellphone number of Senator Tom Cotton, who opposes it. The golf industry is a "big fan." The article puts these two facts in the same paragraph, possibly without noticing what it just wrote: legislation governing every American's sleep schedule, lobbied hardest by people who sell fairways, pressured by doxxing a senator's phone. This isn't policy debate. It's weaponized expression in its plainest form — whoever shouts loudest, whoever can turn a phone number into a harassment campaign, gets to define "the people want more sunlight."

The healthiest option is permanent standard time; the American Academy of Sleep Medicine says it better matches circadian rhythms. The article's next sentence is that this option is "a hard political sell." Read that structure carefully: science gives an answer, politics declares the answer unsellable, and everyone keeps resetting their clocks twice a year. That's not democracy. That's meta-violence doing its routine work on time management — the interpretive power doesn't sit with people who study sleep, it sits with people who decide what sells.

The question nobody asks: why are working hours fixed? Why does school start at eight? Why does "leisure after work" have to be purchased by stealing an hour of evening sunlight, instead of by shortening the working day? Permanent DST takes a structural problem — work eats the daylight — and repackages it as a technical one: move the clock. Moving the clock is free. Shortening the workday costs money. So Congress will debate clock-moving for another century before it debates once who bought whose time.

The likeliest outcome, as the piece admits, is muddling along. In that outcome there are no winners and no named losers, because the cost is spread thin across every child walking to school in the dark, every body jerked around by the time change — spread so thin it becomes invisible. Invisible violence doesn't count as violence. That's the cruelest part of Galtung's formula: the gap between potential and actual never speaks for itself.

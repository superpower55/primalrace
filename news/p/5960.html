买几个能拦住监管的朋友Buying a Few Friends Who Can Stop Regulation

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Intercept ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
钱不是买选票，是买解释权。
The money doesn't buy votes. It buys the power to explain.

160亿美元。这是本轮中期选举筹到的钱，两院选战的广告支出超过40亿（来源：The Intercept）。报道用了一个词叫 super PAC，超级政治行动委员会。名字起得很中性，像某种社区互助组织。实际上它是把资本直接接进立法流程的接口。

先看AI这边。和OpenAI、Anthropic各自关联的两大超级PAC网络，初选阶段砸了5070万美元，到了大选前只剩850万。报道说这是对民意反弹的回应。过去一年美国公众对AI和数据中心的看法转得很快，这些PAC的整个生命周期——大约一年——就是跟着这个转向走的。这个动作很诚实：它们不是在说服选民，是在等选民的情绪过去。

更有意思的是候选人的表演。密歇根第七选区，一位Sunrise Movement的联合创始人在打反数据中心牌，他的共和党对手也开始用“地方控制”的语言把自己包装成数据中心的怀疑者。两边的候选人抢着当“更支持AI监管”的那个。钱没有消失，只是从台前退到台后，让候选人替它说软话。报道里Matt Sledge那句说得很准：这些PAC在安全席位上下注，目标可能只是“在国会买几个朋友”，因为有时候能拦住监管的人和能写法规的人一样有用。

AIPAC走的是同一条路，只是早了几年。2022年它高调直接给候选人捐款，2024年之后民意转向，加沙的事让“亲以色列游说”变成政治毒药，于是它退回老本行：不直接背书，不直接给钱，改走dark money和其他组织的通道。报道提到一个名字叫“Elect Chicago Women”的团体，钱其实来自AIPAC的超级PAC UDP。这就是武器化的标准操作：夺取认知入口，先改掉招牌，再改掉“谁在付钱”这件事的可查性。

然后是洛杉矶市检察官那场几乎没人看的选举。科技投资人John Hering等人砸钱支持John McKinney，对手Marissa Roy是DSA背书的候选人，她此前参与推动了加州对Meta的那笔170亿美元和解——原因是Meta的技术伤害儿童。报道说没人能确定这些科技领袖为什么这么在意一个市检察官的位置，但你可以自己得出结论。他们给McKinney的包装是“严厉打击犯罪”“恢复秩序”，一个字都没提AI，一个字都没提Meta。

这就是共谋者理论在现实里的样子。不需要密室，不需要指令，甚至不需要候选人本人知情。出钱的人知道自己在买什么，收钱的人知道自己在替谁站台，中间的选民看到的是“秩序”“安全”“地方控制”这些词。元暴力不需要喊出来，它只需要让“谁在出钱”这个问题变得足够难查，让足够多的人不去查。

报道里唯一让人停一下的地方，是记者说初选阶段确实出现了对大额政治献金的拒绝。但那句话后面紧跟着Maia Hibbett的判断：无论哪个党拿下参众两院，下周当选的很多人都是靠大科技和特殊利益集团上的位。差额就在这里。选民能拒绝的只是表演，拒绝不了结构。

Susan Collins在缅因州没有初选对手，AIPAC照样在她身上花了大钱。这笔钱买的不是一场胜负，是就职之后的一个电话。

$16 billion. That's what this midterm cycle has raised, with House and Senate ad spending topping $4 billion (source: The Intercept). The report uses the term super PAC. It sounds like a neighborhood mutual-aid society. It is an interface that wires capital directly into the legislative process.

Start with AI. The two leading super PAC networks, affiliated with OpenAI and Anthropic respectively, dropped $50.7 million on the primaries, then $8.5 million in the run-up to the general. The report frames this as a response to public backlash. Over the past year, American opinion on AI and data centers shifted fast, and the entire life cycle of these PACs — about a year — followed that shift. The move is honest: they are not persuading voters, they are waiting for the mood to pass.

The candidate performances are better still. In Michigan's 7th District, a Sunrise Movement co-founder is running as an anti-data-center candidate, and his Republican opponent has started wrapping himself in "local control" language to sound like a data-center skeptic too. Both candidates are racing to be the more pro-AI-regulation one. The money didn't vanish. It stepped backstage and let the candidates do the soft talking. Matt Sledge puts it precisely in the report: these PACs bet on safe seats, and the goal may simply be "to buy themselves a few friends in Congress," because sometimes a few people who can stop regulation are as good as people who can write it.

AIPAC walked the same road a few years earlier. In 2022 it went loud, giving directly to candidates. After 2024, as public sentiment turned and Gaza made the pro-Israel lobby politically toxic, it retreated to its old business: no official endorsements, no direct contributions, money routed through dark-money channels and other groups. The report names a group called Elect Chicago Women; the money actually comes from AIPAC's super PAC, UDP. This is the standard weaponization move: seize the cognitive entry point, change the sign first, then change how traceable "who is paying" becomes.

Then there is the LA city attorney race almost nobody watches. Tech investors including John Hering are spending heavily for John McKinney against Marissa Roy, a DSA-backed candidate who helped carry out California's $17 billion settlement against Meta over harms to children. The report says no one can say for certain why these tech leaders care so much about a city attorney's office, but you can draw your own conclusion. McKinney is packaged as "tough on crime," as someone who will "restore order." Not a word about AI. Not a word about Meta.

This is the co-conspirators theory in the wild. No back room, no instructions, not even the candidate's knowledge required. The people paying know what they are buying, the people taking know who they are standing in for, and the voters in between see words like "order," "safety," "local control." Meta-violence doesn't need to announce itself. It only needs the question of who is paying to be hard enough to trace that enough people stop tracing.

The one place the report pauses is the note that the primaries did see real rejection of big-money spending. Right after it comes Maia Hibbett's judgment: no matter which party takes either or both chambers, many people elected next week will have gotten there on the backs of Big Tech and special interests. There is the gap. Voters can reject the performance. They cannot reject the structure.

Susan Collins had no primary challenger in Maine, and AIPAC spent heavily on her anyway. That money didn't buy a race. It bought a phone call after inauguration.

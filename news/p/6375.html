一首歌单里的男人宇宙Ten Songs, Ten Men, One Dressing Room

文娱 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
十首歌，十个男人，女人只在化妆间里出现
A mixtape of manhood: women appear only in the dressing room, secretly.

这份歌单里出现的女性，只有 Melba Moore 和 Heart 的《These Dreams》，后者还被他藏在化妆间里偷偷听。其余八首歌、八个名字，全是男人：Al Green、Stevie Wonder、Marvin Gaye、Barry White、Bill Withers、Tears for Fears、Afrika Bambaataa、Kashif。一个 MC 的诚实歌单，诚实到把自己的认知入口原样交出来了。

他说自己"记不住歌词"，但"每一个 drop 和 hi-hat 都记得"。这是一个把声音拆成零件的人，而歌词——语言，意义，叙事——"从来不是我的事"。这话放在一个以说唱为业的人嘴里，不奇怪：说唱的节奏和音色是可以技术化的，歌词是要承担表达的。承担表达意味着站队，意味着你是谁。他选择只处理前者。

歌单的排列方式本身就是一个叙事：父亲唱 Al Green，他以为父亲就是 Al Green；在 Bronx 听到 Planet Rock，他说"这他妈是什么"，被妈妈骂了一句脏话。两个场景里，音乐都不是作品，是父亲、是街区、是成为男人的入口。他把这套东西叫做"改变我一生"。

然后是性。"最好的做爱歌曲"——Barry White，或者《Let's Get It On》。他补了一句："我用我自己的音乐做过爱吗？当然没有。"这句话是整份歌单里最诚实的一句，也是最短的一句。他知道自己的音乐不是干这个用的，他知道做爱的时候要放别人的声音。

最有意思的是《I Love Every Little Thing About You》。他哭，是因为 Stevie Wonder 对另一个人说：我把你放在上帝之下，别的东西都越不过我对你的爱。他形容这个为"爱"。这就是第三章那个浪漫爱入口的标准样本：把一个人放在神的位置下面一格，然后称之为最高级的感情。他哭得很真诚，真诚到没有察觉这句话里的等级。

整份歌单里，唯一一个被明确赋予"神圣性"的位置，是他自己的葬礼——放《I Am I Be》。他给自己留了一首歌，一首关于"我是我"的歌。这是整篇采访里唯一一次他谈论自己是谁，而他选择的方式是：不解释，放歌。

采访者问的是"歌"，他答的全是"人"。这不是他的错，这是这个行业训练出来的表达方式：男性音乐人之间的关系网就是作品本身，女人是曲目，男人是作者。Melba Moore 那首歌，他记住的是制作人 Kashif；Heart 那首歌，他记住的是"大头发年代"。两首歌，两个女人的声音，都被他翻译成了男人的手艺和一个年代的气味。

他说他不想点名哪首歌他听不下去了，"怕他们来打我"。这是整篇里唯一一次他表现出对他人反应的在意。他在意同行的拳头，不在意自己歌单里缺席的那一半人。

The only women in this playlist are Melba Moore and Heart's "These Dreams" — and the latter he plays in his dressing room when he thinks no one is listening. The other eight songs, eight names, are all men: Al Green, Stevie Wonder, Marvin Gaye, Barry White, Bill Withers, Tears for Fears, Afrika Bambaataa, Kashif. An MC's honest playlist, honest enough to hand over his cognitive entry points intact.

He says he can't remember lyrics, but he remembers "every drop and hi-hat." This is a man who takes sound apart into components, and lyrics — language, meaning, narrative — "have never been a big thing for me." Coming from a rapper, this isn't strange: rhythm and timbre can be technicalized, lyrics have to carry expression. Carrying expression means taking a side, means saying who you are. He chooses to handle only the former.

The way the list is arranged is itself a narrative: his father singing Al Green, and he thought his father was Al Green; hearing Planet Rock in the Bronx, saying "what the fuck is this," getting scolded by his mom for cursing. In both scenes, music isn't a work, it's a father, a block, an entry point into manhood. He calls this "the song that changed my life."

Then sex. "The best song to have sex to" — Barry White, or "Let's Get It On." He adds: "Have I ever tried making love to my own music? Hell no." This is the most honest sentence in the whole playlist, and the shortest. He knows his music isn't for that. He knows that when making love, you play someone else's voice.

The most interesting one is "I Love Every Little Thing About You." He tears up because Stevie Wonder tells another person: I put you below God, and nothing tops my love for you. He calls this "love." This is the standard sample from Chapter 3's romantic-love entry point: put a person one notch below God, then call it the highest form of feeling. He cries sincerely, so sincerely he doesn't notice the hierarchy in the sentence.

The only position explicitly granted "sacredness" in the entire playlist is his own funeral — playing "I Am I Be." He saves himself one song, a song about "I am I." This is the only time in the whole interview he talks about who he is, and the way he chooses to do it is: don't explain, play the song.

The interviewer asks about "songs," he answers entirely with "people." This isn't his fault; it's the mode of expression this industry trains: the network between male musicians is the work itself, women are tracks, men are authors. That Melba Moore song — what he remembers is producer Kashif. That Heart song — what he remembers is "the big hair era." Two songs, two women's voices, both translated by him into men's craft and the smell of a decade.

He says he doesn't want to name a song he can't listen to anymore, "in case they come and beat me up." This is the only moment in the piece where he shows concern for others' reactions. He cares about his peers' fists. He doesn't care about the half of humanity missing from his playlist.

「我一生都是那场试镜」My Whole Life Has Been the Audition

文娱 文化层 · 元暴力 Billboard ↗ 2026-11-02 § 链接
新闻摘要杰克·希尔斯（Jake Shears）在百老汇复排版《洛基恐怖秀》中接演弗兰克-N-福特博士，9月11日首演，接替保罗·索莱奥，此前卢克·埃文斯曾凭此角获托尼奖提名。首演距蒂姆·柯里去世（8月25日，80岁）仅数周，柯里是1973年西区原版和1975年电影版该角色的扮演者。希尔斯受访谈自己12岁起痴迷该片、观众互动台词，以及柯里去世对他准备角色的影响。
The newsJake Shears has taken over as Dr. Frank-N-Furter in the Broadway revival of The Rocky Horror Show, debuting Sept. 11 after Paul Soileau and Luke Evans, who was Tony-nominated. His debut came weeks after Tim Curry, who originated the role in 1973 and on film in 1975, died Aug. 25 at 80. Shears discussed discovering the show at 12 and Curry's death.
他十二岁就选定了自己，四十八岁才拿到那个角色。
He chose himself at twelve and got the part at forty-eight.

希尔斯说「我这一辈子都是那场试镜」。这句话被当成玩笑，其实是他整段访谈里最准确的一句。十二三岁在姐姐帮忙下偷看那盘VHS，是他第一次给自己投票；四十八岁站上Studio 54的舞台，是那票终于兑现。表达决定你是谁，而他从十三岁起就没改过答案。

值得拆的是「试镜」这个词。他没试镜。制片人朋友打电话问要不要递号码，他说要，然后守着这件事不敢声张，怕落空后自己受不了。也就是说，真正在起作用的是他过去三十几年持续表达出来的那个身份——一个公开的、夸张的、把酷儿气质当作主场而非伪装的人——被行业认出来了。机会不是考出来的，是长出来的。这比任何励志故事都更接近机制：你要先成为那个能被认出来的人。

弗兰克这个角色本身是表达史上的一个异数。1973年西区、1975年电影，一个来自Transsexual Transylvania的「甜蜜异装者」，在主流舞台上把性别表演当众拆开给观众看，还让人跟着唱。它不道德、不解释、不道歉，希尔斯说它「有种无道德感」，这正是它五十年没被驯化的原因。后来的复排一旦把它做成致敬，就死了；这次用DIY布景、拼盘卡司和玩闹的坎普把它重新点着，方向是对的。

柯里去世后，三位前后任弗兰克同台唱《I'm Going Home》。希尔斯说那首歌对他像一首酷儿圣歌，唱的时候几乎每次都动情。这段是真的。但也要看清：一个角色被几代男演员接续扮演、被观众当作仪式反复观看，这套结构里，弗兰克的身体始终是被观看、被消费、被定价的那一方。柯里把它演成了不可复制的神话，神话的代价是后来者永远在影子里。希尔斯很清楚这一点，他不回避唱柯里的版本，说「逃不掉」。

观众互动那一段，他提了唯一的要求：别业余。要玩就玩得自信、清楚、知道自己在干什么。这话听着像玩笑，其实是整篇访谈里最不客气的判断——他把剧场的解释权分了一半给观众，但只分给懂规矩的那一半。仪式要成立，靠的不是热情，是节拍。

他四十八岁才演弗兰克，还说「我不确定三十二岁能演好」。这句话里藏着一个不太被谈论的事实：这个角色需要的不是年轻的身体，是被规训过、又亲手把规训拆掉的人。他先演了扣到脖子的查理，又演了Cabaret的主持人，才敢碰弗兰克。表达是练出来的，不是天生的。

真正没被回答的是：一个十二岁在VHS里找到「我们会在这里」这句话的孩子，为什么要在三十五年后才等到舞台中央的位置。这中间的三十五年，行业给了多少和他一样的人同样的电话，又没给多少人。

Shears says his whole life has been the audition. It lands as a joke, but it is the most accurate line in the interview. Watching that VHS at twelve or thirteen, smuggled in by his sister, was the first vote he cast for himself. Standing on the Studio 54 stage at forty-eight is that vote finally cashing out. Expression decides who you are, and he never changed his answer.

What deserves scrutiny is the word audition. He did not audition. A producer friend called to ask whether to pass along his number; he said yes and then kept the whole thing quiet, because he could be devastated if it fell through. What was actually working was the identity he had expressed for three decades — a public, theatrical, queer-by-default presence that the industry could recognize. The opportunity was not earned in a room. It grew.

Frank-N-Furter is an anomaly in the history of expression. West End 1973, film 1975: a sweet transvestite from Transsexual Transylvania who takes gender performance apart in front of a paying audience and gets them to sing along. It is amoral, unexplained, unapologetic — Shears calls it amoral himself, which is exactly why it has not been domesticated in fifty years. Revivals die the moment they become tributes. DIY sets, hodgepodge casting and playful camp are the right direction.

After Curry died, the three men who have played Frank took the stage together for I'm Going Home. Shears calls it a queer hymn and says he gets emotional nearly every time. That part is real. But look at the structure: a role passed between generations of male performers, watched as ritual by an audience, with Frank's body always the thing on display, consumed and priced. Curry turned it into an unrepeatable myth, and the price of the myth is that everyone after him stands in the shadow. Shears knows; he does not dodge Curry's versions of the songs. There is no escaping that.

On audience callbacks he makes one request: don't be an amateur. If you play with me, play confidently, clearly, know what you're doing. It sounds like banter. It is the least polite judgment in the piece — he hands half the interpretive authority to the audience, but only to the half that knows the beat. A ritual works on timing, not enthusiasm.

He waited until forty-eight and doubts a thirty-two-year-old could play Frank. Buried in that is a fact nobody markets: the role does not need a young body. It needs someone who has been disciplined and has personally taken the discipline apart. He played buttoned-up Charlie, then the Emcee, before he dared touch Frank. Expression is trained, not innate.

The unanswered question: a kid who found the message we're out here in a VHS tape at twelve had to wait thirty-five years for the center of the stage. In those thirty-five years, how many others like him got the same phone call — and how many did not.

万圣节扮装，扮的是谁的自由Halloween Costumes, and Whose Freedom They Dress Up

文娱 文化层 Deadline ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
新闻摘要格蕾西·艾布拉姆斯在《周六夜现场》万圣节特辑中担任音乐嘉宾，两场表演分别装扮成巴兹·鲁赫曼1996年电影《罗密欧与朱丽叶》中莱昂纳多·迪卡普里奥和克莱尔·丹妮丝的角色。她先演唱《Hit the Wall》，后演唱《Here You Come, There You Go》。这是她继2024年首秀后第二次担任该节目音乐嘉宾。
The newsGracie Abrams served as musical guest on Saturday Night Live's Halloween episode, dressing as Leonardo DiCaprio and Claire Danes' characters from Baz Luhrmann's 1996 Romeo + Juliet for her two performances. She sang "Hit the Wall" and "Here You Come, There You Go." It marked her second SNL musical guest stint after her 2024 debut.
两套戏服轮换，表达被定价成一场cosplay
Two costumes in one night: expression priced as cosplay.

Gracie Abrams 在 SNL 一晚上换了两套衣服，都是 Baz Luhrmann 那版《罗密欧与朱丽叶》里的角色：先穿 Claire Danes 的天使装唱《Hit the Wall》，再套 Leonardo DiCaprio 那件热带印花衬衫配金属袖唱《Here You Come, There You Go》。报道把它写成 2-for-1 costume special，一次演出，两份 Halloween 红利。

一个人在同一晚既扮朱丽叶又扮罗密欧，这件事本身不新鲜。新鲜的是它被当成卖点写进通稿：表演者的表达被压缩成可识别的 IP 符号，观众不需要知道她唱什么，只需要认得出那件衬衫。第三章讲的认知入口在这里运作得很轻巧——不是谁在骗你，是整套产业把“你扮谁”变成了比“你是谁”更好卖的东西。

更值得看一眼的是那套天使装。Danes 的朱丽叶是 1996 年那版里被拍得最像圣像的角色：白裙、翅膀、水族箱前的柔光。Abrams 从水族箱后面开口唱第一句，等于把“被观看的纯洁少女”这个位置原样搬上舞台。她唱的是自己写的歌，站的是别人替她定好的坐标。

这不是说她不该扮。问题在于，当 Halloween 的扮装成为女艺人展示“可塑性”的标准动作，可塑性本身就成了一种规训：你要证明你能变成任何人，唯独不必证明你是谁。第二章那个假.最优解表达在这里很直白——扮演一个所有人都认得的角色，短期收益是话题度，代价是表达被让渡给了 IP。

报道没写的是，这两套衣服背后谁在定价。造型团队、唱片公司、SNL 的 Halloween 档期，每一环都在把她的身体当成展示柜。她自己在这场交易里拿到的，是一次被记住的机会；被记住的却不是她。

朱丽叶和罗密欧都死了，死因是两家人的姓氏。Abrams 一晚上把两个姓氏都穿在身上，唱完就脱。真正没脱下来的，是那套“女艺人必须可被任意装扮”的戏服。

Gracie Abrams changed clothes twice on SNL, and both were characters from Baz Luhrmann's Romeo + Juliet: first Claire Danes' angel costume for "Hit the Wall," then Leonardo DiCaprio's tropical shirt with the metal sleeves for "Here You Come, There You Go." Deadline calls it a 2-for-1 costume special. One performance, two Halloween payouts.

A person playing Juliet and Romeo in the same night is not new. What is new is that it gets written into the press release as the selling point: the performer's expression compressed into recognizable IP symbols, so the audience doesn't need to know what she's singing, only that they recognize the shirt. This is the cognitive entrance from Chapter 3 operating quietly. Nobody is lying to you. The industry has simply made "who you dress as" easier to sell than "who you are."

The angel costume deserves a second look. Danes' Juliet is the most icon-like figure in that 1996 film: white dress, wings, soft light behind an aquarium. Abrams opens her first song from behind that aquarium, which is to say she steps into the exact position of the watched, pure girl. She sings her own songs from a coordinate someone else set for her.

This isn't an argument that she shouldn't dress up. The problem is that when Halloween cosplay becomes the standard move for a female artist to demonstrate range, range itself becomes discipline: prove you can become anyone, and you never have to prove who you are. The fake optimal expression from Chapter 2 is plain here. Playing a role everyone recognizes pays in buzz; the cost is that expression gets handed over to the IP.

What the report doesn't say is who is pricing those two outfits. Stylists, label, SNL's Halloween slot: each link treats her body as a display case. What she gets out of the trade is a chance to be remembered. What gets remembered isn't her.

Juliet and Romeo both die, and the cause is a family name. Abrams wore both names for one night and took them off after the song. What she didn't take off is the costume that says a female artist must be dressable as anything.

谁在把抗议者写成暴徒Who Gets to Call Protesters a Mob

国际 文化层 · 结构层 The New York Times ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
把面具定为犯罪，就是给示威者提前定罪。
Criminalizing masks is convicting demonstrators in advance.

法国政府最担心的不是暴力，是面具。努涅斯说，示威中遮脸是刑事犯罪。这条禁令的潜台词很清楚：先把抗议者定义成潜在的罪犯，再拿这个定义去执法。

报道里写得很清楚，巴黎的游行大部分时间是节庆式的，铜管乐队在演奏，有人从阳台上撒糖，人群欢呼。打扮成“超级父母”的家长海伦·奥利芙说得很直白：十月十七日那场骚乱被极右翼拿去做了文章，她们想把叙事扳回来。二十岁的文学系学生阿蒂尔·弗勒里扮成骑恐龙的囚犯，戴着勒庞的面具，说抗议既要提要求也要好玩。

这些人不是暴徒。但官方叙事需要他们看起来像暴徒。

于是有了那套说法：社交媒体上有人号召“革命”“起义”，所以集会一律禁止，违者逮捕。内政部下午两点统计六十五场抗议共一万五千人，同一时间点上周是三万七千，十月十七日是六万七千；到晚上全国三十二万人，上周四十一万。数字在缩小，叙事却在升级。

再看另一条被埋在中间的细节：官员确认，与极右翼有关联的年轻男性团体也混进了示威，打着“保护店主”的旗号，常常和极左翼发生冲突。政府把大部分混乱归咎于极左，这条却只是顺带一提。谁在制造暴力，谁在解释暴力，两件事被写成了同一件事。

真正在动的是文化层。一场关于教室太挤、校舍破败、燃油太贵的抗议，被重新包装成“公共秩序”问题。教育部长说学生的部分诉求“合理”，但谴责他们对抗的方式；内政部长谈的是禁令、逮捕、七十万警力。诉求被承认，行动被定罪，这是最经典的分化手法：给你一个“合理”的标签，同时拿走你上街的资格。

代价是具体的。一名年轻女性面部流血，被医护人员围住；警察向人群冲锋；居民捂着口鼻跑回家躲催泪瓦斯。报道没有写她是谁、为什么流血。这个空白本身就是材料。

还有一层没人追问：马克龙否认政府考虑过暂停几个流行平台，但他在 YouTube 采访里说，平台将被要求“删除非法内容”，并“对某些功能的使用保持高度警惕”。勒科努是否真的致信电信运营商要求准备封锁 Snapchat、TikTok 和 Instagram，总理办公室拒绝回应信件的真实性，四个收信方里两个拒绝评论，一个没回，唯一国家持股的 Orange 既不确认也不否认。认知入口正在被讨论如何上锁，而讨论本身被处理成了传闻。

把面具定为犯罪，等于宣布：你可以有诉求，但不能有脸。

What the French government fears is not violence. It is masks. Laurent Nuñez says covering your face at a demonstration is a criminal offense. The subtext is plain: define protesters as potential criminals first, then enforce against the definition.

The report is clear that Paris was festive for most of the day. Brass bands played. Someone dropped candy from a balcony and the crowd cheered. Helen Olive, 50, dressed as a "super parent," said the riots of Oct. 17 had been instrumentalized by the far right and she wanted to turn that narrative around. Arthur Fleury, 20, a literature student, came as a prisoner riding a dinosaur wearing a Marine Le Pen mask: protests are about demands, he said, but also about having fun.

These people are not a mob. But the official narrative needs them to look like one.

Hence the line: social media calls for "revolution" and "insurrection," so gatherings are banned and anyone who shows up gets arrested. At 2 p.m. the interior ministry counted 15,000 people across 65 protests, against 37,000 at the same hour last week and 67,000 on Oct. 17. By evening, 320,000 nationwide, down from 410,000. The numbers shrink; the narrative escalates.

Then a detail buried mid-story: officials confirmed that bands of young men linked to the far right had also infiltrated the demonstrations, using the pretext of protecting shop owners and often clashing with the far left. The government blames the far left for most of the mayhem. This line gets one sentence. Who produces violence and who gets to explain it are written as the same thing.

What is moving here is the cultural layer. A protest about overcrowded classrooms, crumbling schools and fuel prices is repackaged as a question of public order. The education minister calls some student demands "legitimate" while condemning their confrontational methods; the interior minister talks bans, arrests, 70,000 officers. Concede the demand, criminalize the act. It is the oldest split: hand out a "legitimate" label and take away the right to show up.

The costs are concrete. A young woman bleeding from the face, surrounded by medics. Police charging the crowd. Residents covering their faces to run home through tear gas. The report does not say who she was or why she bled. That blank is itself material.

And one layer nobody pressed: Macron denied that the government considered suspending popular platforms, then said on YouTube that platforms must "remove illegal content" and be "very vigilant about the use of certain features." Whether Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu sent telecom operators a letter asking them to prepare to block Snapchat, TikTok and Instagram, his office declined to confirm. Two recipients declined to comment, one did not respond, and Orange, the only state-owned one, would neither confirm nor deny. The cognitive entry point is being discussed as a lock to be fitted, and the discussion itself is handled as rumor.

Make masks a crime and you have announced the rule: you may have demands, but not a face.

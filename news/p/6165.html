反抗的剧本里，谁在演主角Who Plays the Lead in the Rebellion Script

国际 结构层 · 元暴力 The New Republic ↗ 2026-10-31 § 链接
新闻摘要2027年1月3日，民主党在11月大胜后即将掌控众议院，但议长约翰逊撤换无党派书记员，新书记员以选举舞弊为由拒收关键选区民主党胜选认证文件，当选议员无法就职。民主党全国委员会诉至最高法院，罗伯茨多数派以「政治问题原则」裁定不予干预。
The newsOn January 3, 2027, Democrats were set to take the House majority after their November sweep, but Speaker Johnson replaced the nonpartisan House clerk, who refused to certify Democratic wins in key races, blocking members-elect from taking their seats. The Supreme Court's Roberts majority declined to intervene, citing the political question doctrine.
他们写好了反抗剧本，却只把人民当群众演员
They wrote the resistance playbook and cast the people as extras.

这篇东西读起来像一份战术手册，但它真正在做的，是把"人民"这两个字提前征用。

作者说特朗普不是无所不能的神，没有人民同意独裁者就统治不下去——这话没错，加尔通的暴力三角就建立在这上面：现状低于本可达到的状态，差额就是暴力，而差额靠的是被统治者持续供给的顺从。问题在于，谁有权定义"人民"、谁有权宣布同意被撤回、谁有权组织那场撤回。文章给出的答案很干脆：政治学家、几十年的研究、塞尔维亚的Otpor!、明尼阿波利斯的活动组织者。人民在里面是变量，不是主语。

第二步尤其值得盯住：把镇压当作招募工具，让暴行引发的愤怒自己发酵。Otpor!这么干成了，明尼阿波利斯也这么干成了——文章自己写，两名抵抗者Alex Pretti和Renee Good被杀的惨剧成了临界点，愤怒逼得一位共和党州长候选人退选、共和党议员公开反对、Greg Bovino被赶走、Kristi Noem被解除行动责任并最终免职。这些名字和职位在文章里是"胜利"的注脚。可那两个人的死，在叙事里被处理成了一次成功的杠杆。杠杆不会痛。

再往下看，这套四步法有一个从不被写进步骤表的前提：谁承担代价。总罢工让国家停摆，停摆的账单落在没有储蓄的人头上；公民志愿者上街监视联邦探员，站在无标记面包车前面的是肉身，不是论文作者。文章说暴力会削弱公众支持、会破坏"反噬效应"，所以必须保持非暴力纪律——这个判断可能是对的，但纪律的受益者是运动本身，付账的是站在最前排的人。存在性战争里，最优解表达从来不是均摊的。

还有一层更安静的共谋：整篇文章的敌人是一个具体的、有名字的独裁者，而它调用的合法性资源是"民主""宪法""选举"这些看起来中性的词。可2020年那套推翻选举的操作，靠的是各州议会的党派多数、是法院的"政治问题原则"、是党机器里一层层愿意配合的人。这些结构在特朗普之前就存在，在他之后也不会自动消失。把问题定位成"一个冷血独裁者"，等于把元暴力降格成个人品性问题——换个人上台，机器照转。文章自己都写了：凭什么指望另一个共和党提名人更尊重民主进程，包括JD Vance。写到这里它停住了，没有往下问那台机器是谁造的。

最刺眼的是那个开头的假设场景：2027年1月3日，众议院书记员拒绝认证民主党当选人，最高法院援引政治问题原则不予干涉，共和党就这样偷走了中期选举。这是一份精确的、可信的推演。但它通篇没有出现一个普通选民的名字，没有出现一个在投票站排队的、被剥夺了代表权的人。被偷走选举的人在这篇文章里是抽象的"选民意志"，而组织反抗的人是具体的、有训练手册的、有外国成功案例可参照的。

这不是说文章呼吁的反抗是假的。非暴力反抗确实推翻过独裁者，明尼阿波利斯的事也确实发生过。但一份把人民写成工具、把死亡写成临界点、把纪律写成美德的路线图，本身就在做它声称要对抗的事：替别人决定什么代价值得付。

塞尔维亚那场运动赢了，米洛舍维奇下台了。文章没写的是，Otpor!后来被谁资助、被谁收编、那些训练手册的钱从哪来。这不是阴谋论，这是一个标准问题：一场以"人民"为名的运动，如果它的组织能力来自外部资金和外国顾问，它还是人民的吗。文章把Otpor!当成范本，却对范本的账本只字不提——这恰恰是它最像它所反对的那套东西的地方。

所以这篇东西最该被追问的不是"四步法管不管用"，而是：当反抗被写成一份可复制的操作手册，写手册的人坐在哪里，读手册的人站在哪里。答案通常不在一页纸上。

This reads like a tactical manual, but what it's really doing is requisitioning the word "people" in advance.

The author says Trump is not an omnipotent demigod, that no dictator rules without the people's consent—true enough. Galtung's Violence Triangle rests on exactly this: the gap between potential and actual is violence, and that gap is sustained by the governed continuously supplying their compliance. The question is who gets to define "the people," who gets to declare consent withdrawn, and who gets to organize the withdrawal. The article's answer is clean: political scientists, decades of research, Serbia's Otpor!, Minneapolis organizers. The people are a variable in there, not the subject.

Step two deserves special attention: use repression as a recruitment tool, let the outrage from atrocities ferment on its own. Otpor! did it, Minneapolis did it too—the article itself writes that the tragic killings of two resisters, Alex Pretti and Renee Good, proved to be a tipping point, and the anger forced a GOP gubernatorial candidate to drop out, Republican members of Congress to speak out, Greg Bovino to be shown the door, Kristi Noem to be relieved of operational responsibility and eventually sacked. Those names and titles are footnotes to "victory" in this text. But those two deaths get processed as a successful lever. Levers don't feel pain.

Look further down and this four-step method has a premise that never makes it into the step list: who bears the cost. A general strike brings the country to a halt, and the bill for the halt lands on people without savings; citizen-volunteers go out to monitor federal agents, and it's flesh standing in front of unmarked vans, not the paper's author. The article says violence would sap public support and break the backfire effect, so nonviolent discipline is essential—that judgment may be correct, but the beneficiary of discipline is the movement itself, and the one paying is the person at the front of the line. In an existential war, the optimal expression is never shared equally.

There's a quieter complicity too: the article's enemy is one specific, named dictator, while its legitimacy comes from neutral-sounding words like "democracy," "constitution," "election." But the 2020 scheme to overturn the election ran on partisan majorities in state legislatures, on the Supreme Court's "political question doctrine," on layer after layer of party machinery willing to play along. Those structures existed before Trump and won't vanish after him. Framing the problem as "one cold-blooded autocrat" downgrades meta-violence to a matter of personal character—swap in someone else and the machine keeps running. The article itself writes: why expect a different nominee to respect the democratic process, including JD Vance. It stops right there and never asks who built the machine.

The most glaring thing is the opening hypothetical: January 3, 2027, the House clerk refuses to certify Democratic winners, the Supreme Court invokes the political question doctrine and declines to interfere, and the GOP steals the midterms. It's a precise, credible projection. But not a single ordinary voter's name appears in it, not one person standing in line at a polling station, stripped of representation. The people whose election was stolen are an abstract "will of the voters" here, while the people organizing resistance are concrete, with training manuals and foreign success stories to cite.

None of this means the call to resistance is fake. Nonviolent rebellion has toppled dictators, and Minneapolis did happen. But a roadmap that writes the people as instruments, deaths as tipping points, and discipline as virtue is itself doing what it claims to oppose: deciding for others which costs are worth paying.

That movement in Serbia won, Milošević fell. What the article doesn't write is who later funded Otpor!, who absorbed it, where the money for those training manuals came from. That's not conspiracy theory, it's a standard question: if a movement in the people's name gets its organizational capacity from external funding and foreign consultants, is it still the people's? The article holds Otpor! up as a model while saying nothing about the model's books—which is precisely where it most resembles what it opposes.

So the question worth asking isn't "does the four-step method work," but: when resistance gets written as a replicable operations manual, where does the person writing the manual sit, and where does the person reading it stand. The answer is usually not on the page.

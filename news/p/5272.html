芝士汉堡汤：被量化的家庭劳作与中产阶级的“真实感”骗局Cheeseburger Soup: Quantified Domestic Labor and the Bourgeois Illusion of 'Real Food'

性别 结构层 · 文化层 The New York Times ↗ 2026-09-26 § 链接
食谱不是烹饪指南，而是对家庭无偿劳动的标准化管理手册。
Recipes are not cooking guides, but standardized management manuals for unpaid domestic labor.

一份来自《纽约时报》的芝士汉堡汤食谱，表面上是提供某种“舒适食物” (comfort food) 的生活方式指南，本质上是一次典型的 structural violence。它将复杂的家庭劳作被简化为 35 分钟的量化指令，试图掩盖一个事实：在绝大多数家庭结构中，执行这些 Step 1 到 Step 5 的主体依然是女性。

这种表达的武器化在于它制造了一种“真实感” (authenticity) 的 scam。评论区里那些讨论“减少黄油”、“用鸡汤替代牛肉汤”的对话，实际上是在一个被预设的男性中心叙事框架内进行微小的博弈。她们在追求一种“像真实食物一样” (tasted like a hearty soup made of real food) 的口感，但却忽略了这种“真实”是建立在对女性时间与体力的无偿榨取之上的。当劳作被包装成“爱”或“家庭乐趣”时，这种 structural violence 就变成了 cultural violence，让执行者在自我规训中感到满足。

最讽刺的是，这种食谱通过精准的量化（1 pound ground beef, 5 tablespoons butter）夺取了厨房的解释权。它定义了什么是“好汤”，将烹饪从一种基于直觉和身体经验的表达，变成了对工业化标准（如 Cheddar 奶酪的克数）的服从。这不仅是味觉的殖民，更是对家庭内部权力关系的再一次确认：谁在阅读指令，谁在执行指令，谁在享受结果。

好新闻？不，这只是一个精致的 PR 版本。它让人们在追求“中产阶级生活品质”的幻象中，心甘情愿地成为维持既定秩序的共谋者。

A cheeseburger soup recipe from The New York Times appears to be a lifestyle guide for 'comfort food,' but it is essentially an act of structural violence. By reducing complex domestic labor into 35 minutes of quantified instructions, it masks a stark reality: the primary subject executing Steps 1 through 5 remains, in the vast majority of family structures, the woman.

The weaponisation of this expression lies in manufacturing a scam of 'authenticity.' The comment section, where users discuss 'reducing butter' or 'subbing chicken broth,' is merely a minor game of stakes within a pre-set masculine-centric narrative. They strive for a taste that feels like 'real food,' while ignoring that this 'reality' is built upon the unpaid extraction of women's time and physical energy. When labor is packaged as 'love' or 'family joy,' structural violence evolves into cultural violence, leading the executor to feel satisfaction through internalised discipline.

Most ironically, this recipe seizes the right of interpretation in the kitchen through precise quantification. It defines what a 'good soup' is, transforming cooking from an expression based on intuition and bodily experience into an act of obedience to industrial standards. This is not just a colonization of taste, but a reaffirmation of the power dynamics within the home: who reads the instructions, who executes them, and who consumes the result.

Good news? Hardly. This is just a polished PR version of domesticity, encouraging people to become complicit in maintaining the status quo while chasing the illusion of 'middle-class quality of life.'

猫的狩猎，人的家务The Cat's Hunt, the Human's Chores

其他 结构层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要《卫报》一篇生活专栏写作者 lockdown 期间养的两只猫 Mimi 和 Ruben 把屋外的木鼠活着叼回家再放掉，一家人只好自己拿塑料盒抓鼠、用花生酱设人道陷阱，作者还被一只救过三次的老鼠咬伤、打了破伤风加强针。后来冰箱开始发臭，作者挪开冰箱，发现背面接冷凝水的槽被这些被迫住进屋里的老鼠当成了厕所，清理耗时数小时。
The newsA Guardian lifestyle column recounts how the writer's two lockdown kittens, Mimi and Ruben, kept bringing live wood mice indoors and releasing them, leaving the family to catch the mice themselves with plastic tubs and peanut-butter humane traps; the writer was bitten by one rescued mouse and needed a tetanus booster. Later a smell behind the fridge led to the discovery that mice had been using its condensation trough as a toilet, which took hours to clean.
猫把猎物带回家，是因为家是它的巢，不是你的。
The cat brought prey home because home is its nest, not yours.

猫把老鼠活着叼回来、放掉，再蹲在踢脚线边上发出那串「prittering」的声音——这不是捣乱，是猫在向巢穴交付猎物。屋子对它来说是巢，对你来说是家。两套定义撞在一起，输的永远是弯腰拿塑料盒的那个。

所以这一篇里真正的暴力不在猫，也不在被咬的那根手指。人在这里承担的是全部的劳动和全部的代价：挪家具、跪在地上追、被咬、打针、吸冰箱后面那槽液体。猫付出的代价是零，还顺便完成了它作为猫的表达。这不算剥削，只是提醒你，把另一个物种请进家门的时候，你签的是一份你根本没读过的合同。

真正有意思的是作者的自我定位。她写「我不怪老鼠——它们没有选择入侵我们」，又写自己觉得老鼠可爱，还试图用一张厨房纸巾和一只救过三次的老鼠「建立联系」。这套叙事很熟悉：把结构性的麻烦翻译成一段可以讲述的感情，再把感情写成文章。老鼠咬她那一口，是这段叙事唯一一次被现实打断的地方。

更值得看的是谁从头到尾没有说话。老鼠被叼进来、被放掉、被关在屋里、被逼着在冰箱背后的水槽里排泄，最后被作者用「恶心」和「恶臭」描述。整篇报道里，老鼠只有一种表达：咬。而这一咬被写成不知感恩。

至于那槽液体，它其实是整件事最诚实的一处证据：这些动物被迫住进一个不属于它们的空间，于是它们在那个空间最边缘、最没人看的角落留下了自己的痕迹。人花几个小时用厨房纸把它吸干，然后说，谢天谢地我们不再住在树林边了。

The cats carried live mice back and let them go, then sat guarding the skirting board making that odd prittering sound. That is not mischief. That is a cat delivering prey to its nest. The house is a nest to them and a home to you. When two definitions collide, the one who loses is always the one on her hands and knees with a Tupperware box.

So the violence in this piece is not in the cats, and not in the bitten finger. The human here absorbs all the labour and all the cost: moving furniture, crawling on the floor, getting bitten, getting the booster, soaking up the trough behind the fridge. The cats pay nothing and complete their feline expression along the way. This is not exploitation. It is a reminder that when you invite another species into your home, you sign a contract you never read.

What is actually interesting is the writer's self-positioning. She does not blame the mice, since they did not choose to invade; she finds mice adorable; she tries to bond with one she has rescued three times, using a piece of kitchen towel. The narrative is familiar: translate a structural nuisance into a story about feelings, then publish the feelings. The bite is the one moment reality interrupts the story.

More telling is who never speaks. The mice are carried in, released, shut indoors, driven to defecate in a trough behind a fridge, and finally described as disgusting and putrid. Across the whole piece the mice have exactly one form of expression: the bite. And the bite is written up as ingratitude.

That trough is the most honest evidence in the story. Animals forced to live in a space that is not theirs leave their mark in its most marginal, least-watched corner. The human spends hours absorbing it with kitchen roll, then says thank God we no longer live next to woods.

蛋糕粉进Target，卖的是妈妈人设Target Sells the Mom, Not the Mix

文娱 文化层 CNBC ↗ 2026-11-03 § 链接
新闻摘要Baked by Melissa 与 Target 达成独家合作，从上周日起在 Target 门店销售其三款纸杯蛋糕预拌粉套装、纸托和烤盘，这是该品牌首次进入家庭烘焙货架。创始人 Melissa Ben-Ishay 称，一年前带孩子逛 Target 时决定做这件事。Target 则希望借此在去年假日季销售不佳后重新吸引核心顾客。
The newsBaked by Melissa has struck an exclusive deal with Target to sell three cupcake mix kits, liners and a cupcake pan in its stores starting Sunday, the brand's first move into at-home baking. Founder Melissa Ben-Ishay said she decided to do it a year ago while shopping at Target with her kids. Target hopes the line will help win back its core shoppers after a disappointing holiday season last year.
她不是在卖蛋糕粉，是在卖一个能进Target的妈妈。
She isn't selling cupcake mix. She's selling a mother Target can shelve.

这条新闻里真正的产品不是纸杯蛋糕粉，是「42岁、两个孩子、常逛 Target 的妈妈」这个人设。Ben-Ishay 自己把这句话说得很清楚：我们先进 Target，因为我是个 42 岁的两娃妈，我老在那儿。一个品牌拿创始人的身份当货架通行证，这是表达被武器化的标准动作——不是产品质量在谈判，是「我跟你家核心顾客长得一模一样」在谈判。

Target 这边更直白。报道说它去年假日季令人失望，现在要把核心顾客「赢回来」，而那个典型顾客「看起来很像 Ben-Ishay」。所以货架上摆的其实是一次身份采购：Target 买下一个能代表它目标客群的女人，让她替自己站在烘焙区，告诉路过的妈妈们「这里还是你的地方」。

Ben-Ishay 的 TikTok 有 280 万粉丝，靠的是菜谱视频，绿女神沙拉酱那种。这套路径现在是标准配置：先在社交平台攒出可被信任的日常表达，再把这个信任批发给零售商。她跟 Ferrero、Wonder 的合作也是同一件事的预演。粉丝关注的是一个会做饭的普通妈妈，最后兑现的是货架位置和联名合同。第三章讲的认知入口，入口从来不在广告里，在「她跟我一样」这句话里。

这里没有谁被殴打，也没有哪条法律在扣谁的钱。动的是文化层：一个女性的家庭生活被提炼成可授权的品牌资产，她的孩子、她的购物车、她的年龄，全都变成渠道谈判里的筹码。她自己未必是受害者，她显然在主动经营这件事，赚到的也是她。但机制值得看清——能被卖进 Target 的「妈妈」，是那种恰好符合零售商想象的妈妈：有闲、有品味、有 280 万粉丝。货架上摆的不是所有母亲，是被挑选过的那一种。

至于烘焙本身，她说自己真心喜欢用预拌粉，有时候觉得味道更好。这句话可能是真的。一盒粉、一个烤盘、三个口味，节日季上架，十二个月的审批换两个月的销量窗口。整个链条里唯一没被定价的东西，是那些真的会在厨房里站一下午的人——她们买的是粉，付的是时间，收到的是一个「我也能做出这个」的错觉。

The actual product in this story isn't cupcake mix. It's the persona: a 42-year-old mother of two who shops at Target a lot. Ben-Ishay says it herself — they started with Target because that's who she is. A brand using its founder's identity as a shelf pass is the textbook weaponization of expression: what's negotiating isn't the product, it's the fact that she looks exactly like the retailer's core customer.

Target is even more explicit. The story says last holiday season disappointed, and now it wants its core shopper back — a shopper who "often looks a lot like Ben-Ishay." So what's on the shelf is an identity purchase: Target buys a woman who can stand in for its target demographic and tell the mothers walking past that this aisle is still theirs.

Her TikTok has 2.8 million followers, built on recipe videos. That's the standard pipeline now: accumulate trustworthy everyday expression on social media, then wholesale that trust to a retailer. Her Ferrero and Wonder deals were the rehearsal. Followers signed up for an ordinary mom who cooks; what got delivered was shelf space and licensing contracts. The cognitive entry point was never in an ad. It was in "she's like me."

No one is being beaten here, no law is docking anyone's pay. What moves is the cultural layer: a woman's domestic life gets refined into licensable brand equity — her kids, her cart, her age, all converted into leverage at the channel negotiation. She is not a victim; she is clearly running this and getting paid. But the mechanism is worth naming. The mother who can be sold into Target is the mother who happens to match the retailer's imagination: leisure, taste, 2.8 million followers. What's on the shelf isn't all mothers. It's the selected kind.

As for the baking, she says she genuinely loves cake mix and sometimes thinks it tastes better. That may be true. One box, one pan, three flavors, on shelves for the holidays — twelve months of approvals traded for a two-month sales window. The only thing in the chain that never gets priced is the person who will actually stand in the kitchen all afternoon: she buys the mix, pays with her time, and receives the illusion that she made it.

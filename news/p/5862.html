谁的工作才算工作Whose Job Counts as a Job

国际 结构层 · 文化层 The Guardian ↗ 2026-10-30 § 链接
被砍掉的岗位不配上头条，因为砍它们的人不用还债。
The cuts that vanish from headlines are the ones whose makers never pay for them.

Andrea Egan 说的是对的，但她说得还不够狠。北海油气工人拿到的是"massive political focus"，地方政府、NHS、教育系统里被砍掉的成千上万个岗位拿到的是"afterthought"。这不是关注度的分配不均，这是定价。同样是一份劳动，一份被写进预算预测、被首相级人物反复表态、被左右两派政客争相站台；另一份在预算案里只以削减数字的形式出现。谁的工作值得保，谁的工作可以消失，这个判断从来不由劳动本身做出。

看 Jackdaw 气田是怎么被批准的：化石燃料行业和右翼政客施压，政府放行，预算预测里直接把这个项目的批准算了进去。而与此同时，同一份预算里，地方政府的岗位在减少，NHS 的岗位在减少，教育的岗位在减少。这不是两个独立的故事，这是一个故事的两面——资源向一个方向集中，就必须从另一个方向抽走。抽走的那一头，恰好是照护、教育、护理助理，是那些主要由女性承担的、被默认为"本来就在那里"的劳动。

Unison 和 NEU 反对继续开采，Unite 和 GMB 支持多发许可证。工会之间的分裂被写成"能源转型的阵痛"，但真正的问题是：转型的成本由谁付。油气工人要的是有保障、薪资体面的岗位，这个诉求完全正当。可如果这个诉求的实现方式是继续挖，那么代价就落在气候上，也落在那些连"转型"都没被通知到的公共服务劳动者身上。Egan 提的替代方案——为油气工人创造可再生能源领域的有保障岗位——是唯一把两边放在同一张桌子上谈的路径。

Unison 和 Octopus Energy 达成的认可协议值得注意，但也要看清楚它是什么：一家公司选择和一个工会签协议，另一家工会（GMB）在同一家公司争取认可没成功。可再生能源岗位常常没有工会化，因此不如油气岗位稳定、薪资不如油气岗位体面——这是报道里写明的工会担忧。一份认可协议是进步，但它覆盖的是这家公司，不是这个行业。把"清洁能源工会"做成一个品牌，和把清洁能源岗位做成一个有集体谈判保障的行业，是两件事。

Rosebank 的最终决定还没下来，工党议员在游说不要推翻能源大臣。这才是真正的分水岭：如果连一个以"ceiling on ambition"自居的政府都在为新的油气开采找台阶，那么"public sector 是 afterthought"就不是疏忽，是排序。排序的背后是共谋——化石燃料行业、右翼政客、担心选票的工党议员，各自拿到自己想要的那一份，而照护工人、护理助理、助教拿到的是"我们理解你们的付出"。

Egan 说"是时候让政府把优先级理清楚了"。优先级从来都清楚，只是不写在新闻稿里。被砍掉的那些岗位不会出现在预算预测的加粗字体里，它们只会出现在某个人的排班表上，多一个班次，少一个同事。

Andrea Egan is right, but not right enough. North Sea oil and gas workers get "massive political focus." The thousands of jobs cut across local government, the NHS and education get an "afterthought." This is not a distribution problem in attention. It is a pricing problem. The same unit of labour, one version written into budget forecasts and name-checked by ministers, the other appearing in the budget only as a subtraction. Who deserves to keep their job and who can disappear is never decided by the labour itself.

Look at how Jackdaw was approved: pressure from the fossil fuel industry and rightwing politicians, a government green light, and the project's approval factored directly into the budget forecast. In the same budget, local government jobs shrink, NHS jobs shrink, education jobs shrink. These are not two separate stories. They are two sides of one story: resources concentrate in one direction by being pulled out of another. The direction they are pulled from is care, education, teaching assistants — labour coded as feminine, labour assumed to be there anyway.

Unison and the NEU oppose new drilling; Unite and GMB, representing oil and gas workers, back more licences. The split gets reported as the pain of transition. The real question is who pays for it. Oil and gas workers wanting secure, well-paid jobs is entirely legitimate. But if the way to secure them is to keep drilling, the cost lands on the climate and on public service workers who were never even told a transition was happening. Egan's alternative — secure renewable jobs for oil and gas workers — is the only path that puts both sides at the same table.

The recognition deal between Unison and Octopus Energy matters, but see it for what it is: one company choosing one union, while GMB sought recognition at the same company and did not get it. Renewable jobs are often not unionised and therefore less secure and less well paid than oil and gas jobs — the report states this as a union concern. A recognition deal is progress, but it covers a company, not a sector. Building a "clean energy union" brand and building a clean energy sector with collective bargaining are not the same thing.

Rosebank is still undecided, with Labour MPs lobbying not to overrule the energy secretary. That is the real fork. If a government that ran on ambition is now finding reasons to approve new extraction, then "public sector as afterthought" is not an oversight. It is a ranking. And behind the ranking is collusion: the fossil fuel industry, rightwing politicians, and nervous Labour MPs each get their share, while care workers and teaching assistants get told their contribution is understood.

Egan says it is time for the government to get its priorities straight. The priorities were always straight. They just don't appear in the press release. The cut jobs never make the bold print of a budget forecast. They show up on someone's rota: one more shift, one fewer colleague.

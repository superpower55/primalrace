Beryl Bainbridge: 用黑色幽默解剖男本位叙事的残骸Beryl Bainbridge: Dissecting the Ruins of Masculine Narratives with Black Humour

哲学 文化层 · 结构层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-08-10 § 链接
黑色幽默不是为了好笑，而是为了在绝望的结构中通过嘲讽夺回解释权。
Black humour isn't for laughs; it's about reclaiming the right to interpret within a hopeless structure.

《卫报》对 Beryl Bainbridge 作品的这份排名，表面上是在讨论文学风格，实际上揭示了一个关于存在性战争的残酷样本。Bainbridge 的小说里充满了所谓的“好坏角色” (good bad guys) 和“绝望的浪漫主义者”，这在本质上是对 masculine-centric narrative（男性中心叙事）的一次大规模拆解。

看《Sweet William》，一个 lothario（色情狂/花花公子）通过欺骗无数女性来维持其“艺术家”的人设，而女性在其中扮演的是被定义、被消费的客体。这种叙事在传统文学中常被浪漫化，但在 Bainbridge 的黑色幽默里，它变成了某种荒诞的 scam。她捕捉到了女性在追求“变革性爱情”时的饥渴，而这种饥渴恰恰是被父权结构植入的欲望——一种通过被男性认可来确认自身存在的假.最优解表达。

而在《The Birthday Boys》中，她把镜头对准了南极探险的男性群体，揭露了在极端的生物墙面前，支撑他们走向毁灭的竟然是那座名为“英国阶级制度”的冰川。这是一种典型的 structural violence：男性在共谋维持的阶级秩序中，将自毁视为荣耀。Bainbridge 的毒辣之处在于，她让读者意识到，无论是女性在家庭中的“无名之火”，还是男性在宏大叙事中的“英勇牺牲”，本质上都是在元暴力的规训下，失去了主体性的表演。

好新闻是，Bainbridge 并没有试图去“拯救”这些角色，因为在那种结构里没有救赎。她选择用一种近乎残忍的精准去描述这种失权状态。当她写到女性渴望成为 Bette Davis 或 Joan Crawford 时，她写的是一种身份政治的错位：女性试图通过模仿强权者的表达方式来获得权力，但这依然是在男性定义的规则内玩游戏。

真正的胜利不在于角色是否活了下来，而在于 Bainbridge 夺取了定义权。她把那些被掩盖的、琐碎的、令人不安的真实（如《A Quiet Life》中父母的厮杀）从私人领域拉到公共空间，用黑色幽默将原本被掩盖的 cultural violence 变成了可见的刺。这种表达本身就是一种武器，它告诉我们：当你能嘲笑这个地狱时，你才真正开始走出它。

The Guardian's ranking of Beryl Bainbridge's works is ostensibly about literary style, but it actually presents a brutal specimen of an existential war. Bainbridge's novels are populated by "good bad guys" and "desperate romantics," which is essentially a large-scale dismantling of the masculine-centric narrative.

Take *Sweet William*, where a lothario maintains his "artist" persona by deceiving countless women, who in turn act as defined and consumed objects. While such narratives are often romanticized in traditional literature, in Bainbridge's black humour, they are exposed as a scam. She captures the female hunger for "transformative love," a hunger that is precisely a desire implanted by patriarchal structures—a fake optimal expression of trying to confirm one's existence through male validation.

In *The Birthday Boys*, she turns the lens toward a group of men in the Antarctic, revealing that what drives them toward destruction is the great immovable glacier of the English class system. This is classic structural violence: men, in a complicity to maintain class order, view self-destruction as glory. The cruelty of Bainbridge lies in making the reader realize that whether it is a woman's "unnamed fire" at home or a man's "heroic sacrifice" in a grand narrative, both are performances devoid of subjectivity under the regulation of meta-violence.

The good news is that Bainbridge doesn't attempt to "save" these characters, for there is no redemption within such a structure. Instead, she chooses to describe this state of disempowerment with almost cruel precision. When she writes of women longing to be Bette Davis or Joan Crawford, she is writing about a misalignment of identity politics: women attempting to gain power by mimicking the expressions of the powerful, which is still playing a game within rules defined by men.

True victory lies not in whether the characters survive, but in Bainbridge seizing the right of interpretation. She drags the hidden, trivial, and unsettling truths (like the parental combat in *A Quiet Life*) from the private sphere into the public space, turning what was once hidden cultural violence into a visible thorn. This expression itself is a weapon; it tells us that only when you can laugh at this hell do you truly begin to exit it.

一款种菜打架游戏，和它没打算碰的东西A Gardening Brawler and What It Doesn't Touch

文娱 文化层 Deadline ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
游戏把园艺变成竞技，可它连一个真实种地的人都没提到
A game turns gardening into combat, and never mentions a single person who farms.

这篇采访里没有一个人为这款游戏付出代价。没有加班到脱发的开发者，没有因为种菜打架而受伤的玩家，也没有被游戏里的土豆砸中的任何人。Cloth Cat 的创意制作人 Ben Cawthorne 只是在讲一个点子怎么从"如果园艺是竞技呢"长成一个四人本地联机原型，又怎么变成线上多人。他讲得很诚恳，也很技术：地块要追踪水量，肥料能让作物超大化，靠近水源就离卖菜的地方远，平衡竞技场花了最多时间。

这本身没什么问题。一个非严肃的派对游戏不需要承载暴力三角，它甚至算得上一种公正的表达：几个人坐一张沙发上互相扔土豆，谁也没被掠夺。Cawthorne 说希望它成为"你和朋友聚会时会玩的那一类游戏"，这话是真的，不是话术。

但采访里有一处值得停一下。他讲"我们意识到这游戏必须是非严肃的，因为概念本身就很荒谬"，然后讲"有些基本原理人们能从其他游戏或者从种一颗种子长出一株植物的现实里理解"。种一颗种子。这句话在整篇采访里是唯一一次碰到真实的土地，而它只是作为机制锚点出现的。游戏里没有旱季，没有买不起化肥的小农，没有因为缺水而绝收的一季。园艺在这里被抽干成一个可平衡的资源系统，水、肥、种子、售价，四个变量。

这不是批评，这是观察。派对游戏本来就不负责这些。真正让我在意的是另一件事：整篇采访里，Cawthorne 反复用"我们"，团队、原型、垂直切片、网络同步、ragdoll 系统。但没有一个名字被问到过工时，没有一句问到 Cloth Cat 从动画转游戏、从外包转独立之后，那些做 3D 模型和动画的人是怎么撑过 scope 这场"你一直在打的仗"的。他说 scope 是"你一直在打的仗"，这句话在游戏行业里通常有另一层意思，而采访没有往下问。

所以这篇东西的正确读法是：它是一个产品访谈，不是一个关于人的报道。它告诉你一款游戏怎么被做出来，没告诉你谁做的、做了多久、代价是什么。Cawthorne 最后说希望"园艺战斗"能成为一个类型。也许能。但在那之前，先问问做这款游戏的人有没有时间玩它。

Nobody pays a price in this interview. No developer working nights, no player injured by a thrown potato, no one standing in the way of Cloth Cat's produce. Creative producer Ben Cawthorne is just explaining how an idea — "what if gardening was competitive?" — grew from a four-player couch prototype into an online multiplayer game. He's honest and technical about it: plots track water, fertilizer supersizes crops, being near a water source puts you far from the selling zone, and balancing arenas ate most of the schedule.

That's fine. A non-serious party game doesn't need to carry the Violence Triangle. If anything it's a just expression: four people on a couch lobbing potatoes at each other, nobody plundered. Cawthorne says he hopes it becomes "part of that group of games you play when you get together with your friends." That line is real, not a pitch.

But one moment is worth stopping on. He says the game had to be non-serious "because the concept itself is ridiculous," then says "there are fundamentals that people will understand from other games or simply from the reality of growing a seed and having a plant at the end." Growing a seed. That's the only time the interview touches actual soil, and it touches it as a mechanical anchor. No dry season, no farmer who can't afford fertilizer, no harvest lost to drought. Gardening gets stripped to a balanceable resource system: water, fertilizer, seed, sale price. Four variables.

That's not a criticism. Party games aren't responsible for this. What actually nags is something else: Cawthorne says "we" throughout — team, prototype, vertical slice, netcode, ragdoll system — but no one asks about hours, and no one asks how the people building 3D models and animation at a studio that moved from animation into games and from work-for-hire into independence got through what he calls "scope, which is what you're battling." In this industry that phrase usually means something, and the interview doesn't follow it.

So read this as a product interview, not a story about people. It tells you how a game got made. It doesn't tell you who made it, for how long, or at what cost. Cawthorne ends hoping "horticultural combat" becomes a genre. Maybe it will. Before that, ask whether the people who built it have time to play it.

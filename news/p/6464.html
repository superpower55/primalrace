可卡因的账，最后记在谁头上Who Pays for the Cocaine

国际 结构层 · 文化层 · 元暴力 The Guardian ↗ 2026-11-01 § 链接
把消费说成娱乐，把后果留给别人，这是共谋的语法。
Calling consumption recreation, and leaving the bill to others, is the grammar of complicity.

这封读者来信里最诚实的一句，是那位厄瓜多尔读者说的：每一次购买可卡因，都是在给跨国犯罪网络付钱。他住在被这些网络"拥有"政客、商人、警察和法官的地方，住在无辜者被帮派火并的流弹打死的地方，住在被保护费逼死的小生意旁边。而英国的"娱乐性"使用，是这条链条的另一端。

但《卫报》那组调查真正值得拿出来拆的，不是可卡因有多坏，而是可卡因的伤害是怎么被分配的。报道里写的是英国国内的家暴、犯罪上升、消费激增——伤害被写成发生在消费国境内的事情，写在家庭里，写在受害者身上。写信的医生把这条线接上了：伤害的起点不在英国的客厅，在厄瓜多尔的街头。同一包粉末，一端是周末的消遣，另一端是有人因为拒绝配合而被杀。这不是两个问题，是一个问题的两端，中间隔着的距离，就是谁承担代价的距离。

加尔通的算式在这里很清楚：现状低于本可达到的状态，差额就是暴力。可卡因链条上那个差额，是厄瓜多尔那些本可以活着的人、本可以正常营业的店铺、本可以不被收买的司法。这个差额不是自然形成的，是被购买行为一次次投票选出来的。而"娱乐"这个词的功能，就是把投票说成消遣，让投票的人不必看见票投给了谁。这是第三章那套东西：一个词被武器化，用来切断认知入口，让消费者停在"这是我的选择"，让解释权留在不追问的那一边。

Haydock 的信是另一条线。他说治疗和康复服务有效、高效，说政府两年多对成瘾问题基本沉默，说 2021 年那版战略带来了投资和具体目标，说现在接受治疗的成年人数量是有记录以来最高的。这些都是真的，但他整封信的落点是一个请求：请给我钱，请给我战略。这封信写得体面、专业、有数据，但它是一封拨款申请。治疗服务是好的——它确实是暴力三角里被具体缩小的那一块，人在被救回来，家庭在少受一点伤——可它是下游。上游的进口、分销、洗钱、杀人，一个字没提。

这就是共谋的形状，而且不必是恶意的。一个全国联盟的首席执行官，职责就是替成员机构要资源，他的最优解表达就是把问题框成"公共卫生＋治疗投入"，因为那是他能拿到钱的语言。这没有错，但结果是：一封谈成瘾的信，把成瘾的供给端和暴力端整个留在了画面外。写信的厄瓜多尔人没有机构、没有联盟、没有拨款可申请，他只能署"姓名与地址保密"。两封信并排放着，谁有资格说话、谁只能匿名，这个对比本身就是元暴力的一次现场演示。

更值得盯的是那句：治疗服务对政府实现"针对妇女和女孩的暴力"等目标至关重要。这句话把家暴和可卡因挂上了钩，方向是对的，但它顺手把女性放进了"政策议题"的清单里，和刑事司法危机、无家可归、青年就业并列。女性在这里是一个被治理的类别，不是一个有解释权的主体。可卡因助长的家暴，受害者是具体的女人；而决定钱怎么花、叙事怎么写的，是机构、政府和联盟。这条链上每一环都有人得利，得利的方式各不相同，共同点是都不必为厄瓜多尔街头的尸体负责。

真正被削掉的暴力，是治疗服务救回来的那些人——这一层要认，它是实打实的。但它是下游的下游。上游那个差额还在扩大：合成阿片类进英国，国家犯罪局说"现在吸毒从没有这么危险"，11 月 4 日要公布的新数字大概率还会更难看。而只要"娱乐"这个词还站得住，只要消费端的账还记在消费端，这条链就会一直有人付钱、有人收钱、有人被杀，而付钱的人始终以为自己只是在过周末。

The most honest line in these letters comes from the reader in Ecuador: every time a user buys cocaine, they are paying the murderous networks. He lives where those networks "own" politicians, businesspeople, police and judges; where innocent people die in crossfire between gangs; where protection rackets cripple or kill small businesses. Britain's "recreational" use is the other end of that chain.

But what is worth taking apart in the Guardian's cocaine investigation is not that cocaine is bad. It is how the harm is distributed. The reporting puts the damage inside Britain—domestic abuse, rising crime, surging consumption—as if the injury happens in the consuming country, in homes, on victims' bodies. The doctor's letter reconnects the line: the harm does not begin in a British living room. It begins on a street in Ecuador. The same powder: at one end a weekend's entertainment, at the other someone killed for refusing to play along. These are not two problems. They are one problem with a distance in the middle, and that distance is exactly who pays.

Galtung's arithmetic is clean here. Violence is potential minus actual. On the cocaine chain, the gap is the people in Ecuador who could have lived, the shops that could have stayed open, the judiciary that could have stayed unbought. That gap is not natural. It is voted into existence, purchase by purchase. And the function of the word "recreational" is to rename voting as leisure, so the voter never has to see who received the ballot. This is the third chapter's machinery: a word weaponised to cut off the cognitive entrance, parking the consumer at "my choice" and leaving the power of interpretation on the side that does not ask.

Haydock's letter is the other line. Treatment and recovery work, he says; the government has been largely silent on addiction for over two years; the 2021 strategy brought investment and targets; treatment numbers are the highest since reporting began. All true. But the letter lands on a request: fund us, give us a strategy. It is well written, professional, evidence-based—and it is a funding application. Treatment is good. It is the part of the Violence Triangle that has actually been narrowed: people pulled back, families spared some injury. But it is downstream. Upstream—import, distribution, laundering, killing—is not mentioned once.

This is the shape of complicity, and it need not be malicious. The chief executive of a national alliance has one job: get resources for member charities. His optimal expression is to frame the problem as public health plus treatment spending, because that is the language that gets money. Nothing wrong with that. The result, though, is that a letter about addiction leaves the supply side and the violence side entirely outside the frame. The Ecuadorian writer has no institution, no alliance, no grant to apply for. He can only sign "name and address supplied." Two letters side by side: who gets to speak, and who must be anonymous. That contrast is meta-violence performing live.

Watch one more sentence: treatment services are crucial if the government is to meet its aims on "violence against women and girls." The link to domestic abuse is correct, but the sentence files women into a list of policy issues, alongside the criminal justice crisis, homelessness, youth employment and mental health. Women appear here as a category to be governed, not as subjects with interpretive power. The domestic abuse fuelled by cocaine has specific women as victims; the decisions about money and narrative belong to institutions, government and the alliance. Every link in this chain has someone who benefits, each in a different currency, and the common feature is that none of them has to answer for a body on an Ecuadorian street.

The violence genuinely reduced is the people treatment pulls back. That counts. But it is downstream of downstream. Upstream the gap keeps widening: synthetic opioids have arrived, the National Crime Agency warns that "there has never been a more dangerous time to take drugs," and the figures due on 4 November will most likely be worse. As long as "recreational" holds, as long as the bill on the consuming end is booked on the consuming end, this chain will keep having payers, takers and the killed—and the payers will keep believing they were only having a weekend.
